# Chapter One: Opening Day

The oven was dead when Hannah Brooks walked into her grandmother's bakery, and the cash box was nearly empty.

She stood in the doorway with her suitcase in one hand and the key in the other, and let the cold settle into her. Cutter Street was quiet at seven in the morning. Snow had fallen overnight, a thin white layer on the sidewalks, and the sign above the door creaked in the wind. THE CUTTER STREET BAKERY, it said, in red letters her grandmother had painted herself. Under that, in smaller letters: EST. 1961.

Hannah had not been inside in eleven years. The room still smelled the way it always had, of yeast and cinnamon and a little burnt sugar from a pan someone had forgotten. The glass cases were spotless. Jam jars stood in a neat row on the shelves, each labeled in her grandmother's looping hand. Nothing was out of place, and that was almost the worst part. It meant nobody had been able to bring themselves to finish the last day.

She set down her suitcase and walked to the back. The deck oven stood against the far wall like a sleeping animal. Its door was cold. Its dials were dark.

"Oh, Gran," she whispered.

An envelope lay on the workbench beside it, with her name written across the front. She knew the handwriting before she picked it up.

*Hannah,* it began. *The oven is older than your mother and twice as stubborn. Don't let it beat you. Don't let anyone talk you into selling before you have tasted the bread. And if the man who fixes things on this street gives you trouble, be kind to him. He is more frightened than he looks.*

Hannah read it twice, pressed it to her chest, and eyed the oven the way she eyed difficult customers.

"Right," she said. "Let's see who blinks first."

Behind her, the bell over the door jingled, and a man's voice said, "The sign says open, but the lights say otherwise."

She turned around.

He filled the doorway the way weather fills a valley, all shoulders and dark hair and a canvas work jacket dusted with snow. He had a toolbox in one hand and a coffee cup in the other, and he was looking at her with the flat, polite expression of someone who has been up since four and has no intention of being charmed.

It took her a second to place him. Then it took no time at all.

"Eli Carver," she said.

"Hannah." He didn't smile, but something in his face eased, the way a door eases when a draft stops. "I heard you were coming back. I figured you'd come in through the front, so I came in through the front, too. Your grandmother always said the back door was for deliveries and sad people."

"Did she?"

"She said it to me about four hundred times." He set the toolbox down on the floor, carefully, the way a person sets down something that might break. "The oven's not dead. It's just tired. The thermostat cable gave out sometime last winter, and nobody wanted to say so to Mabel."

"Mabel?"

"The starter." He nodded toward a crock on the shelf above the sink, covered with a damp cloth. "She's been fed every morning since your grandmother went into the hospital. Somebody's been keeping her alive. I'd like to know who."

Hannah looked at the crock, and then at the cloth, and then at the man who was looking at her with the faintest trace of accusation in his eyes.

"Not me," she said. "I've been in Chicago for eleven years."

"I know," said Eli Carver. "That's what makes it interesting."

"Mabel is a sourdough starter," Hannah said. "She's flour and water. She doesn't need anyone to keep her alive."

"Everything needs someone to keep it alive," Eli said. "Even flour and water, apparently." He pulled the cloth back an inch, and a warm, sour smell rose out of the crock, rich and faintly sweet, like apples left in the sun. Hannah's throat tightened without warning. She had not smelled that in eleven years, and her body remembered it before her mind did.

"She's bubbling," Eli said. "Somebody's feeding her twice a day, and it isn't me. I've been too busy with the boiler at the church and the sink at the post office."

"You've been feeding a starter?"

"I've been feeding the bakery." He shrugged. "Your grandmother asked me to keep the lights on and the pipes from freezing. The starter was extra."

Hannah looked around the room properly for the first time. Under the dust that had settled on the counters there was no real dirt. The floor had been swept. The cases had been wiped. Someone had been coming in, quietly, for months, and tending to the place the way you might tend to a grave.

"Who has the key?" she asked.

"Your grandmother gave me a copy in March," said Eli. "Then she gave me a list. Fix the boiler. Keep the freezer cold. Don't let Mabel die. Don't let Hannah sell the building to Garrett Pike before she's tasted the bread." He paused. "I'm not sure that last one was a joke."

"It wasn't," said Hannah, and she felt something cold move through her chest. "Who is Garrett Pike?"

Eli's face changed. It didn't soften, exactly. It closed a little, like a shop shutting its shutters at dusk.

"He's the man who wants to buy this street," he said. "Or most of it. He sent your grandmother a letter in January. She sent it back with one word written on the bottom."

"What word?"

"No," said Eli. "Underlined twice."

Hannah laughed, a short surprised sound that filled the empty room. It was the first time she had laughed since the phone call in Chicago, the one where a lawyer she had never met had said the words *estate*, *bakery*, and *decision* in the same sentence, and then waited politely while she tried to breathe.

Eli watched her laugh. He didn't join in, but he didn't look away, either.

"The oven's going to cost you," he said finally. "Thermostat cable, maybe a new relay. Three hundred dollars if I'm lucky. Four if the wiring behind the wall is as bad as I think it is."

"I'm not sure how much I have."

"I know," he said. "I've seen the cash box."

Hannah bristled. "You've been going through my grandmother's money?"

"Your grandmother's money was in the cash box for two months with nobody to sign for it," said Eli evenly. "I counted it because someone had to. It came to ninety-one dollars and a button." He picked up his coffee, realized it was cold, and put it down again. "I'll fix the oven. You can pay me when the bread starts selling. If it starts selling."

"Thank you," she said stiffly. "I can pay for my own repairs."

"I'm sure you can." He turned toward the door, then stopped and looked back over his shoulder. "Hannah?"

"What?"

"Don't touch the starter until I've checked the temperature. She's sensitive."

"I'm not an idiot, Eli."

"No," he said, and for the first time, there was something almost like warmth in his voice. "You were never that. You were just very sure of everything, and it used to drive me crazy."

The bell jingled again as he left, and the cold air came rushing in after him. Hannah stood in the middle of her grandmother's bakery with the envelope in her hand, a dead oven at her back and a living crock of sourdough on the shelf, and she told herself, firmly, that she was not going to think about the way he had said *used to*.

She lasted about four minutes.

# Chapter Two: The Man With the Wrench

Eli Carver came back at noon with a box of parts under one arm and a paper bag of sandwiches under the other, and he did not knock.

Hannah had spent the morning doing what she was good at, which was making lists. She had written down everything in the bakery that needed to be fixed, then everything she could afford to fix, then everything she could sell to pay for the rest. The lists were getting longer, and the bottom line was getting shorter, and she had reached the point of staring at the numbers without seeing them when the bell over the door rang and a paper bag landed on the counter beside her laptop.

"Turkey on rye," said Eli. "No mayonnaise. Your grandmother said you used to eat it with mustard and a pickle and refuse to admit that the pickle was the best part."

"I still don't like mayonnaise."

"Then I'm glad I didn't bring it." He set the box of parts on the floor and knelt beside the oven with the ease of a man who had spent a lot of time on kitchen floors. "Two hours. Maybe three. Don't hover."

"I'm not going to hover."

"You're hovering now."

She was, in fact, standing at the end of the counter with her arms folded, close enough to see the flat, precise way he laid out his tools on a clean towel. A socket set. A multimeter. A small plastic tub of wire nuts sorted by color. He worked without hurry and without wasted motion, and when he took off his jacket and rolled up his sleeves she made a point of looking at the pastry case instead.

"What's wrong with it?" she asked, because silence was worse.

"Three things." He held up a finger. "The thermostat cable is frayed where it runs behind the back panel. Somebody, a long time ago, wrapped it in electrical tape and hoped for the best. That's the reason it kept working for a while, and it's the reason it stopped working last winter." Another finger. "The relay's gone. I can replace it for about forty dollars if I can find one that fits, and I can, because I've been hunting for one since March."

"And the third?"

He glanced at her over his shoulder. "The wiring behind the wall is older than both of us, and I'm not going to know how bad it is until I open it up."

"That sounds expensive."

"It might be." He turned back to the panel and unscrewed the cover with a small, neat twist. "Your grandmother wanted to fix it herself. She had a plan. It involved a lot of phone calls to people who owed her favors, and I think she was starting to run out of favors."

Hannah pulled out a stool and sat down, because her knees had started to feel strange. "She didn't tell me," she said.

"She didn't tell anyone." He worked in silence for a moment. "She told me she wanted you to come home on your own. She said if she made it too easy, you'd know something was wrong."

"I wasn't coming home," said Hannah. "I was coming to sign papers."

"I know." He didn't say it unkindly. "But you came."

She didn't answer. She opened the laptop instead and looked at the list she had been staring at all morning. *Oven. Freezer. Walk-in door seal. Espresso machine (dead). Insurance (lapsed?). Rent for the storage unit on Pine (overdue).* At the bottom, underlined twice by her own hand, was a single line: *Ask the lawyer about the offer.*

"Can I ask you something?" she said.

"You're going to anyway."

"Who is Garrett Pike, really?"

Eli sat back on his heels and wiped his hands on a rag. For a long moment he looked at the oven, as if the answer might be written somewhere inside the dark metal.

"He's a developer," he said. "Pike Ridge Development. He builds the kind of places that look like a village from a distance and turn out to be parking garages up close. He's got money, and he's got a lawyer in Columbus who never sleeps. He wants Cutter Street because it's the last street in town with its own bakery, its own hardware store, and its own diner, and because the land behind the buildings runs down to the river."

"That doesn't sound like a crime."

"It isn't a crime." Eli picked up a small screwdriver and turned it over in his fingers. "It's just a plan. Most of the time, plans like his work. He buys a few buildings, then a few more. He offers people more than the building is worth. The ones who say no get letters from the city about code violations. The ones who say yes move to a condo by the highway and think they did well."

"And you think my grandmother was going to say yes?"

"I think your grandmother was going to say no until the day she died," said Eli. "And I think she was afraid she'd leave you the decision."

Hannah stared at him. The oven hummed faintly as a fan kicked on somewhere inside, and the smell of hot dust drifted across the kitchen.

"That's not fair," she said.

"Life isn't fair," said Eli. "Your grandmother used to say that, too, usually right before she gave me a second cookie."

She laughed in spite of herself, and he smiled at the floor, a short, reluctant smile that made him look younger for a moment. Then he bent back over the panel, and the moment passed, but not all the way. It left something behind in the room, like the warmth that stays in a chair after someone gets up.

By two o'clock the oven was humming. By two-thirty, the relay was in and the cable was wrapped in a new sleeve of heat-shrink tubing that Eli had bought from a hardware store in a town twenty minutes away. By three, the wiring behind the wall had been opened, photographed, and closed again, and Eli had stood in the back room with his arms folded, looking at the results with an expression Hannah could only call grim.

"How bad?" she asked.

"Bad enough that I'm going to need a day to think about it," he said. "Not bad enough to call the fire department."

"That's a comfort."

"It's meant to be." He wiped his hands again and picked up his jacket. "Test the oven tomorrow, not today. Let it run empty for an hour, then put a loaf in. Use the cheap flour first. If it burns, it's the oven. If it doesn't, it's you."

"Thanks for the vote of confidence."

"I'm not confident about anything, Hannah." He shrugged the jacket on, and for a second his face was unguarded in a way she had not seen since they were teenagers, cutting through the alley behind the post office on their way to the river. "But I think you can do this. I always did."

He left before she could answer, and the bell rang behind him like a small, cheerful insult.

Hannah sat alone at the counter with the sandwich untouched beside her and the oven humming at her back. She picked up her phone and typed a message to her sister in Chicago. *The oven works. The man who fixed it is still insufferable.*

The reply came within a minute. *Which man?*

Hannah stared at the screen. Then she typed, *You know which man*, and put the phone face down on the counter.

Outside, the snow had started again, fine and dry, drifting across Cutter Street in long pale ribbons. Across the road, a light came on in the window of the old hardware store, and a figure moved behind the glass, tall and broad-shouldered, walking slowly toward the back.

Hannah watched until the light went out.

# Chapter Three: Sourdough and Old Promises

The first loaf came out of the oven at six the next morning, slightly lopsided and dark on one side, and Hannah stood in front of it for a full minute before she dared to touch it.

It was the bread from her childhood. It was not perfect. The crust crackled when she pressed it, and the crumb inside was open and chewy, with the faint sourness she had tasted in the back of her throat for eleven years whenever she thought of home. She tore off a piece, still warm, and ate it standing in the empty kitchen with flour on her hands and tears on her face, and she did not stop to wonder why.

It was just bread. It was also her grandmother's voice, saying *slow down, you're eating it like a bank robber*.

When she could breathe again, she went to find the notebook.

It lived in the drawer beneath the register, under a rubber-banded stack of old invoices and a jar of dried-up pens. Her grandmother had called it the recipe book, though it held far more than recipes. There were pages of measurements in pencil, notes about humidity and flour brands, sketches of the ovens and their dials, phone numbers with names beside them, and, on the last few pages, a careful list in blue ink that had been written in the spring.

*Hannah: the oven. Eli: the wiring. Mabel: keep alive. Don't sell before the Fair.*

Hannah frowned at the last line. *Don't sell before the Fair.* There was no Fair on Cutter Street that she knew of. There had not been one since the year before she left for college, when the town council had tried to organize a spring market and the rain had washed it away.

She turned the page.

The next entries were older, written in a different pen, the ink faded to brown. They were dated, year by year, going back into the years before Hannah was born. Her grandmother had kept track of the bakery's receipts, its suppliers, the names of the people who worked there, and small notes that read like diary entries. *Ray came in with the flour, his back is worse. Priced the rye too low again. Mabel sulking.*

On one page, dated the autumn of 1989, there was a single sentence underlined twice: *Eli's father was here. Asked for a loan. Said no, and then said yes. Wish I'd said no.*

Hannah read it three times.

Eli's father had run the hardware store across the road. He had been a big, quiet man with forearms like tree trunks, and he had taught Hannah to hold a hammer properly when she was eight. He had died when she was sixteen, in a winter that had seemed to go on forever, and she remembered the funeral, the cold church, and the way Eli had stood at the grave with his hands in his pockets and refused to cry.

She had not known about a loan.

She had not known anything, really, about the Carver family's business in the years after the funeral. She had been busy with her own life, with school and then Chicago, and she had let the town become a place she visited in her memory rather than in person. It had been easy to do. It had also, she was beginning to suspect, been a mistake.

She went back to the counter, sat down, and wrote a text message to her sister.

*Did you know Mr. Carver borrowed money from Gran?*

Jess answered after about ten minutes, which was long for her. *Gran never said anything. Why?*

*Found a note in her book. Dated 1989.*

*Hannah, I wasn't born in 1989. I know nothing about 1989.*

Hannah put the phone down. Then she picked it up again, because there was something else she needed to ask.

*Did Gran ever mention a Garrett Pike to you?*

The reply was immediate this time. *The developer? Yes. She called him a vulture. She said he had the manners of a man who thinks everyone is for sale, and the bad luck to be right about most of them. Why are you asking me about Garrett Pike at six in the morning?*

*Because he's already tried to buy the street. And because Eli says Gran was afraid of him.*

There was a long pause, and then Jess wrote, *Eli Carver is still around?*

*He fixed the oven.*

*Huh.* Another pause. *Be careful, Hannah. You always think the thing in front of you is the only thing going on.*

Hannah stared at the message until the screen went dark.

It was seven-thirty when Eli came back, carrying two coffees and looking as if he had not slept at all. He stopped in the doorway, sniffed the air, and his expression did something complicated and private before it settled back into its usual careful blankness.

"That's the bread," he said.

"It's a bread," said Hannah. "It's lopsided."

"It smells like your grandmother's kitchen." He came in and set one of the coffees on the counter beside her. "She used to burn the bottom every single time. Said it gave the crust character."

"Did you know she borrowed money from your father?"

The question came out sharper than she intended. Eli went still. He set his own coffee down on the counter without taking a sip, and for a moment he looked at her as if she were a stranger who had just walked up and taken him by the collar.

"Where did you hear that?" he asked quietly.

"It's in her notebook. Nineteen eighty-nine. She wrote that she should have said no."

Eli turned away and looked out the front window at the street. Across the road, the hardware store's sign hung dark and still, the paint peeling from the S.

"Yes," he said after a while. "She did. And then she paid him back, every cent, with interest he never asked for. He used to say it was the only honest loan he ever made, and the only time he ever lost money on purpose."

"Why would she have needed the money?"

"Because the bakery was failing," said Eli. "Because her husband had just died, the bakery was losing money every month, and your grandmother had decided that Cutter Street was worth saving even if it meant going into debt to do it." He turned back, and his face was tired in a way that made her want to reach out and touch his arm. "My father never told anyone. Neither did I. It was the one thing in this town that was none of anybody's business."

"Until now," said Hannah.

"Until now," said Eli. "And now it's yours."

She didn't know what to say to that. He seemed to understand, because he picked his coffee back up and took a long drink, then nodded at the loaf cooling on the rack.

"You should sell that," he said.

"It's not for sale."

"Everything is for sale, Hannah. That's Pike's whole theory." He finished the coffee and crushed the cup in one hand with a dry little crackle. "But that bread isn't. Not to me, anyway. I'll take the heel."

She cut him a slice without asking, spread it thinly with butter, and handed it across the counter. He ate it standing up in three bites, then looked at the empty crust in his hand as if it had surprised him.

"Well," he said. "That's annoying."

"What is?"

"It's good." He wiped his fingers on his jeans and headed for the door. "Don't tell your grandmother I said so. She'll never let me hear the end of it."

"She's not here to hear anything," said Hannah, and the words came out raw.

Eli stopped in the doorway, his hand on the frame. He didn't turn around.

"I know," he said. "I think she'd want us to talk about her anyway."

And then he was gone, and the bell rang behind him, and Hannah stood alone in the kitchen with the open notebook in front of her and the ghost of a loan from 1989 pressing down on her like the weight of the snow outside.

# Chapter Four: The Cash Box

By Friday, Hannah had learned three things about running a bakery that no one had told her in Chicago.

The first was that bread sold itself only in the stories. In real life, you had to stand at the counter and smile at people who came in for a single roll and stayed for twenty minutes to ask about your grandmother, your lease, your love life, and whether you planned to keep the place open or sell it to Garrett Pike, which was how everyone in town seemed to phrase the question.

The second was that a cash box, however small, could hold an astonishing amount of sadness. Hers held ninety-one dollars and a button, as Eli had said, plus a handful of receipts from the spring, a roll of stamps, and a yellow sticky note that read *ASK ABOUT THE PARTS* in her grandmother's handwriting. It was the most unhelpful note Hannah had ever seen.

The third was that she was going to need a lot more than ninety-one dollars.

She sat at the little desk in the back room on Friday night, with the radiator clanking and the shelves of flour bags leaning over her like patient giants, and went through every bank statement she could find. The bakery had been running on fumes for almost two years. The electric bill was overdue. The gas bill was two months behind, and the company had sent three increasingly polite letters, the last of which used the phrase *final notice* twice. There was a credit card in the name of Dorothy Brooks, with a balance that made Hannah close her eyes and count to ten, and a line of credit from a local bank that had been drawn to its limit in March.

At the bottom of the stack was a letter she had not expected: a formal notice from the county assessor, addressed to the estate, informing the executor that the property taxes on the building at 14 Cutter Street were due in full by the end of the year.

*By the end of the year.*

Hannah put the letter down very carefully, as if it might go off in her hands.

Her phone buzzed. It was her lawyer, Mr. Dorsey, a precise, cheerful man in Columbus whom she had spoken to exactly twice. She let it ring. It rang again, and then a message popped up on the screen.

*Hannah, I know you're in town. Please call me tomorrow morning. Garrett Pike's office has asked for a meeting, and I'd like to know your position before they make an offer in writing.*

She stared at the message for a long time. Then she put the phone face down and went to the front of the shop, because she could not stand to be in the back room any longer, not with the numbers and the letters and the ghost of a loan from 1989 following her around like a bad smell.

The lights were off outside. The street was empty except for a single car idling at the corner, its headlights cutting a pale path through the snow. Across the road, the hardware store was dark, but there was a glow from somewhere in the back, a warm yellow that seemed to come from a small window above the loading dock.

She stood at the glass and looked at it for a while.

When she turned away, she caught sight of her reflection in the window: a woman with flour on her cheek, a streak of soot on one sleeve, and a face that looked far older than twenty-nine. She had left this town at eighteen, certain that she would never need it again. It had seemed simple back then. She had been brilliant at knowing what she wanted, and terrible at understanding what she was giving up to get it.

She went back to the desk, opened her laptop, and typed a reply to Mr. Dorsey.

*I'll call you in the morning. I'm not selling yet.*

She looked at the words for a minute, then added one more line.

*Please send me everything Pike has sent this family, including anything from 1989.*

She sent it before she could change her mind.

The reply arrived at 6:12 the next morning, so early that Hannah suspected Mr. Dorsey had been awake all night. It was a single line.

*Hannah, I have no idea what happened in 1989, but I'm glad you asked. Coffee at nine?*

She laughed out loud in the empty kitchen, then made herself a cup of the instant stuff from the back shelf, because the espresso machine was still dead and she had not yet found the courage to ask Eli to fix it. It was not much of a breakfast. It was enough for now.

At nine o'clock sharp, she called Mr. Dorsey from the back room. He answered on the first ring.

"Ms. Brooks," he said. "I've been reading your grandmother's correspondence for a week, and I have to say, she was a more sophisticated opponent than I ever gave her credit for."

"Opponent?"

"Garrett Pike has been trying to buy that block for three years," said Mr. Dorsey. "Your grandmother refused every offer. In the last six months, she refused four, and she sent back two letters with notes I would not repeat to a judge. She also paid the back taxes on the building from a private account, which I'd like to talk to you about, because I don't think she had the money to do that."

Hannah closed her eyes. "She borrowed it," she said. "From Mr. Carver's father. In 1989."

There was a pause on the line.

"Ah," said Mr. Dorsey. "That explains the letter from Hollis Carver's estate. I wondered why it was in the file."

"What letter?"

"It's a release," said Mr. Dorsey carefully. "Signed by the late Mr. Carver's son, Eli, in March. It releases your grandmother from any remaining obligation on the 1989 loan, and it states that the Carver family will not pursue any claim against the bakery or its property. It was witnessed by a notary in this county. The signature is very neat."

Hannah sat very still. The radiator clanked. Somewhere out front, the bell over the door rang, and she heard Eli's voice say something to a customer, low and polite, and then a woman's laugh.

"He never told me," she said.

"Well," said Mr. Dorsey, in a tone that sounded almost kind. "I don't imagine he'd tell you anything he didn't have to. But he went to a lot of trouble to make sure you wouldn't inherit someone else's debt."

Hannah didn't answer. She was staring at the notebook on the desk, at the faded line in her grandmother's blue ink: *Wish I'd said no.*

She had come home expecting to be the only person in Cutter Street with a secret. It was beginning to look as though everyone had one, and they had all been carrying them for years.

# Chapter Five: A Walk Down Cutter Street

Hannah had lived on Cutter Street for the first eighteen years of her life, and she had never once walked the whole length of it on purpose.

She set out on the Monday after the oven was fixed, with a paper cup of coffee in one hand and a leash looped twice around her wrist, its other end clipped to Biscuit, Tom Kowalski's dog, whom Tom had asked her to walk while he drove a load of feed out to the farms. The dog had views on the matter. He wanted to sniff every lamppost, every doorstep, every trash can lid on the block, and he made his feelings known with long, dramatic pauses and a snort of protest whenever she tried to move him along.

"We have a schedule," she told him. "It's a short one. Shops, people, trees. Then bread."

Biscuit sat down on the sidewalk in front of the hardware store and looked at her with the patience of a dog who had heard this sort of speech before and had outlived all of them.

The street began at the river and ran uphill to the church, and the bakery sat near the middle, between the diner and the hardware store, like a tooth in a row of tired but determined old friends. At the river end stood the empty lot where Pike Ridge had cleared the old boatyard two summers ago. Someone had nailed a plywood sign to the fence. It said *COMING SOON*, and somebody else had written underneath it in black marker, *IT'S ALREADY HERE. LOOK AT THE MUD.*

Hannah laughed and took a photograph of it on her phone, then wished she had not, because it felt like a betrayal of something she could not quite name.

Past the boatyard lot was the old Lutheran hall, its doors painted a faded green, and the library, a squat stone building with a bench out front where an elderly man in a tweed cap was feeding crumbs to a pair of very fat pigeons. He lifted his cap to her as she passed, and she lifted her coffee in return.

"Good morning," he called. "You're Dorothy's girl."

"I'm Hannah."

"I know who you are. I'm Walt. I played cards with your grandmother every Thursday for twenty years. She cheated, of course, but she did it with such charm you couldn't be cross." He shook his head, smiling at something only he could see. "She used to say the bakery was the only honest business on the street, because nobody ever tried to sell you anything but bread."

"That sounds like her."

"She was a terrible liar," said Walt, "and a wonderful friend. Don't let them tell you otherwise." He tossed the last of his crumbs to the pigeons. "Come and see me on Thursday. We'll do the cards. I'll let you win."

"I'll take you up on that," said Hannah, and meant it.

The diner was already busy, its windows steamed to a soft white glow, the smell of frying bacon and coffee pouring out onto the sidewalk. Mrs. Ortiz was behind the counter with a pencil in her hair and a tray of eggs in one hand, shouting orders at the cook in a mix of Spanish and English that made the regulars grin into their plates.

She saw Hannah through the glass and jabbed the pencil in her direction, then pointed at the dog.

"No dogs," she called through the open door. "Unless it's the dog. The dog can come in. The dog is a much better customer than most of the men in this town."

Biscuit trotted in as though he owned the place and went straight to the table by the window where an old farmer was eating pancakes. The farmer slid him a corner of sausage without looking up, and the two of them seemed to reach a silent understanding.

"That dog has no shame," said Hannah.

"That dog has excellent taste," said Mrs. Ortiz, and poured her a coffee she had not asked for. "Sit. Eat something with protein. You look like a ghost who's been left out in the rain."

Hannah sat. The coffee was terrible, which she had come to find oddly comforting. Mrs. Ortiz slid into the booth opposite her and folded her arms, and for a moment neither of them spoke while the diner clattered and hummed around them.

"You're walking the street," said Mrs. Ortiz.

"How did you know?"

"Because you have the look your grandmother had when she was deciding whether to forgive someone. Walt told me. He called me from the bench. He said you looked like you were counting the windows." She leaned forward. "Are you counting the windows, mija?"

"I'm counting the people," said Hannah quietly. "I didn't know how many of them there were. I thought the street was mostly empty buildings and Garrett Pike's paperwork. But everyone I pass says her name. Walt. The man with the paper shop. The woman on the corner who sells flowers and said she'd been waiting for the bakery to reopen since she was a little girl."

"Ah," said Mrs. Ortiz softly. "Now you're seeing it."

"Seeing what?"

"The thing she never said out loud," said Mrs. Ortiz. "This street is not a row of buildings. It's a row of people who have decided to keep each other company. That is much harder to sell."

\* \* \*
:::

Hannah walked on after breakfast, up past the post office and the pharmacy, and at the top of the hill she stopped by the wrought-iron gate of the old Methodist chapel, which had been closed since before she was born. The gate was rusted, the paint peeling, but someone had planted a row of small white crocuses along the path, and they were coming up through the last of the snow, bright and stubborn.

Biscuit sat down beside them and watched them as if they might attempt an escape.

"Don't eat those," said Hannah. "They're not for dogs."

She crouched down and touched one. The petals were cold and papery, and under them she could see the faintest green of the stem pushing up, determined, as though it had been waiting all winter to be noticed. She thought of her grandmother kneeling in the little garden behind the bakery every spring, her hands black with soil and her face tilted up to the sun, muttering to the plants as if they were difficult customers.

"You'd have liked these," she said to nobody in particular.

From the far end of the path, a voice answered her.

"Everybody likes them. That's why somebody planted them."

She looked up. A woman in a green wool coat was standing by the chapel steps with a watering can, her gray hair cut very short and her face weathered by years outside. She looked to be about seventy, and she had a smile that seemed to have been used sparingly and was all the more valuable for it.

"Edna Price," said the woman. "I garden for the chapel trust. Or I did, until the trust forgot to pay me. Now I just garden for the crocuses."

"I'm Hannah. Dorothy's granddaughter."

"I know. You look like her when she's annoyed." Edna Price set the watering can down. "I planted these the spring after Hollis died. He used to bring me seeds from the store and tell me they'd never grow in this soil, and I'd tell him to mind his own business. We were very rude to each other for thirty years." She glanced at the crocuses. "It's the only friendship I ever had that didn't need a reason."

Hannah felt her throat tighten.

"Did you know my grandmother well?" she asked.

"Better than anybody in town," said Edna Price. "Better than the bank. Better than the mayor. She used to come up here every morning with a loaf of bread for the choir, and she would sit on that bench and tell me everything that was wrong with the world. Then she would go back to the bakery and fix a little bit of it." She smiled, very faintly. "She fixed a lot of things, your grandmother. Including you, apparently. She sent you back here, didn't she?"

"She didn't send me back," said Hannah. "She left me the building. I came back to sell it."

"And?"

Hannah looked at the crocuses.

"I haven't sold it," she said.

"No," said Edna Price, with deep satisfaction. "I didn't think you would. Crocuses don't come up in a place nobody plans to stay."

\* \* \*
:::

Hannah came back down the hill with the dog trotting ahead, his tail up, and the sun finally breaking through the clouds over the river. The whole street was shining with the thaw. Water ran in bright little channels along the gutters, and the awnings dripped, and somewhere up by the church a child was laughing at the sound of the bells.

She stopped in front of the bakery and looked up at the faded red lettering over the door, *THE CUTTER STREET BAKERY*, the paint worn pink by sixty years of weather. For the first time since she had walked through its doors, she did not think about the price of the building or the sum on the offer letter or the number of tenants who might turn up for a Pike Ridge consultation.

She thought about the walk. Walt and his pigeons. Mrs. Ortiz's terrible coffee. Edna Price and her crocuses, planted for a man who had brought her seeds she was sure would never grow.

Her grandmother had walked this street every morning for sixty years. She had known every name, every shop, every bench. She had kept a ledger of debts and favors and promises. And she had left behind, for the granddaughter who had run away at eighteen, a street full of people who were waiting for her to come back.

"All right," Hannah told the dog. "Now we do the bread."

Biscuit barked once, sharply, and led the way.

# Chapter Six: Teacups and Gossip

The regulars found out about the oven before the bread was cold.

Hannah had expected a trickle of curious visitors. What she got was a flood. By nine on Saturday morning there were six people waiting outside the bakery door, stamping snow off their boots and peering through the glass with the expressions of people who had come to see a ghost. By ten there were twelve. By eleven, Mrs. Ortiz from the flower shop had arrived with a casserole dish, a bag of oranges, and a grievance.

"You've been gone too long," Mrs. Ortiz announced, planting the casserole on the counter. "And you've been putting your grandmother's bread in the freezer. I could smell it through the wall."

"It's not in the freezer, it's on the rack."

"It was in the freezer last week. Eli told me. He said you couldn't light the oven and you were living on cereal." Mrs. Ortiz peered at her over the top of tortoiseshell glasses. "You look like a woman who's been living on cereal."

"I've been living on bread," said Hannah. "Mostly the heels."

"Good," said Mrs. Ortiz, approvingly. "That's the best part. Your grandmother always said so."

The crowd shuffled forward. Hannah had the strange sensation of being a new recruit in a very old army, one who had not been told the rules and was expected to know them anyway. The men from the hardware store and the diner wanted to shake her hand. The women from the library wanted to know whether she planned to bake the cherry scones for the Thursday book club, because they had been promised cherry scones since March and the promise had been very much on their minds. A boy of about nine wanted to know whether the oven was haunted. His grandmother pulled him away by his hood and apologized four times.

"It's not haunted," Hannah told the boy. "It's just old and stubborn."

"Like my grandpa," the boy said solemnly.

"Exactly like your grandpa," said Hannah, and the crowd laughed, and something in her chest loosened for the first time in days.

It did not last. At the far end of the counter, two women were whispering behind their hands, glancing at her and then at each other. She caught the name *Pike* and the word *sell*, and then the phrase *already decided*, and the warmth in her chest curdled.

"Excuse me," she said, a little too loudly. "Did you want something?"

The women looked up, flushed. One of them was the wife of the man who ran the diner. The other was a thin, neat woman in a camel coat whom Hannah did not recognize.

"Oh, we're fine," said the diner wife quickly. "Just wondering if it's true."

"If what's true?"

"That you're selling," said the stranger in the camel coat. She had a clipped, pleasant voice with an edge like a paper cut. "Garrett's office says there's a meeting scheduled for Monday. He's very eager to get the matter settled. Such a shame, really, a young woman trying to run a business she clearly doesn't want."

Hannah set down the bread knife with great care.

"I'm sorry," she said evenly. "Who are you?"

"Oh, forgive me." The woman smiled, and the smile did not reach her eyes. "I'm Bridget Sloane. I handle community relations for Pike Ridge. I was hoping to introduce myself and see whether you'd like to be part of the conversation."

"What conversation?"

"About the future of Cutter Street," said Bridget Sloane brightly. "Mr. Pike wants to see this block become a destination. Shops, restaurants, a little plaza by the river. Local businesses would be welcome, of course, on the right terms. Your grandmother was a wonderful baker, I'm told. We'd love to keep her name on the window."

"Is that a threat?"

The diner wife gasped. Bridget Sloane's smile did not move.

"Goodness, no," she said. "It's an invitation. Mr. Pike has been very patient. But the city has a lot of questions about the old buildings on this street. Wiring, drainage, the foundations near the river. He'd hate to see anyone get hurt because of something that could have been fixed years ago."

The crowd had gone quiet. Hannah could feel every eye in the room on her back, and she understood with a clarity that made her stomach clench that this was not a conversation between two women. It was a performance, staged for an audience of people who would tell the story at their dinner tables for weeks.

"I'll think about it," Hannah said.

"That's all anyone can ask," said Bridget Sloane. "Shall I leave my card?"

"No," said a voice from the back of the room. "She won't need it."

Everyone turned.

Eli Carver stood in the doorway of the back kitchen, wiping his hands on a rag, his shirtsleeves rolled to the elbow. He did not look at Bridget Sloane. He looked at Hannah, and his face was perfectly calm, but his eyes were not.

"The oven's running," he said. "Your bread's done in ten minutes. Mrs. Ortiz wants to make sure the rolls aren't the first thing she takes home, so I'd get on that if I were you."

The crowd laughed, uncertainly. Mrs. Ortiz snorted and started to rummage in her bag for money. Bridget Sloane's smile finally flickered, just once, before she gathered her camel coat around her.

"Mr. Carver," she said. "I didn't realize you were back in the business."

"I was never out of it," said Eli. "Good morning, Ms. Sloane. Tell Pike he can send his letters to the post office. Mrs. Brooks isn't taking mail here anymore."

"It's Ms. Brooks," said Hannah, before she could stop herself.

Eli's gaze flicked to her, and for a fraction of a second something warm and surprised crossed his face. Then it was gone.

"Ms. Brooks," he said. "Of course."

Bridget Sloane left without another word. The door swung shut behind her, and the bell rang, and the cold rushed in again. For a moment, the bakery was silent. Then Mrs. Ortiz clapped her hands together sharply, like a schoolteacher calling the class to order.

"Right," she said. "Who wants rolls?"

Everyone wanted rolls. The line formed again, louder and more cheerful than before, and the gossip turned to other things: the price of diesel, the church's new roof, the boy who had broken his arm falling off a trampoline. Hannah served them all with her hands shaking, and she did not look at the kitchen door.

When the last customer had gone, and the rolls were nothing but crumbs on the trays, Eli came out of the back carrying the timer and set it on the counter with a faint click.

"You didn't have to do that," she said.

"Do what?"

"Come in and make them laugh. Get rid of Bridget Sloane."

"I didn't get rid of her," said Eli. "She got rid of herself. She doesn't like it when people look at her too closely." He glanced at the window, where the snow was starting to thicken again. "And I didn't do it for you. I did it for the rolls. Mrs. Ortiz would have taken the whole tray if I hadn't."

"That's a lie."

"Probably." He almost smiled. "But you looked like you needed someone to be rude to her before you said something you couldn't take back."

Hannah leaned on the counter and let out a breath. "You know her?"

"Everyone knows Bridget Sloane. She's been in town for six months, looking at buildings. She rents a room above the post office and drinks too much coffee and doesn't know anything about bread." He paused. "But she knows exactly how to make people nervous. It's a talent. Pike pays her a lot of money to use it."

"Is he going to buy the street?"

"He's going to try," said Eli. "The question is whether he gets the people who matter on his side before Monday."

"Who matters?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and she realized, with a small, unwelcome shock, that he was waiting for her to work it out.

"The town council," she said slowly. "The bank. The people who own the buildings next to ours."

"And the people who buy the bread," said Eli quietly. "That's the one Pike forgets. He thinks a town is a list of names on a map. But it's not. It's the people who come in on a Saturday morning, even when the oven's broken, because they remember what it smelled like when they were children."

He turned to go, then stopped, his hand on the kitchen door.

"Your grandmother knew that," he said. "She used to say that a bakery was the only business in town that could feed a whole street and still make a profit on the side. I thought she was joking."

"And now?"

"Now I think she was right," said Eli, and went back into the kitchen before she could reply.

# Chapter Seven: The Developer's Offer

The offer arrived on Monday morning in a cream-colored envelope, hand-delivered by a young man in a suit that was too thin for the weather.

Hannah read it standing at the counter, with the radiator hissing behind her and a tray of cinnamon rolls cooling beside the register. The letterhead was embossed. The paper was heavy. The first page was a cover letter, full of warm, generic phrases about heritage, community, and *a shared vision for the future of Cutter Street*. The second page was the offer itself, and it made her sit down on the nearest stool before she had finished reading the first paragraph.

Four hundred and eighty thousand dollars.

For the building, the land behind it, and the business. For the recipes and the name. For the oven, which the letter described as *an interesting historical feature of limited commercial value*. For the right to *retain the Cutter Street name for heritage signage*, a phrase Hannah suspected had been drafted by Bridget Sloane.

It was, by any sensible measure, a great deal of money. It was more than she had ever seen in one place. It would clear the back taxes, pay off the credit card in her grandmother's name, and leave enough over to buy a small apartment somewhere with a view of something other than a loading dock.

She read it again. Then she read the third page, which contained the fine print, and her stomach dropped.

*The purchaser reserves the right to withdraw this offer should the property be found to contain structural, electrical, or environmental deficiencies that would materially affect its value. Any costs associated with the remediation of such deficiencies shall be borne by the seller.*

Hannah looked up. Eli was standing in the doorway of the back room, holding a box of wiring diagrams and a pair of pliers. She had not heard him come in.

"That's the one they gave you," he said. It was not a question.

"You've read it?"

"Bridget Sloane read it to half the diner on Saturday night. She does a very good impression of a lawyer." He set the box down on the counter. "Pike's been doing this for twenty years. He makes the offer big enough to make people think twice, and he hides the teeth in the paragraph they don't read."

"Which paragraph?"

"The one about deficiencies." He leaned over and tapped the page with a clean finger. "Right now, your wiring is a disaster, and that's before anyone opens a wall. If the buyer's inspector finds what I found yesterday, they walk away or they knock the price down to nothing. Either way, you pay for the fix, and the building's a shell by the time they're done."

Hannah stared at the words. "Yesterday?"

"Yesterday I went through the old panel in the basement. Two circuits are overloaded. There's a junction box behind the walk-in that's been spliced with tape. And the main line feeding the back wall has a hot spot I'd want to see with a camera before I'd sign off on it." He straightened. "It's not a fire waiting to happen. It's a lawsuit waiting to happen."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"I just did."

"Yesterday, Eli."

"I was going to tell you this morning." He had the grace to look a little guilty. "Then the envelope came, and I thought you'd want to read it first."

Hannah pressed her palms flat against the counter. The cold of the stone seeped up into her wrists. Outside, the street was gray and damp, and a plow truck rumbled slowly past with its orange light turning over and over, painting the snowbanks in bright, sharp bands.

"Four hundred and eighty thousand dollars," she said. "My grandmother spent her whole life in this building, and he thinks it's worth less than a sports car."

"He thinks it's worth what it would cost to make it disappear," said Eli. "That's a different number. Most people don't know the difference until it's too late."

"Then tell me what it would cost to fix it."

"Properly?" He pulled a notepad from his back pocket and flipped it open. "Rewire the back half, replace the panel, open the walls and find out what else is wrong. Maybe forty thousand. Maybe sixty. Maybe more if the foundation near the river has moved, and I think it has."

"I don't have sixty thousand dollars."

"I know."

"So I should take his money."

"I didn't say that." Eli's voice was steady. "I said you should know what you're deciding between. Right now, you're deciding between an offer that looks like a rescue and a building that's slowly falling apart. Pike wants you to pick the rescue before you see the cracks."

Hannah looked at him for a long moment. The radiator hissed. The bell over the door was silent. For the first time since she had come home, she felt the shape of the choice in front of her, and it was not the one she had been pretending it was.

"What would you do?" she asked.

Eli was quiet for a while. When he spoke, his voice was rough.

"I'd fix the wiring," he said. "I'd keep the oven running. I'd sell bread to people who remember what it tastes like. And I'd tell Garrett Pike to put his offer where the sun doesn't reach."

"That's a very specific answer."

"It's a very specific man." He almost smiled. "My father used to say that the easiest thing in the world is to sell something you're afraid of losing. The hardest is to keep it when everyone says you should let go."

"Is that why you stayed?"

"I didn't stay for the building," said Eli. "I stayed because my father did, and because the hardware store across the road is the only place in town that still sells nails by the pound, and because somebody had to keep the lights on for Mabel."

Hannah laughed, despite everything. It came out shaky. "You did all of that for a sourdough starter?"

"For a sourdough starter and a stubborn woman who wouldn't tell anyone she was scared," said Eli. "Your grandmother, not you. Although I'm starting to think you're the same."

She didn't have an answer for that. She picked up the offer letter, folded it in half, then in half again, and set it down under the register where her grandmother's notebook was waiting.

"I'm not signing anything today," she said.

"Good."

"And I'm going to need you to show me the wiring. All of it. Every circuit."

"I thought you'd never ask," said Eli. He picked up the wiring diagrams and handed her the top sheet. It was covered in his small, neat handwriting, with notes in the margins and arrows pointing to places she did not understand. "Start with the basement. And wear something that doesn't matter. It's going to be filthy."

"I don't own anything that doesn't matter."

"Then wear my old coat," he said, and went back into the kitchen before she could tell him what she thought of that idea.

# Chapter Eight: Starter Trouble

Mabel died on Wednesday.

Hannah found out at six in the morning, when she lifted the cloth from the crock and smelled nothing but flat, gray, lifeless flour. The surface was dry and cracked like old paint. The bubbles that had risen so faithfully every morning for a week had vanished, leaving behind a crust that looked more like cement than a living thing.

She stood at the shelf for a long time, holding the cloth in both hands.

She had never been good with living things. Her grandmother had been the gardener, the baker, the one who coaxed tomatoes out of clay and kept a jar of cuttings on every windowsill. Hannah had been the one who read books in the corner and made lists of what she would do when she got out of this town. Mabel had been her grandmother's, and her grandmother had been the only person Hannah had ever known who could make a sourdough starter feel like a member of the family.

It was ridiculous to cry over a crock of flour and water. She cried anyway, quietly, with the cloth against her mouth so she would not wake the radiator.

When she could see again, she called Eli.

He answered on the second ring, his voice thick with sleep. "What's wrong?"

"She's dead."

There was a silence on the line, then the sound of bedsprings and a long, weary exhale. "Mabel?"

"I don't know how else to say it."

"Don't touch anything. I'm coming."

He arrived in twelve minutes, wearing a flannel shirt buttoned wrong and a wool hat pulled low over his ears. He did not say anything when he came in. He crossed to the shelf, lifted the crock down, peered inside, and stood very still for a moment, the way people stand at a graveside when the service is over and nobody quite knows how to leave.

"She didn't die because of you," he said finally.

"Then why does it feel like I killed her?"

"Because you're tired and you haven't slept properly in a week and you're the only person left who knows what she sounded like when she was happy." He set the crock on the counter with a soft, decisive thunk. "Starters don't die overnight, Hannah. They get too cold, or too hot, or they go hungry for a day and nobody notices. Which one did it?"

"I don't know."

"Then we'll find out." He took off the wool hat and rubbed his face with one hand. "Let's start with the thermostat. Your grandmother kept the room at seventy-two. If the oven's been running cold and the back room's been drafty, the temperature could have dropped below the point where she could recover."

"You think it's the wiring?"

"I think it's probably my fault," said Eli. "I replaced the thermostat cable on Monday, but I didn't check the back-room sensor until yesterday. It was reading six degrees too cold." He glanced at her, and his jaw tightened. "I should have checked it sooner."

"You couldn't have known."

"I should have known," said Eli. "That's the job."

Hannah looked at the crock, at the crust and the gray, and felt something hard and bitter rise in her throat. "She'd have yelled at both of us."

"She'd have yelled at me," said Eli. "She'd have said, *Eli Carver, you have hands like a pair of shovels and you can't tell the difference between a thermostat and a teakettle.* And then she'd have made us both eat a whole loaf of rye."

Hannah laughed through her tears, and the sound startled both of them.

"That's exactly what she'd have said," she whispered.

"I've been practicing." He almost smiled. Then he pulled a stool up to the counter, sat down, and nodded at the second chair. "Sit. We're going to try something."

"What?"

"Your grandmother kept a backup." He pulled a small glass jar from the pocket of his flannel shirt and set it on the counter between them. It held a thick, pale paste, wrapped in a cloth and sealed with a band of masking tape. The label on the tape was faded but legible. *MABEL II. Do not drown.*

Hannah stared. "Where did you get that?"

"From the freezer at the back of the walk-in, behind the lard," said Eli. "She told me about it in March. She said if anything happened to the first one, I was to bring the second one out and tell you to be patient. She also said you'd be impossible for at least three days."

"That's not fair."

"Nothing about this is fair." He unscrewed the lid and held out the jar. "Do you want to try?"

She took it from him. Their fingers brushed, and she felt a jolt of warmth at the contact that had nothing to do with the cold of the glass. She pulled her hand back a little too quickly, and he noticed. He did not say anything, but his eyes lingered on her face for a moment longer than was strictly necessary.

"We'll need to feed her," Hannah said, a little breathlessly. "Warm water. Flour. Not the cheap stuff."

"Your grandmother bought it from a mill in Ohio," said Eli. "It's in the storeroom. Behind the sacks marked *DO NOT TOUCH*. I've been living in fear of that sign for a month."

"Then why haven't you moved them?"

"Because I'm a coward," said Eli. "And because they're heavy."

They fed the second Mabel together, sitting side by side at the counter with the warm water running in the sink and the flour dusting both their hands. Eli measured. Hannah stirred. They argued about the ratio, and then about the temperature of the water, and then about whether the flour should be sifted, and somewhere in the middle of it, Hannah realized she had stopped crying and started laughing again.

By the time the sun came up over the river, the starter was sitting in a clean bowl under a damp cloth, looking unremarkable and very hopeful.

"Check her tomorrow," said Eli, standing up and stretching until his spine cracked. "If she's bubbling, we've got a chance. If she's not, we'll try again."

"And if she's still dead?"

"Then we'll try a third time," said Eli, "and a fourth, and a fifth, and we won't stop until she either comes back or the bakery burns down."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only one I've got," he said.

He pulled his hat back on and turned toward the door. Then he stopped, one hand on the frame, and looked back at her. The early light came through the front window and laid a pale gold stripe across his face. He looked tired, and worried, and very much alive.

"You did good, Hannah," he said quietly.

She didn't trust her voice, so she only nodded.

He left, and the bell rang, and the starter sat in its bowl on the counter like a small, stubborn promise, and Hannah stood in the warm sour air of her grandmother's kitchen, wondering how long it would take her to stop noticing the way Eli's voice changed when he said her name.

# Chapter Nine: Mabel's Birthday

Mabel the second turned one month old on a Sunday in mid-October, and nobody on Cutter Street was willing to let the occasion pass without a fuss.

Hannah had not planned a party. She had planned a quiet morning with a cup of tea and the crossword from the Dayton paper, which she had been trying to finish since August. But at seven o'clock, Mrs. Ortiz came through the back door without knocking, carrying a cake box the size of a suitcase, and announced that a starter could not be expected to grow up without a proper celebration.

"She's a jar of flour and water," said Hannah.

"She's a member of this family," said Mrs. Ortiz, opening the box with a flourish. Inside, on a bed of tissue paper, sat a cake decorated with marzipan roses and a single candle shaped like a loaf of bread. Someone had written across the top in pink icing: *HAPPY BIRTHDAY MABEL. YOU ARE NOT A PET.*

"Who wrote that?"

"Tom," said Mrs. Ortiz. "He says it's a joke about Biscuit. I've never understood his jokes, but I've decided to respect them."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

Eli arrived at half past seven, with his hair still wet from the shower and a paper bag of pastries from the diner under one arm. He stopped in the doorway of the kitchen, took in the cake, the candle, and the jar of bubbling starter sitting in the middle of the table like a guest of honors, and burst out laughing.

"You're not going to let it go," he said.

"You're not going to let it go either," said Hannah, "because you helped me feed her every night for a month."

"I fixed the thermometer," said Eli, with dignity. "That was a vital contribution."

He set the pastries down, kissed her on the cheek, and then leaned over the jar to peer at the bubbles with a look of such serious attention that Hannah had to bite her lip.

"She looks well," he said.

"She looks like she's been ready for this since the day we met her."

"She's the most stubborn thing in this building," said Eli, "after you."

Mrs. Ortiz made a noise that might have been a laugh or a sob, and hastily began slicing the cake.

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

They ate on the back step, in the thin autumn sunshine, with the cake on a tray between them and the dog lying across their feet like an overfed draft excluder. Walt came down from the library and sat on the edge of the step with a slice in one hand and his cap in the other, and told them, very solemnly, that in his opinion the starter was the finest thing to have happened to Cutter Street since the mill closed.

"Better than the mill," he said. "The mill never gave anybody anything but cough."

"Better than the Winter Harvest?" asked Hannah.

"The Winter Harvest is a very fine thing," said Walt. "But it only happens once a year. A starter is a daily affair. You have to feed it and look after it and be a little bit afraid of it. That's the mark of a proper relationship."

Eli choked on his cake.

"Don't look at me," said Walt, with great dignity. "I was married for forty-one years. I know what I'm talking about."

Tom Kowalski came up the lane with a tin of flapjacks, a fresh bandana tied around his head, and an expression of enormous, nervous pride. He had brought, as he put it, a small contribution for the guest of honors. The contribution was a tiny wooden spoon, carved by hand, with the name *Mabel II* burned into the handle in slightly uneven letters.

"I made it in the garage," he said, going red. "It took me three nights. I cut my thumb twice. Your grandmother would have called it rubbish."

"She would have kept it on the windowsill," said Hannah, and took the spoon from his hands, and felt her throat go tight. "She'd have kept it right next to the jar and told everybody who came in that the baker's assistant made it with his own two hands."

Tom looked at her, and then at the dog, and then at the cake, and his face went very pink indeed.

"Well," he said. "That's all right, then."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

Later, when the others had gone and the tray was empty and the sun had slipped low over the rooftops, Eli and Hannah stayed on the step, with the jar on the windowsill beside them and the wooden spoon standing in its mouth like a tiny flag.

"A month," said Eli softly. "Can you believe it?"

"No," said Hannah. "I can't believe anything about this autumn."

"It's been a good one," said Eli. "Even the terrible parts."

"Especially the terrible parts," said Hannah. "I'd never have fed her if I hadn't been scared out of my wits about the oven."

He smiled, and leaned his shoulder against hers, and for a while they simply watched the light change over the river.

"Do you know what she is?" Hannah asked eventually.

"A starter," said Eli.

"No. She's the reason I stayed. The first morning I came back, I was going to throw her out. I thought she was a mess. A dead jar of paste and a grief-stricken bread bin." She laughed, very quietly. "Then I found the spoon in the drawer, with Gran's handwriting on it, and I thought, *Well, if she can keep a jar of flour alive for sixty years, I can keep a bakery open for one winter.*"

"And did you?"

"I did," said Hannah. "And here we are."

Eli took her hand, very gently, and turned it over in his, and traced the faint white scar on her thumb with the tip of one finger, the one she had got from the oven door when she was three years old.

"You didn't cry," he said.

"I cried later," said Hannah. "In the car."

"Of course you did," said Eli, and kissed the scar, very softly, as if it were the most precious thing in the world.

# Chapter Ten: Saturday Market

The idea came from Mrs. Ortiz, which meant it was already halfway to happening before anyone had agreed to it.

"A stall," she announced on Thursday morning, arriving at the bakery with her casserole dish under one arm and a clipboard under the other. "On the corner of Cutter and Main. Saturday. The town council owes me a favor, and I've told them the Cutter Street Bakery is going to be there whether they like it or not."

"I haven't said yes," said Hannah.

"You haven't said no," said Mrs. Ortiz. "That's how I know you're going to say yes."

Hannah looked at the clipboard. It was already covered in names: the diner, the library, the hardware store, the church bake sale committee, two of the Hendersons from the bank, and at the very bottom, in neat block capitals that she recognized at once, *ELI CARVER: TABLES, POWER, AND HEAVY THINGS.*

"He signed up?" she said.

"He signed up before I asked him," said Mrs. Ortiz. "He said somebody had to carry the flour. I think he's sweet on you."

"He is not sweet on me."

"Honey, I've known that boy since he was born. He's been sweet on you since the second grade, when you hit him with a snowball and he went home and cried into his cereal." Mrs. Ortiz tapped the clipboard. "Now, do you want a stall or not?"

Hannah thought about the empty bakery, the dead espresso machine, the bank statements under the register, and the letter from the county on the desk in the back room. She thought about the offer that was still folded under the till, waiting for a signature she did not intend to give.

"Yes," she said.

"Good girl." Mrs. Ortiz beamed and scribbled something on the clipboard. "I'll send over the table rental forms. You'll need a permit, a sign, a cash box, and a lot more bread than you think. Do you have enough flour?"

"I have Mabel."

"Mabel's a starter, dear, not a supplier." Mrs. Ortiz paused in the doorway. "But she's also the reason I still buy your rolls. So maybe you're right."

The Saturday market was a small, cheerful thing, held in a corner of the town square with a dozen folding tables and a stage made from two pallets and a painted sheet. In the summers, it drew tourists from the highway. In the winters, it was mostly locals: the people who had lived on Cutter Street for forty years and had no intention of moving to the new development by the highway, even if it had a swimming pool and a gym.

On Saturday morning, Hannah woke before dawn, dressed in the warmest thing she owned, and walked the four blocks to the square with a wheeled cart full of bread and a head full of doubts.

The square was already busy. Eli was there, of course, carrying tables two at a time from the back of a borrowed pickup truck, his breath misting in the cold air. He saw her and paused, a folding table balanced on one shoulder.

"You're early," he said.

"I couldn't sleep."

"Neither could I." He set the table down with a heavy thump. "Your stall's over there, by the lamppost. It's the best spot in the square, and Mrs. Ortiz says she stole it from the diner, so you're welcome."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. Wait until the crowd shows up." He nodded toward the cart. "Is that all the bread?"

"That's half of it."

"Good. Your grandmother always said to bring half of what you thought you'd need and sell out in the first hour." He picked up one end of the table and waited for her to take the other. "She was wrong, of course. She was never wrong about bread, but she was always wrong about how many people would show up."

They set up the stall together in silence, unfolding the tablecloth, arranging the baskets of rolls and loaves, and hanging the hand-lettered sign that Hannah had painted at two in the morning: THE CUTTER STREET BAKERY, EST. 1961. Under it, in smaller letters she had not been able to resist: *Still here.*

Eli looked at the sign for a long moment. "That's good," he said finally.

"Is it?"

"It's better than good." He touched the edge of the board, very lightly, and then took his hand away. "It's a promise."

By nine o'clock, the square was full. By nine-thirty, the line at the stall stretched to the corner of Main Street. People came in twos and threes, stamping their feet, rubbing their hands, calling out to one another across the crowd. They bought rolls by the dozen and loaves by the armful. A little girl in a pink snowsuit bought a cinnamon swirl with a handful of coins and then ran off, shrieking with delight, to show her grandmother. An old man with a cane bought three rye loaves and asked Hannah to tell her grandmother he was still waiting for the recipe.

"I'll tell her," Hannah said, and had to look down at the cash box for a moment before she could go on.

By eleven, the first basket was empty. By eleven-thirty, the second basket was gone. Eli had set up a folding chair behind the stall and was doing a brisk trade in coffee from the diner's urn, pouring for anyone who looked cold and charging them nothing for the privilege. Hannah saw him laugh at something Mrs. Ortiz said, a deep, rusty sound that surprised her with its warmth, and she felt something tighten in her stomach that she did not want to name.

At noon, she sold the last loaf. The cash box held more money than it had in a month. She stood behind the empty table and looked at the crowd, which was still thick and noisy and cheerful, and she felt the strange, unfamiliar sensation of being useful.

Eli came over, holding two cups of coffee. He handed one to her.

"Sold out," he said.

"Sold out."

"Your grandmother would be insufferable." He leaned against the lamppost beside her, the cup warm in his hands. "She'd make you tell everyone the story about the rye loaf that didn't rise until she sang to it."

"Did she really do that?"

"Every single time." He looked at her over the rim of his cup, his eyes crinkling at the corners. "I think she was lonely, after your mother left. I think the bread was the only thing that didn't ask her how she was doing."

Hannah looked down at the coffee in her hands. "I didn't know that," she said softly.

"No," said Eli. "You were in Chicago."

It was not an accusation. It was barely even a remark. But it landed in the quiet space between them with the weight of eleven years, and Hannah found she had nothing to say to it.

The crowd drifted around them, loud and bright. A child laughed somewhere. The church bell rang twelve times across the square. And Eli Carver stood beside her under the lamppost, close enough that she could smell the cold air and the coffee and something warmer underneath, and he did not look away.

"Hannah," he said quietly.

"Yes?"

"Your sign's crooked."

She looked up. The board had shifted in the wind, tilting slightly to the left. She reached up to straighten it, and his hand came up to steady the other side, and for one long, suspended moment their fingers were touching on the cold wooden edge of the sign, his knuckles brushing hers.

Neither of them moved.

Then a voice called from across the square, shrill and cheerful: "Eli! Your coffee's getting cold!" and the moment broke, and they both stepped back, and Hannah pretended very hard that her heart was not hammering against her ribs.

"I'll straighten it later," said Eli, not quite meeting her eyes.

"Right," said Hannah.

He walked away, toward the diner's urn and the laughing crowd. She stood at the empty stall with her cold coffee and the crooked sign above her head, and she did not straighten it, because she did not want him to come back and do it for her.

# Chapter Eleven: Sold Out by Four

The idea of a market stall had been Mrs. Ortiz's, which meant that it had been decided, paid for, and advertised on the church noticeboard before Hannah had been consulted at all.

"It's three tables," Mrs. Ortiz said, on the Thursday before the first Saturday in November. "Two in front of the bakery, one in front of the hardware store. Eli says he'll bring a generator. Walt says he'll bring his pigeons, which I told him was not a contribution. And you, mija, will sell bread."

"What if it rains on us?"

"Then they'll complain about the price in front of the whole town," said Mrs. Ortiz, "and you'll sell the bread anyway, and you'll come back the next week and sell twice as much. That is how business works. Your grandmother did it for thirty years."

Hannah looked at the sign-up sheet pinned to the corkboard by the till. Twenty-two names. Most of them she recognized: the florist from the corner, the woman who ran the paper shop, the two sisters who made soap, Tom Kowalski, who had put his name down for a table of sourdough rolls and then written beneath it, in capitals, *ASK ABOUT THE DOG.*

"Are these all going to sell things?" she asked.

"Some of them are going to sell things," said Mrs. Ortiz. "Some of them are going to stand behind the table and tell everybody else what they should have bought. That's also a market."

The first Saturday of November was bright and cold, with a thin white frost along the curbs and a sharp wind that carried the smell of woodsmoke from the chimneys on Mill Lane. By eight o'clock the three tables were set up in front of the shops, draped with red cloth, and every surface was covered in bread.

Hannah had been up since four. She had shaped and proofed two hundred rolls, a dozen rye loaves, and eight enormous sourdough boules that had taken three attempts to get right because her grandmother's old banneton had cracked in the bottom and she had to make a new one out of a laundry basket and a dish towels. Eli had laughed for a full minute when he saw it, then carried the basket up the street with a face of enormous dignity, as though it were a crown.

By nine, the first customers had arrived. By ten, the line stretched past the hardware store and around the corner onto the square.

It was not what Hannah had expected. She had imagined a few sympathetic faces, a polite word about the oven, a handful of coins. Instead, the street filled with people who wanted to talk. They picked up every loaf and sniffed it and asked what was in it. They asked about her grandmother, and whether the oven had really been dead when she arrived, and whether it was true that the bakery had been given to her in a will with a list of instructions pinned to the lid of the bread bin. They asked whether she was going to sell, and then, before she could answer, they told her what they thought she should do.

"Keep it open," said a stout man in a cardigan, sniffing a rye. "The Saturday crowd will come back. My wife will make sure of that."

"Sell it to Pike and get out," said a thin woman with a shopping bag, not unkindly. "That man has money and nobody in this town can afford the wiring."

"Keep it open, but raise the price on the rolls," said a third customer, to general laughter.

Hannah found, to her surprise, that she was enjoying herself. She had sold bread to people before, in Chicago, at the firm's Christmas fair, but those had been the wealthy and the polite, people who wanted to be seen buying something artisan. These people were different. They were loud, and nosy, and impossible to please. They haggled over the price of a single roll, and then bought six. They argued with each other about whose child had the best taste in cinnamon. And every single one of them came back to the table and said, at the end, "Tell your grandmother I said hello."

Hannah began to say that her grandmother was dead, then stopped, and said instead, "I'll tell her."

"I told them it's just flour and water," he said, when Hannah came over to see how he was getting on.

"What did they say?"

"They didn't believe me." He shook his head, bewildered. "The lady from the library wanted to know whether I used a special yeast. I said it was the starter. She said she'd come back for the starter. I said the starter is not for sale. She said everything is for sale. I said not the starter."

Eli had brought the generator, as promised, and set it up behind the hardware store's back wall, where it coughed and grumbled and powered the coffee urn that Mrs. Ortiz had insisted on guarding with a dish towels over her arm. "People are bringing me knives," he said, when she came over at lunch with two cups of coffee and a rye roll he had not yet asked for. "Scissors. A hatchet. Somebody brought a sword. I told him it was a letter opener, and he said that was a very generous lie, and I said he should keep it."

"Do you have time for a break?"

"I've got time for one roll," said Eli. He took it from her hand, and his fingers brushed hers, and he looked down at the bread with an expression of such open, undisguised admiration that she felt her face go hot.

"Yes," he said simply.

And Hannah, who had spent most of her adult life pretending that she did not need anything from anyone, put her hand on Eli's arm and left it there, in full view of the entire market, while the bread sold out one loaf at a time.

By four o'clock, the tables were bare. The sisters had sold every bar of soap, the florist had sold out of chrysanthemums, and Tom had given his last six rolls to a little boy who had been standing by the bench with his mother's shopping bag and his nose pressed to the glass. The hardware store had sharpened eighty-three knives, one sword, and a rather alarming garden shears, and Eli's hands were covered in oil and his voice was hoarse.

Mrs. Ortiz came out from behind the diner's counter, carrying a notebook and a pencil and a look of grim satisfaction.

"Four hundred and twelve dollars," she announced, to the square in general. "The bakery's share is three hundred and eighty. The rest goes to the church, the library, and Walt, who says he is owed for pigeon food and will not accept an argument."

"That's more than I expected," said Hannah.

"That's because you're a Brooks," said Mrs. Ortiz, and closed the notebook with a snap. "Your grandmother sold out every Saturday for thirty years. Nobody ever knew why. She said it was the bread. I always thought it was the street."

She looked up the length of Cutter Street, at the lamps coming on one by one in the early dusk, and her face softened.

"Next Saturday," she said, "we do it again."

"Next Saturday," said Hannah, and found that she was already planning it in her head: a larger banneton, a second batch of starter, another table for the children who liked the dog best. She had come home to sell a building. She was beginning to suspect that she had come home to run a market.

# Chapter Twelve: Late Nights in the Kitchen

The espresso machine was the last thing Eli fixed, and he did it on a Tuesday night, which was how Hannah ended up in the kitchen at eleven o'clock with her sleeves rolled up, a bowl of dough rising beside her, and a man she did not entirely trust elbow-deep in the guts of an Italian steam pump.

"It's not the pump," he said, without looking up. "It's the gasket. Somebody glued it with superglue in about 1998."

"That was my grandmother."

"I know. She told me. She said it was the only thing she'd ever done to a machine that she was ashamed of." He pulled out a small rubber ring, held it up to the light, and grimaced. "She also said it was the best coffee in the county, so I'm not sure she was ashamed of anything."

Hannah laughed and went back to folding the dough. It was a simple recipe, the one her grandmother had taught her at the age of six, standing on a step stool with flour in her hair. Water, flour, salt, and time. Time was the part no one could hurry.

They had been working late every night for a week. It had started with the wiring, which Eli had insisted on finishing before the building inspector came around on Friday, and had turned into everything else: the walk-in door, the freezer compressor, the drain in the back room that smelled like a swamp in August. Hannah had discovered that she could cut a tray of rolls with her eyes closed and that Eli could sing every word of an eighties power ballad without once missing a note. She had also discovered that she liked him, which was the most inconvenient discovery of her life.

"You're staring," said Eli.

"I'm not staring. I'm thinking about the dough."

"The dough isn't going anywhere."

"Neither are you, apparently."

He glanced up, and the corner of his mouth lifted. "Is that a complaint?"

"It's an observation."

"Your observations are very judgmental, Hannah." He set the gasket down and wiped his hands on a rag. "Did you know that? You've been judging me since you walked into the bakery with that suitcase."

"I haven't been judging you."

"You judged the oven. You judged my boots. You judged the way I eat a sandwich." He ticked the items off on his fingers, his eyes bright. "You judged my coffee, which was perfectly reasonable, I'll grant you."

"Your coffee is terrible."

"It's the diner's coffee. I'm not allowed to make it better." He pushed off the counter and came around to her side, stopping a respectful distance away, close enough that she could see the flour in his dark hair and a faint scar on his jaw she did not remember. "Do you want to know what I think?"

"Not particularly."

"I think you came back to sign papers and get out as fast as you could," said Eli. "And I think you've been here since the summer, and you've made bread that makes grown men cry, and you're terrified that if you stay, you'll never leave."

Hannah's hands stopped moving on the dough.

"That's not fair," she said quietly.

"No," said Eli. "It isn't. I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize for telling the truth."

"I wasn't apologizing for the truth. I was apologizing for saying it in the middle of a Tuesday night while you're covered in flour and I'm holding a gasket." He smiled, ruefully. "I've got terrible timing."

"You've got terrible everything," said Hannah, but her voice wobbled.

For a moment neither of them spoke. The oven hummed. The starter bubbled in its jar, a sign that Mabel the second was thriving two months on and had long since stopped sulking. The clock above the sink ticked past eleven-fifteen. Outside, a truck rumbled past on Cutter Street, its headlights sweeping across the window and then gone.

"Tell me about Chicago," Eli said.

She hesitated. "It was good. For a while."

"What happened?"

"I got a job at a firm that did corporate events. Conferences, galas, product launches. I was very good at it." She shrugged, looking at the dough. "I was very good at making people believe in things that weren't real for an evening. Then I'd go home and eat cereal over the sink and wonder why I felt like a fraud."

"Did you ever bake?"

"Not once. I told everyone I was too busy. I think I was scared."

"Of what?"

"Of finding out I wasn't as good as she was," said Hannah. "Of coming back and seeing the bakery without her in it and having nothing to say."

Eli was quiet for a moment. Then he reached past her and touched the edge of the bowl, lightly, with two fingers.

"It's rising," he said.

"It's always rising."

"Your grandmother used to say you could tell a good dough by the way it breathed." He tilted his head, studying the surface of the dough, which had risen perfectly under the cloth. "This one's breathing like it's been waiting for you."

Hannah laughed, a little unsteadily. "That's the most ridiculous thing I've ever heard."

"It's the truth," said Eli. "I've been listening to that dough for two weeks. It's been asking where you went."

She looked up at him. He was standing very close now, his hand still resting on the edge of the bowl, his face serious and unguarded in the yellow light. There was flour on his cheekbone and a small smear of grease on his chin, and she had the sudden, absurd urge to wipe it away with her thumb.

She did not do it. She stepped back instead, and her hip bumped the counter, and the spoon she had been holding clattered to the floor.

They both bent to pick it up at the same time and nearly knocked heads.

"Sorry," said Eli, catching her elbow to steady her.

"It's fine."

"It's not fine, you almost fell."

"I didn't almost fall."

"You almost fell into a bowl of dough, Hannah, and I'd like to point out that this is exactly why I'm not allowed to work late with you."

"Then leave," she said, and it came out too sharp, too fast.

He let go of her elbow. He straightened slowly, and for a moment his expression was shuttered, closed off in the way it had been that first morning in the doorway.

"Right," he said. "Of course."

He turned to pick up his jacket from the back of the chair, and she saw the stiff line of his shoulders and felt her stomach drop.

"Eli," she said.

He stopped. He didn't turn around.

"I didn't mean that."

"I know." His voice was quiet. "But you meant something."

She didn't answer, because he was right. He pulled on his jacket, took his toolbox from the floor, and walked to the back door without looking at her.

"Check the dough at one," he said, pausing with his hand on the frame. "It'll need another hour in the cold. Don't let it rush."

"I won't."

"And Hannah?"

"What?"

"Your coffee's terrible, too."

She laughed, helplessly, and the sound followed him out into the cold night air, and the door clicked shut behind him, and the dough breathed quietly under its cloth, waiting for her to decide what she wanted.

# Chapter Thirteen: A Dog Named Biscuit

The dog arrived on Thursday, sitting on the bakery step in the rain like he had been waiting for an appointment. He had walked the length of Cutter Street with Hannah once before, on a Monday in early autumn, and had apparently decided the bakery was the best stop on the route.

He was a big, shaggy, apricot-colored creature with a tangle of curls, a tail that swept the wet sidewalk, and a pair of brown eyes so earnest that Hannah opened the door before she had decided whether to. He padded inside as if he owned the place, sniffed the air with great seriousness, and then sat down beside the bread rack and looked up at her expectantly.

"No," said Hannah.

The dog thumped his tail twice against the tiles.

"You can't be in here. This is a food establishment."

He lay down with a sigh, his chin on his paws, and gazed at the rolls cooling on the rack with an expression of deep, sorrowful longing.

"Oh, for heaven's sake," said Hannah, and went to find a towel.

By the time Mrs. Ortiz arrived with her usual casserole dish and her usual opinions, the dog had been dried off, fed a small piece of plain turkey, and installed in the corner by the radiator with a folded blanket that had once belonged to Dorothy Brooks. He was snoring.

"Oh, that's Biscuit," said Mrs. Ortiz, peering over the top of her glasses. "He's not yours, dear. He belongs to Tom Kowalski at the feed store, but he's been running loose since his wife left him for a pastry chef in Dayton. Tom can't keep him out of the street, and Biscuit has decided that Cutter Street is his personal property."

"He's very well behaved."

"He's a nuisance," said Mrs. Ortiz fondly. "But he's a good judge of character. He sat outside your grandmother's door every Thursday for twelve years. Wouldn't let anyone else in."

Hannah looked at the dog, who opened one eye, considered her with the serene dignity of a creature who had never been in a hurry in his life, and closed it again.

"I'll call Tom," she said.

"Do that. But don't be surprised if he's back on your step by Friday." Mrs. Ortiz set the casserole down on the counter with a thump. "Now. Is that bread for the church or for me?"

"It's for whoever pays for it."

"Then it's for me," said Mrs. Ortiz, and handed over a crumpled ten-dollar bill with the air of a woman doing a great favor.

Tom Kowalski came by at four, a tall, sad, slump-shouldered man in a feed-store cap, and he stood in the doorway with his hands in his pockets and apologized three times before he saw the dog.

"Biscuit," he said, in the tone of a man who had been defeated many times by the same opponent.

Biscuit rolled over onto his back, all four paws in the air, and wagged.

"He's been here since morning," said Hannah. "He seems to like it."

"He likes everywhere with food." Tom sighed and crouched down, scratching the dog's belly. "He broke into the feed store twice last week and ate a bag of chicken pellets. Vet says he'll be fine, but I'm running out of places to put him."

"You can leave him here," said Hannah, before she could think about it. "Until you sort something out."

Tom looked up, surprised. "You'd do that?"

"He's no trouble."

"He's a lot of trouble," said Tom, but there was a grateful lightness in his voice. "Thank you. Really. I'll bring his food over tomorrow."

When he had gone, Biscuit climbed back to his feet, shook himself thoroughly, and sprayed the entire bread rack with water. Hannah shrieked. Biscuit looked delighted with himself.

"That's it," she said. "You're sleeping in the cold room."

He wagged, and then leaned against her legs with his whole weight, and sighed as if she had made his day.

Eli came in at six, carrying a bag of dog food under one arm and a paper cup of coffee in the other. He stopped dead in the doorway and stared at the dog, who was now asleep on the blanket again, one paw twitching.

"Is that Biscuit?" he said.

"He's been here all day."

"Tom's been looking for him since lunch. He called the diner and the post office and half the church choir." Eli set the dog food on the counter. "I told him you'd probably be the one he'd end up with."

"Why?"

"Because you can't say no to anything with four legs and big eyes," said Eli. "I've seen you with the Hendersons' cat. You let it sleep on the cash register."

"That cat was very cold."

"It was in the middle of August."

"It was a very cold cat," Hannah insisted. Then she looked at the dog again, at his tangled curls and his drooling smile, and she laughed. "He's sweet, though."

"He's a menace," said Eli. He crouched down, very carefully, and held out one hand. Biscuit lifted his head, sniffed the air, and then slowly stood, tail swaying. He walked over to Eli, put his nose in the man's palm, and sighed deeply, as if the matter had been settled.

Eli scratched behind one floppy ear. The dog leaned into it with his eyes half closed.

"He likes you," said Hannah, a little surprised.

"Everybody likes me," said Eli. "It's my most annoying quality." He looked up at her from his crouch, and there was a softness in his face she had not seen before. "Your grandmother used to say that a dog is the only honest judge of a person. If he sits by your door, you can trust you're not a fool."

"And what does it mean that he's sitting by mine?"

"That you're doing better than you think," said Eli, and went back to scratching the dog's ears.

Hannah stood at the counter and watched him for a while. The bakery was warm and quiet, the radiator ticking, the rain tapping gently against the window. The dog snored. The man talked softly to him, nonsense words, the kind people use with animals and children. And Hannah found that she had to look away, because the feeling in her chest was far too close to something she had sworn she would not feel again.

"Tom's coming back at eight," she said, a little too briskly. "He wants to take Biscuit home."

"Then we'd better feed him," said Eli, and stood up.

They fed Biscuit together at the back door, the dog eating with great dignity from a bowl Eli had found in his truck. Afterward, Biscuit trotted to the front of the shop, circled three times on the rug by the door, and lay down with a thump that rattled the glass.

"He's not going anywhere," said Eli.

"Neither am I," said Hannah, and then wished she had not said it at all.

Eli looked at her. For a moment, neither of them moved.

"Good," he said softly.

Outside, the rain kept falling. Inside, the dog began to snore again, and the radiator clanked, and two people who had been very careful for two weeks stood in the warm, bread-scented quiet and tried very hard not to look at each other.

# Chapter Fourteen: The Roof on Mill Lane

The roof of the hardware store leaked in four places, and Eli had been patching all four of them since he was eleven years old.

Hannah found out about this on a Tuesday evening in late November, when a sudden storm rolled in off the river and water began to drip through the ceiling of the back office in a steady, patient rhythm. She came across from the bakery with a flashlight and a bucket and found Eli standing on a stepladder in the middle of the room, his hair plastered to his forehead, a plastic sheet in one hand and a look of profound exhaustion on his face.

"Don't say anything," he said.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say that the roof is older than your grandmother and I should call a professional."

"I was going to say that the bucket is in the wrong place," said Hannah, and moved it three feet to the left, directly under the drip. "There. Now it's a useful bucket."

He laughed, a tired, rueful sound, and climbed down. "Thank you. I've been trying to get that thing under the right spot for an hour. My father always said it was the only thing in the building that knew its own mind."

"Your father fixed this roof?"

"Every year." Eli leaned against the desk and wiped his face with the back of his arm. "Every year from when I was eleven, we'd go up there on the first clear day in spring and tar the seams and replace the flashing. He'd talk the whole time. About the store, the customers, the weather, the cost of nails. I'd hand him things and try not to fall off the edge."

"Did you ever fall off?"

"Once," said Eli. "I was thirteen. I stepped on a loose tile and went through the gutter, right down the side of the building, and landed in the trash bins behind the diner. Mrs. Ortiz pulled me out by the collar and said she was going to tell my father I'd been stealing cheese." He smiled, a little crookedly. "My father didn't say anything at all. He just climbed down the ladder, picked me up, and carried me home over his shoulder, past the whole street. I was so embarrassed I wanted to die. I think he was more scared than I was."

Hannah leaned against the wall beside the ladder and listened to the rain drumming on the old tin roof above them.

"Tell me more about him," she said.

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

Hollis Carver had been a big man with a quiet voice and enormous, careful hands. He had grown up on a farm outside Coshocton and come to Cutter Street at twenty-two, with a secondhand van, a box of tools, and a wife named Ruth who had once been a schoolteacher and never quite forgiven the town for making her give it up. They had opened the hardware store in 1978, when the mill was still working and the street was full of men in work boots, and they had raised a son in the rooms above the shop.

"Mom left when I was nine," said Eli, very simply, "so it was just Dad and me. He was terrible at talking about feelings. He thought they were a sort of weather, and you just had to wait for them to pass. So when I got angry, he'd take me up on the roof and make me help him with the tar. When I was sad, he'd make me sort the nails by size. When I was happy, he'd whistle, very badly, and make me whistle with him."

"He sounds like a very good father."

"He was a very frustrating father," said Eli, "and I loved him more than anybody I've ever known. He never once told me it was going to be all right. He just kept showing up on the roof."

Hannah swallowed. The rain had softened to a gentle patter, and the bucket under the leak was beginning to fill with a steady, reassuring tock.

"Did you ever tell him you were angry with him?" she asked. "For the roof, or the nails, or any of it?"

"No," said Eli. "Not once. I was too proud. I thought if I said it out loud, he'd leave the way Mom had, and I'd be standing in the rain with nobody to hand the tar to."

"Eli."

"I know." He looked at the bucket. "I know now. He knew too. I think that's why he wrote that letter. He wanted me to hear it from him, in the end, even if it was too late to say it back."

\* \* \*
:::

The storm passed by ten. The air smelled of wet earth and cold iron, and the street outside was silver under the lamps, every gutter running. Eli fixed the last flashing with a bent nail and a length of old copper, and when he came down the ladder, he was soaked to the bone and grinning like a child.

"That'll hold until spring," he said.

"You said that last year."

"I meant it last year. I mean it this year." He sat down on the edge of the desk and put his head in his hands, laughing softly. "God, I miss him. Every time it rains."

Hannah went and sat beside him. She did not say anything. She simply put her shoulder against his and stayed there, the bucket ticking softly under the drip, the storm easing slowly away over the river.

After a while, he lifted his head.

"Come upstairs," he said. "There's something I want to show you. It's not much."

The rooms above the hardware store had been empty since his father's death, Hannah realized, as he led her up the narrow stairs and through a door at the top. There was a kitchen with a formica table and two chairs, a sitting room with a sagging sofa and a radio the size of a small suitcase, and, at the back, a bedroom with a single window looking out over the river and the roofs of Cutter Street.

On the wall above the bed hung a child's drawing, pinned very carefully in a cheap wooden frame. It showed a tall man with enormous hands and a small boy on his shoulders, standing on the roof of a building with a red-painted sign on the front, and above them, in wobbling yellow crayon, a sun with a face.

Underneath, in faded pencil, a child's handwriting said: *DAD AND ME ON THE ROOF. WE ARE FIXING IT.*

"I drew that when I was seven," said Eli. "He never took it down."

Hannah pressed her fingers to her mouth.

"He kept everything," said Eli quietly. "Every bit of wood and nail and paper, every drawing, every letter. Gran used to say he'd keep a broken hinge for thirty years because it might come in useful one day." He smiled, a little helplessly. "I think he was waiting for somebody to come along and tell him what it was all for."

"Eli," said Hannah.

"Yes?"

"It was for you."

He looked at her for a long moment in the lamplight. Then he crossed the room, very slowly, as though he were afraid the floor might give way, and put his arms around her, and held on, while outside the rain stopped completely and the whole street went quiet, listening.

# Chapter Fifteen: The Town Meeting

The town hall smelled of floor wax, old radiators, and nervous people.

Hannah had been inside it exactly once before, at the age of seven, for a school talent show that had ended in a tangle of tinsel and a fire alarm. The room had not changed. The ceiling was still stained in the corner where the roof had leaked in 1998. The wooden chairs still squeaked. The flag at the front still hung a little crooked, and someone had hung a poster of the Spring Fair beside it, faded and curling at the edges, with a drawing of a Ferris wheel that looked as if it had been drawn by a child.

By seven-thirty, every chair was full. By eight, people were standing along the back wall, their coats still on, arms folded, faces pinched with the kind of anticipation that comes before a fight nobody wants to start.

Hannah sat in the third row beside Mrs. Ortiz, who had insisted on bringing a thermos of tea and a bag of ginger cookies. Biscuit had been left at home, under protest, with a strict instruction from Tom Kowalski to stay out of the feed store. Eli sat on the far side of the aisle, alone, his arms crossed and his face unreadable.

He had not looked at her once since they came in.

Garrett Pike arrived at eight-fifteen, through a side door that had been opened for him by a man in a gray suit. He was not what Hannah had expected. He was perhaps fifty, slim and well-groomed, with silver at his temples and an easy, confident smile that he gave to everyone who turned to look at him. He shook hands with the mayor. He nodded at the head of the council. He touched Bridget Sloane lightly on the shoulder as he passed her in the front row, and she smiled up at him as if he were the sun.

Then he stepped up to the lectern, placed a leather folder on top of it, and began to talk.

It was a good speech. Hannah would give him that, grudgingly, sitting in the third row with her hands knotted in her lap. He talked about history, about the old mill on the river, about the way small towns died when the young people left and the old buildings crumbled. He talked about jobs and parking and a children's playground by the water. He talked about *honoring the legacy of Cutter Street* while building something new on top of it, and he did it so smoothly that several people in the room nodded along without realizing they were doing it.

"And I want to be clear," he said, smiling warmly around the room. "I have no interest in forcing anyone to sell. Nobody is being pushed out. But I do believe that this street, these buildings, can have a future that is better than their past. A future that puts this town back on the map."

There was a murmur of agreement from somewhere near the front. Hannah saw Bridget Sloane nod, her camel coat draped over the back of her chair.

"Which is why," said Garrett Pike, "I'd like to invite Ms. Hannah Brooks to speak. Ms. Brooks is the granddaughter of the late Dorothy Brooks, and I know she has a great deal of love for this street, as I do. I'm sure she has ideas about how this project can honor her grandmother's memory."

The room turned to look at her.

Hannah felt the heat rise in her face, and then something cold and furious settled behind her ribs. She stood up slowly, aware of every eye on her back, and walked to the front of the room with her head high.

She did not go to the lectern. She stood beside it, facing the crowd.

"My grandmother," she said, and her voice was steadier than she expected, "refused Mr. Pike's offers for three years. She refused them in writing. She refused them in person. And she refused them again three weeks before she died, in a letter that I have here in my bag."

She held up the envelope. A murmur ran through the room.

"I don't know what Mr. Pike means by honoring her memory," Hannah said. "But I know what she meant. She meant that this street is not a list of properties on a map. She meant that the bakery on the corner has fed this town for sixty years, and that it will keep feeding it, whatever the developer's plans are."

Somebody near the back clapped, once, sharply. Then someone else joined in.

Garrett Pike's smile did not change, but his eyes narrowed a fraction.

"Ms. Brooks," he said pleasantly, "I admire your passion. But you're a long way from home, and you've only just come back. Perhaps you don't yet understand what it takes to keep a building like that standing. The wiring alone—"

"Is being repaired," said a voice from the far side of the room.

Every head turned.

Eli Carver had stood up. He was not looking at Pike. He was looking at the council, and his voice carried clearly across the silent hall.

"The wiring in that building," he said, "was condemned in a report that the city never filed. I know, because I was the electrician who found it. Then I spent three weeks fixing it, and I have the invoices and the photographs and the inspection certificate in my truck. If anybody wants to see them, they can come and look."

The silence stretched out, thick and uncomfortable.

"Mr. Carver," said the mayor, carefully. "Are you suggesting—"

"I'm not suggesting anything," said Eli. "I'm telling you what happened, and I'm telling you that the building is safe. It's safer than it's been in thirty years." He paused, and his gaze flicked across the room, finally landing on Hannah. It lingered there for a moment, steady and warm, and she felt her breath catch. "And I'm telling you that Dorothy Brooks was right."

He sat back down.

For a long moment no one spoke. Then Mrs. Ortiz turned to Hannah, eyes wide, and whispered, "Well, *that's* what he's been doing for two weeks."

Hannah could not answer. She stood at the front of the room, letter in hand, her heart hammering so loudly she was sure everyone could hear it.

Garrett Pike was looking at Eli now. The smile was gone. In its place was something colder and far more dangerous.

"Mr. Carver," he said softly. "I didn't realize you'd taken such a personal interest in the Brooks family."

"I didn't realize you'd taken such a personal interest in the city's inspection records," said Eli, without looking up.

The mayor cleared his throat loudly. "I think we've heard enough for one evening," he said. "Thank you, Mr. Pike. Ms. Brooks. Mr. Carver. We'll take the matter under advisement."

People began to rise, murmuring, buttoning their coats. Garrett Pike gathered his leather folder and stepped down from the lectern with a faint, polite nod to the crowd. As he passed the row where Eli sat, he paused.

"You'll regret this, son," he said quietly.

Eli looked up at him, expression unreadable. "Maybe," he said. "But I'll still know where the wires are."

Pike walked on. Bridget Sloane followed him out, her heels clicking on the wooden floor, and she did not look back.

Hannah sat down in the third row and realized her hands were shaking. Mrs. Ortiz poured her a cup of tea from the thermos and pressed it into her palms.

"That," said Mrs. Ortiz, "was the most exciting thing that's happened in this town since the Hendersons' goat got loose at the Christmas parade."

"He didn't have to do that," said Hannah.

"No," said Mrs. Ortiz. "But he did, and you should go and thank him before he disappears again."

When Hannah looked up, Eli was already at the door, his jacket over one shoulder, his back to the room. He did not turn around, but he paused, just for a moment, before he stepped out into the dark.

She was on her feet before she knew it, pushing past the crowd, shouldering through the coats and the murmured goodbyes, but by the time she reached the steps he was halfway down the street, walking fast, his shoulders hunched against the cold.

She stood on the top step of the town hall and watched him go, the letter still folded in her fist, and did not call his name.

# Chapter Sixteen: Frost on the Window

The morning after the town meeting, there was frost on the inside of the bakery window.

Hannah noticed it when she came down at five-thirty, half asleep and wearing two sweaters. The glass was patterned with feathery white ferns that curled out from the corners and met in the middle, and for a moment she simply stood and looked at them, as if they had been painted there by someone with a very steady hand.

The radiator had died in the night. The room was freezing. Biscuit was sitting beside the cold stove, leaning against the warm brick of the old chimney, looking betrayed.

"I know," said Hannah. "I'm not happy either."

She lit the gas ring under the kettle, put a loaf in the oven to proof, and checked the dough, which had risen slowly overnight under its cloth, breathing softly like a sleeping animal. Then she sat down on the stool by the counter, wrapped her hands around a mug of tea, and let herself think about the thing she had been avoiding since the town hall.

Eli had not come in yesterday. He had not answered her text. He had not answered the phone call she made at midnight, which had gone straight to voicemail and then to a message that said, simply, *This is Eli. Leave a message if it's important.* She had left one. It had been three sentences long, and she had deleted the last two.

She had kept the first one, though. *Thank you.*

She had meant it. She had meant much more than that, and she did not know how to say it without sounding either ridiculous or desperate, and so she had settled for two words and a silence, and now the silence was getting longer and louder every hour.

At six-thirty, her phone rang. It was Jess.

"You're up early," said her sister. "Or you never went to bed. I can hear it in your voice."

"I'm freezing," said Hannah.

"The heating's broken?"

"The heating's broken, Jess. The radiator's dead, the oven's fine, and I've got a dog sitting next to the chimney looking at me like I've personally betrayed him."

"Whose dog?"

"Tom Kowalski's."

"You're keeping a dog?"

"Temporarily."

"Hannah." Jess's voice softened. "Is this about Eli Carver?"

Hannah closed her eyes. "Why does everybody assume it's about Eli Carver?"

"Because you've said his name four times in the last ten minutes, and you haven't said anyone else's name once." Jess paused. "I saw the video."

"What video?"

"Somebody filmed the town hall, Hannah. It's already on the diner's Facebook page. Twelve hundred views." Jess sounded impressed. "The part where he stands up and says *Dorothy Brooks was right* got two thousand shares by lunch. Someone in the comments called him a hero. Someone else called him a rude, meddling electrician. And someone called you 'the fiery granddaughter,' which I think is supposed to be a compliment."

Hannah put her head in her hands. "Oh, God."

"Oh, God is right." Jess was laughing now, but there was an edge of warmth under it. "Did he do it for you?"

"He did it for Gran."

"Then why did he look at you like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like he'd rather be standing on the other side of the room, holding your hand, than in front of the mayor reading out invoice numbers." Jess sighed. "I watched it three times, Han. Pause at the second minute. There's a moment where he almost smiles."

Hannah said nothing. She was thinking about the moment at the town hall door, when Eli had paused, just for a breath, before he walked out into the dark.

"I don't know what to do," she said finally.

"Yes, you do," said Jess. "You just don't want to do it, because it's easier to pretend you're only here for the paperwork."

"I am only here for the paperwork."

"Hannah."

"I'm supposed to sell the building, Jess. That's the plan. That's what Gran's lawyer wants. That's what I've been planning since I got on the plane. And now there's a developer with a fancy speech and a woman in a camel coat, and the wiring is a disaster, and the oven's been running on a prayer, and I've got a sourdough starter with a name and a temper and a dog that won't leave the building." She took a breath. "And I can't stop thinking about the way he said my name."

There was a long silence on the line.

"Okay," said Jess quietly. "That's a start."

"A start to what?"

"To admitting that you want to stay," said her sister. "Whatever that looks like. Whatever it costs. You've wanted to stay since you were eighteen, Hannah. You just kept leaving so you wouldn't have to find out if you were brave enough."

Hannah didn't answer. Outside, the frost on the window was starting to melt, the ferns dissolving into clear, dripping lines. Through the gap, she could see the hardware store across the road. The light was on in the back window, the same warm yellow as before.

"I'm going to call him," she said.

"Good," said Jess. "Don't make it a speech. He hates speeches. He told Mom that once, at the Christmas party, and Mom said she'd never seen a man look so miserable at a plate of cookies."

"You're not helping."

"I'm always helping," said Jess cheerfully. "Call me after. I want details. Or, if you're too shy, I'll find the video and make you watch the second minute again."

Hannah hung up and sat very still for a long moment, listening to the water drip from the window and the dog breathing quietly by the chimney.

Then she picked up the phone, scrolled to his name, and pressed call before she could lose her nerve.

It rang four times. Then his voice came on, rough and sleepy, as if he had been awake for hours and had only just got up.

"Hannah."

"Hi," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"Is something wrong?"

"No. Yes. I don't know." She swallowed. "The radiator's dead."

A pause. Then, very carefully, "Are you cold?"

"I'm freezing."

"Stay where you are," said Eli. "I'm coming."

"You don't have to—"

"I know," he said. "I'm coming anyway."

He hung up before she could argue. She sat on the stool with the phone in her lap and a dog's warm weight sliding across her feet, and she let herself smile, just a little, at the frost melting on the glass.

# Chapter Seventeen: Where Is Biscuit?

The dog had been gone for forty minutes before Hannah noticed, which was the longest forty minutes of her life.

It had been a bright, cold morning in early December, the first properly frosty day of the year, and Biscuit had been lying in his favorite patch of sunshine by the bakery's back door, snoring gently, with his chin on his paws and one ear twitching at every passing sparrow. Hannah had turned around to fetch a tray of cinnamon rolls from the oven. When she turned back, the patch of sunshine was empty, and the back gate was standing open an inch.

"Biscuit?"

She ran into the yard. She ran around the front of the shop. She ran up the street to the church and back down again, calling his name until her voice cracked, and by the time she reached the hardware store, the dog was still nowhere to be seen.

Eli was on the front step with a box of screws in one hand and his mouth open in alarm. "What happened?"

"He's gone," said Hannah, and her voice shook. "He's gone, Eli. I turned around for two minutes."

"Okay." Eli put down the screws and grabbed his coat. "Okay. He can't have gone far. He's got no sense of direction and a very strong sense of smell. He'll have followed something."

"What if he's been hit by a car?"

"He hasn't been hit by a car," said Eli, with a firmness that was not quite convincing. "We're going to find him. Tom! Tom, come out here!"

\* \* \*

Tom Kowalski came running from the diner in his apron, his face white and his eyes wide, and within ten minutes half of Cutter Street had come out to help.

Mrs. Ortiz organized the search with the efficiency of a general. She sent the florist up towards the church and the paper-shop woman down towards the river, and she dispatched the two sisters who made soap to the trash bins behind the diner, in case he had found the leftover roast chicken. Walt the pigeon man sat on his bench with his cap in his hands and told everybody who passed that he had seen a dog go past the library at about nine, going in the direction of the boatyard, and was quite certain it was not Biscuit, because Biscuit had never been that brave.

"He's been brave since the day he arrived," said Tom, close to tears. "He chased a fox off the church steps last month. The vicar had to hold him back with both hands."

"He's a very brave dog," said Hannah, "and a very stupid one."

"He is not stupid," said Tom fiercely. "He's loyal. There's a difference."

Hannah ran a hand over her face. She was shivering, though she had not noticed the cold.

"I should never have let him out of my sight," she said.

"You did nothing wrong," said Eli, and took her hand, and pulled her gently towards the river. "Come on. Let's look by the water."

They found him at the far end of the boatyard lot, behind the plywood fence where Pike Ridge had cleared the ground two summers ago.

The fence had a gap at the corner, where someone had kicked out a panel, and the dog had squeezed through it. He was standing in the middle of a churned-up patch of mud with a broken chunk of old brick in his mouth, his tail wagging furiously, and beside him, half-buried in the frozen earth, was something long and gray and dripping, which turned out, on closer inspection, to be the handle of a rusted pump.

"Biscuit!" Hannah dropped to her knees in the mud and held out her arms. "You idiot. You absolute, glorious idiot. What have you done?"

The dog dropped the brick at her feet, wagged so hard his whole back end went sideways, and then, with great dignity, sat down in the mud and looked at her as though he had just solved a crime.

"He's found something," said Eli slowly.

He was not looking at the dog. He was looking at the ground.

Hannah looked too. The churned earth had been dug in a rough line along the edge of the old boatyard, and at the bottom of the trench, where the dog had been scrabbling, the mud had fallen away to reveal a long stretch of old stone wall, black with age and cracked straight through in a jagged line.

"Is that the river wall?" asked Hannah.

"That's the old mill race wall," said Eli, very quietly, crouching down beside her. "It's a hundred and forty years old. It's supposed to hold the bank back from the river. Somebody's dug right into the foundation." He put his hand on the broken stone. It was wet and cold. "Look at the crack. That's not from weather. That's from a drill."

"Pike's crew," said Hannah.

"Pike's survey," said Eli. "They were boring for the drainage test in October. Bridget Sloane told the council it was just a routine geotechnical check, but nobody ever told the council they'd gone through the foundation."

He stood up slowly. His face was grim.

"If that wall goes," he said, "the whole bank goes. Every building on Cutter Street with its back to the river is in danger. Including the bakery."

Hannah stared at him.

"And including the hardware store," said Eli. "Including Walt's library. Including the diner, the church hall, and the boatyard, which Pike is going to fill with his parking garage. He's not even trying to hide it anymore. He's just cracking the foundations and hoping the town will be too busy arguing to check."

Biscuit, who had lost interest in the wall and found a dropped sausage roll in the mud, sneezed loudly and wagged his tail at both of them.

"We need to tell somebody," said Hannah. "Mr. Dorsey. The council. The state."

"We need proof," said Eli. "A photograph won't be enough. Pike's lawyers will say the crack was there for years. The city will say it's a drainage issue and send a man in a van. We need someone who knows what they're looking at to put their name to it."

"Who?"

Eli was silent for a moment, looking out over the gray, churning river.

"There's a man in Columbus," he said at last, "who inspected this wall for the state in 1994. He wrote a report, and it's in the county archives, and he was a friend of my father's. He once told Dad that if anyone ever tried to dig into that wall, he'd know by the sound of the drill." He turned back to her, and his expression was determined. "I'll call him tonight. And tomorrow morning, I'm going to take the dog to the county records office, because he's going to be the best witness we've got."

Hannah began to laugh, helplessly, through the cold and the mud and the lingering fear.

"You're going to take the dog to court," she said.

"I'm going to take the dog to the records office," said Eli, "and he's going to sit under the table and sneeze at the clerk, and she's going to give us exactly what we need just to make him stop."

At the top of the street, Tom Kowalski came running down from the diner, his face streaked with tears, and when he saw the dog, he dropped to his knees on the sidewalk and gathered him up in both arms, mud and sausage roll and all.

"You stupid, stupid dog," he sobbed into the wet fur. "Don't you ever do that to me again."

Biscuit licked his face, once, very firmly, as if to say he had a perfectly good explanation and was not going to share it.

Hannah stood by the bakery door, with mud on her jeans and the cold seeping through her coat, and watched Cutter Street come out of its houses one by one to see the dog come home. The florist, the paper-shop woman, the sisters with their soap, Walt with his cap in his hands. Mrs. Ortiz came out of the diner with a tray of hot chocolate and a face like thunder, and when she saw the dog, she burst into tears, and then scolded Tom for ten minutes, and then gave the dog a hot chocolate with extra marshmallows.

Eli came up beside Hannah, wiping his hands on a rag, and leaned against the doorframe.

"Well," he said. "That was the most useful dog I've ever met."

"He's a menace," said Hannah.

"He's a very good judge of where the trouble is," said Eli, and then, more quietly, "We're going to need him."

# Chapter Eighteen: The Winter Harvest

The radiator was fixed by noon, and the Winter Harvest Festival began at four, and by five o'clock Hannah had forgotten that she had ever been cold.

Cutter Street had been closed to traffic since the morning. Strings of white lights ran from one lamppost to the next, sagging gently under the weight of the snow, and somebody had hung paper lanterns from the awnings in shades of orange and gold. The smell of cider and roasting chestnuts drifted from the diner's cart on the corner. A brass band from the high school was playing something cheerful and slightly out of tune near the church steps, and children in mismatched mittens ran between the stalls with their cheeks red and their voices high.

Hannah had been pressed into service on the bakery's table, which sat in front of the shop with a banner that Mrs. Ortiz had insisted on painting herself: CUTTER STREET BAKERY, HOME OF THE HEEL. Beneath it, a smaller sign said *Ask about the rye*, which was not Hannah's idea and which she had decided not to dispute.

She had baked for three days straight. Gingerbread men with currant eyes. Cinnamon rolls with orange glaze. Two dozen mince pies in her grandmother's tins, the crust crimped by hand in the old way, with a thumbprint of jam at the center of each. The display case was full, and the cash box was getting heavier by the hour, and for the first time since she had come home, Hannah felt something like pride.

Eli had not come to the bakery all afternoon.

He had come to the bakery that morning, of course. He had arrived at the back door with the radiator parts, a thermos of coffee, and an expression of such careful neutrality that she had almost laughed. They had fixed the heating in a silence that was more companionable than awkward, passing tools back and forth, arguing about the right size of wrench. When it was done, he had stood up, wiped his hands, and said, "There. You're warm."

"Thank you," she had said.

"You're welcome." He had looked at her for a moment, his eyes dark and unreadable. "Good luck with the festival."

"You're not coming?"

"I'm on the cocoa stand," he had said, and then, with the faintest twitch of a smile, "Mrs. Ortiz insisted."

Now, at five-thirty, Hannah looked up from the display case and saw him across the street, standing behind a steaming urn at the church stall, pouring cocoa into paper cups for a line of laughing children. He wore a red wool scarf that she had never seen before, and his hair was dusted with snow. He caught her eye, and lifted one hand, very briefly, in a small, uncertain wave.

She waved back.

"Oh, for goodness' sake," said Mrs. Ortiz, at her elbow. "Go and get a cup of that cocoa before the children drink the whole urn."

"I'm working."

"You're staring," said Mrs. Ortiz. "There's a difference. Go on. I'll mind the rolls."

Hannah went.

The street was crowded and loud and glittering with lights, and she had to push past a group of teenagers taking selfies beneath the lanterns and a man with a shopping bag full of wind chimes. When she reached the church stall, Eli was refilling the urn with a long-handled ladle, his sleeves rolled up, his scarf tucked into his coat.

"You're blocking the line," he said, without looking up.

"I'd like a cup of cocoa."

"There's a line."

"I'm a paying customer."

"You're a baker." He filled a cup, put a marshmallow on top with exaggerated care, and held it out. "Free for bakers. Don't tell anyone. The vicar will be furious."

She took the cup. Their fingers didn't touch this time, but she was aware of the space between them anyway, the way you are aware of a stove that is still warm.

"Thank you," she said.

"You said that already."

"I mean it more this time."

He looked at her then, really looked, and something in his face shifted. "Hannah," he said quietly. "About this morning—"

"Eli!" someone called from the back of the stall. "We're out of paper cups!"

He swore under his breath, very softly, and she laughed. "Go," she said. "Save the children."

"I'll come find you later."

"I'll be at the bakery."

"I know," he said. "I'll bring the dog."

She walked back across the street with the cocoa warm in her hands, the lights blurring above her, the band playing something tender and off-key, and she felt a small, bright flicker of hope she had not allowed herself since she first walked into the shop.

By seven o'clock, the crowd had thinned to a steady stream of families and late-evening strollers. The mince pies were gone. The gingerbread men had been reduced to a few crumbs and one lopsided survivor with a broken arm, which Biscuit had been given as a reward for good behavior. The cash box held more money than Hannah had ever seen in one day. She was tired, and sore, and happy in a way that made her feel slightly dizzy.

Eli came back just before eight, carrying an empty urn and two paper bags. Biscuit trotted at his heels, his tail held high, a red ribbon tied around his neck.

"Tom sent it," said Eli. "He says the dog's been demanding it since breakfast."

"He looks very dignified."

"He looks like a clown." Eli set the urn on the counter and handed her one of the bags. It was warm, and it smelled of cinnamon and sugar. "Doughnuts. From the diner. Mrs. Ortiz says they're terrible, but I think she's jealous."

"Did you eat one?"

"Three," said Eli. "I'm a terrible man."

She laughed and opened the bag. The doughnuts were misshapen and too sugary, and they were the best thing she had tasted in years.

They sat on the back step of the bakery in the cold, the door propped open behind them, the light from the kitchen spilling out across the snow. Biscuit lay between them with his head on Eli's boot, snoring softly. Across the street, the lanterns swayed in the wind. Somewhere on the corner, the band was packing up, the brass instruments catching the light as the players laughed and shouted to each other.

"It was a good day," said Eli.

"It was a very good day."

"Your grandmother would have loved it."

"She'd have said the doughnuts were disgusting."

"She'd have eaten six." He leaned back on his elbows, looking up at the sky, where the clouds were thinning and a few stars were showing through, sharp and cold. "She used to say that a festival is only as good as the people who stay until the end."

"Did she?"

"She did. She'd always stay until the last crumb was gone." He turned his head, and his eyes caught the light from the doorway. "I think you're the same."

Hannah swallowed. "I don't know if I am."

"You are," said Eli. "You've been here since the summer, and you haven't left once. Not even when you should have."

She didn't answer. She leaned sideways, very slowly, until her shoulder was touching his. He did not move away. The night was cold, and the step was hard, and the dog snored, and the stars came out one by one over Cutter Street.

"Eli," she said softly.

He turned to look at her. The distance between them was barely anything at all.

"Yes?"

She didn't know what she had been going to say. Whatever it was, she never found out, because Biscuit lifted his head, sneezed loudly, and then staggered to his feet and trotted into the bakery, tail wagging, as if he had just remembered there was a whole tray of gingerbread left in the kitchen.

Eli laughed, low and rough, and put his hand over his eyes.

"Well," he said. "That's one way to end a conversation."

"He's a menace," said Hannah.

"He's a very good judge of character," said Eli, and when he dropped his hand, he was smiling, and so was she, and neither of them moved to stand up for a long, quiet minute while the lanterns swung above them and the snow began, very gently, to fall.

# Chapter Nineteen: First Day, 1961

The county archive kept its oldest photographs in a gray steel cabinet on the bottom floor, and Mrs. Ortiz had the key.

"Your grandmother gave it to me in 1989," she said, unlocking the drawer with a flourish and a theatrical sigh. "She said she wanted me to have a copy of everything, in case she ever lost her memory, or her temper, or her lease. She was very dramatic about it. I said she was being ridiculous. She said I'd thank her one day."

"Did you?"

"I've thanked her every day since," said Mrs. Ortiz, and pulled out a gray cardboard box with the words *CUTTER ST — PERSONAL — DO NOT LOSE* written across the lid in faded blue ink. "Sit. We're going to look at the past. It's terribly boring, and it's going to make you cry."

Hannah pulled up a chair beside her at the long oak table. It was the first week of December, and the reading room was almost empty, the radiators ticking softly and the windows fogged with the warmth of the old building. Biscuit, who had been forbidden entry to the archive by a stern notice on the door, was waiting patiently outside with Tom Kowalski, and every few minutes his face appeared at the glass with an expression of tragic, betrayed loyalty.

Mrs. Ortiz lifted the lid of the box. Inside were dozens of photographs, black and white and fading to sepia, each one tucked into a paper sleeve, each one labeled in a careful hand.

"Nineteen sixty-one," said Mrs. Ortiz, and picked up the first sleeve.

The photograph showed a shopfront. The sign above it was painted in neat, slightly crooked letters, and beneath the sign, a young woman stood in the doorway with her arms folded and a look of fierce determination on her face. They argued about the price of eggs every morning. They never once argued about the bread."

"Did you find what you needed?" asked Tom.

"We found the photograph," said Mrs. Ortiz. "And the story. And the reason."

"Reason for what?"

"For everything," said Mrs. Ortiz, and linked her arm through Hannah's, and walked her slowly down the steps, past the lamplight and the frost and the dog's wagging tail, towards the warm lights of the diner.

# Chapter Twenty: Ledgers and Letters

The second ledger was hidden in the flour bin.

Hannah found it on Monday morning, while she was digging through the bottom of the storeroom for a sack of rye that she was almost certain her grandmother had kept there. The flour bin was an old galvanized tub, big enough for a grown man to sit in, and it had been pushed against the back wall beneath a stack of empty crates. She had to shift two of the crates before she could get her arm in far enough to feel the edge of something flat and hard beneath the flour.

It was a notebook, wrapped in a plastic bag and tied with string. The cover was green cloth, cracked and faded. On the front, in her grandmother's looping hand, were the words: *Accounts, Private. Not for the bank.*

Hannah sat down on the floor of the storeroom with her back against a sack of cornmeal and opened it.

The first page was dated 1988. The entries were neat, precise, in pencil and blue ink. They recorded every payment the bakery had made in the year before her grandfather's death, every bill, every supplier, every loan. There was a column of figures at the bottom of each page, adding up to a sum that made Hannah's throat tighten with a strange sort of grief.

Then, on a page dated the autumn of 1989, the handwriting changed.

The letters grew smaller and more careful. The pencil pressed harder into the paper. There was a note at the top of the page, underlined twice: *Hollis Carver, 1,500 dollars, cash, no paper. For the flour bill and the back rent. He wouldn't take it otherwise.*

Below, in a different pen, a second entry: *Paid back, 1990, all of it, plus the interest he wouldn't take. He said it was a favor. I told him no favors on Cutter Street. He said there was no such thing as a favor in this town, only a debt you hadn't yet been asked to repay.*

Hannah read it twice. She could hear the old man's voice in the words, dry and sharp and tender all at once, the voice of someone who had kept a hardware store open through three recessions by refusing to believe that anything was free.

She turned the page.

There was another list, and this one made her breath catch. It was headed *People Who Matter*. There were eleven names. Some of them she recognized: Mrs. Ortiz, the diner owner, the vicar, the baker's son who now ran the bakery supply in Dayton. Near the bottom, in blue ink, was a name she did not expect.

*Eli Carver. The boy has his father's hands. He will stay. Don't ask him to be a hero. Let him decide when to be one.*

Hannah pressed her hand flat against the page. The paper was soft under her palm.

"Oh, Gran," she whispered.

She heard the back door open and the sound of boots on the tiles, followed by the particular soft huff that Biscuit made when he was delighted to see someone. She quickly shut the ledger and pushed it under the sack of cornmeal, then stood up, dusting flour from her jeans.

Eli appeared in the doorway of the storeroom, a box of lightbulbs under one arm and a coffee in his other hand. He looked at her, then at the crates, then at the empty flour bin with a faint, puzzled frown.

"Did you lose something?"

"Yes," said Hannah. "I found it."

"What was it?"

"Your father's name," she said, and watched his face go very still.

He set the box of lightbulbs down on the floor, slowly. "Where?"

"In the flour bin. With Gran's private accounts." She took a deep breath. "There's a page about the loan. And a list of names. Yours is on it."

"Hannah—"

"She wrote that you'd stay," said Hannah quietly. "That you had your father's hands. That I shouldn't ask you to be a hero, but to let you decide when to be one."

Eli said nothing for a long moment. He looked down at the floor, at the lightbulbs, at the faint trail of flour where Hannah had been kneeling. Then he looked up, and his eyes were bright in a way she had not seen before.

"She wrote that?" he said hoarsely.

"Yes."

"In the flour bin."

"Apparently it's where she kept her secrets," said Hannah. "I think she thought nobody would look in a place that smelled of rye."

He laughed, a short, startled sound, and then he put his coffee cup down on the nearest crate and walked across the storeroom and took the ledger out from under the cornmeal sack with great care. He sat down on an overturned bucket and opened it to the page about the loan. He read it silently, his lips moving slightly as he went through the entries, the way he might read a set of wiring diagrams.

Hannah sat down on the floor beside him, her back against the cornmeal, and waited.

After a while, Eli closed the ledger and set it on his knee. He put one hand over his mouth and sat very still.

"He never told me," he said, behind his fingers. "Not once. Not in twenty years of working side by side. He said it was the only honest thing he ever did with money, and he never wanted it to be a burden on anyone else."

"Eli."

"He left me a letter, too." Eli's voice was thick. "After the funeral. It's locked in the safe at the store, under the nail drawers. I never opened it. I told myself I didn't need to, because I knew what he'd say. Keep the store open. Don't sell the nails. Don't let them make you a villain for being the only one who stayed." He lowered his hand and looked at her, eyes wet. "I thought it was about the store. I didn't realize it was about this street."

"It's about this street," said Hannah gently.

"It's about the people on it." He sniffed and rubbed his face hard with his sleeve. "God, I'm an idiot."

"You're not an idiot."

"I spent three weeks trying to make Garrett Pike look like the enemy, and the whole time, your grandmother and my father were the only two people in this town who had already figured out how to save it."

"And us," said Hannah softly.

He looked at her.

"The two of us," she said. "We're the ones who have to finish it."

Eli was quiet for a long moment. Then he reached out and took her hand, very gently, his fingers warm and rough against her palm. He did not say anything. He did not need to.

They sat together on the floor of the storeroom among the flour sacks and the empty crates, holding hands, while the morning light crept in through the high, dusty window and laid a pale gold line across the ledger on his knee.

After a while, Biscuit padded in, sniffed the air, and sat down in front of them with a deep, long sigh, as if he had been waiting all his life for someone to remember he was there.

"Even the dog knows," said Eli, and his voice was almost steady.

"The dog always knows," said Hannah, and did not let go.

# Chapter Twenty-One: The Letter in the Safe

The safe at the hardware store had been bolted to the floor of the back office since before Hannah was born, and it had a dial that stuck on the number seven.

"You have to lift as you turn," said Eli, kneeling in front of it with his ear pressed to the metal. "Like you're asking it nicely."

"I've never asked a safe nicely in my life."

"Then you're going to learn today." He handed her a flashlight, then leaned back on his heels and gave her a look of mild amusement. "Go on. Show me your Chicago technique."

Hannah knelt beside him on the cold concrete, the flashlight in one hand, and tried to remember how her grandmother had taught her to open the cash drawer when she was twelve. She turned the dial to the left, then the right, lifting slightly on every turn. The mechanism groaned and shifted under her fingers.

"Left," said Eli. "Two full turns. Stop at the four."

"How do you know the combination?"

"Because your grandmother told me, and then she made me promise not to forget it, and then I wrote it on a piece of paper and stuck it inside the lid of my toolbox." He shrugged. "I'm not proud."

Hannah stopped turning and stared at him. "You've had the combination to this safe for thirteen years?"

"Since I was eighteen. My father trusted me with it the summer before I started at the community college." His voice was quiet. "He said if anything ever happened to him, I'd be the one who had to open it. He didn't want to think about it, but he wanted it to be settled."

"And you never opened it?"

"Once," said Eli. "The week after the funeral. I looked at the envelope, and I couldn't read it. I put it back."

Hannah went back to the dial. Left, two full turns, stop at four. She lifted, turned, and felt the mechanism give with a soft click, like a breath let out at last.

The door swung open.

Inside was a metal cash box, a stack of old receipts, a bundle of photographs held together with a faded red ribbon, and a single cream-colored envelope with a name written across the front in a big, careful hand.

*Eli.*

He did not reach for it at first. He knelt there, staring at it, his hands loose on his thighs, while the light from the flashlight trembled slightly across the page.

"Go on," said Hannah softly.

He picked it up. The envelope was old and soft at the corners, the flap sealed with a strip of tape that had gone yellow with time. He turned it over once, twice, as if he were trying to decide whether it would bite. Then he slid one finger beneath the tape and opened it with a slow, careful tear.

There were two pages inside, folded in thirds. He unfolded them on his knee, smoothing the creases with the flat of his hand, and began to read.

Hannah did not look over his shoulder. She sat beside him and watched his face instead, which was the only thing she could bear to watch. She saw him frown, then go still, then close his eyes for a long moment before opening them again. She saw his mouth tighten, and then soften, and then tighten again. When he got to the bottom of the second page, he made a small sound, half laugh and half sob, and pressed the back of his wrist to his mouth.

"Eli," said Hannah.

"He says he's proud of me," said Eli, very quietly. "That's all. He says I'm a stubborn, bossy, impossible son who never listens to a word anyone says, and he's proud of me anyway. He says I'll stay. He says I'll try to fix everything myself, and I'll get it wrong, and I'll have to learn to ask for help." He swallowed hard. "He says the bakery is more important than the building, and I should stop pretending I don't care about the Brooks women because I'm too proud to say it."

Hannah's breath caught.

"He says that?" she said.

"He says that." Eli laughed, wet and rough. "Dad always was a terrible judge of subtlety."

He handed her the second page. It was short, written in the same square, careful hand, dated the spring before he died.

*Hannah,* it began. *You won't remember me teaching you to hold a hammer. You were eight and very serious, and you hit your thumb twice and didn't cry either time. I always thought you'd come back someday, because a girl like that doesn't belong in a city that doesn't know her name. Eli will tell you what happened in 1989. He was never able to tell me he forgave me for doing it, and I want you to know that I never regretted it. Your grandmother was the bravest woman on this street. Keep the lights on for her. And don't let Eli carry everything by himself. He's worse at it than I was.*

Hannah read it twice. Then she folded it carefully and handed it back to him.

"He never told me about the hammer," she said.

"He told me about it every Christmas," said Eli. "Like it was the greatest day of his life. You hit your thumb and didn't cry, and he said you were going to be the toughest woman in Ohio." He looked at her, and his eyes were bright with tears he was not bothering to hide. "He was right, wasn't he?"

"I cried," said Hannah. "Later. In the car."

"Of course you did." He smiled, a broken, real smile. "That's the best thing anyone's ever told me about you."

She laughed through her own tears and reached for his hand. He took it without hesitating, and for a long moment they knelt on the cold floor of the back office with the safe open beside them and the letter between their knees, and neither of them said anything at all.

Outside, a truck rumbled past on Cutter Street. A dog barked somewhere down the block. The radiator in the corner clanked and hissed, and the faint smell of machine oil and old paper drifted through the air.

"There's something else in here," said Hannah eventually, nodding at the cash box.

Eli opened it. Inside was a bundle of yellowed papers, held together with a rubber band, and on the top sheet, in her grandmother's looping hand, was a note: *For Eli, when he's ready. Not before. Dorothy.*

He took the papers out and unfolded the top sheet. It was a deed, dated 1961, for the building at 14 Cutter Street. Beneath it was a list of every owner since, and every mortgage, and every loan.

At the very bottom, in ink so faded it was nearly invisible, was a single sentence in a different hand.

*The building was never meant to be sold. Only saved.*

Eli stared at it for a long time. Then he laughed, a short, startled sound, and pressed the paper flat against his knee.

"She knew," he said. "The whole time. She knew Pike would come, and she knew we'd end up here, and she left it all for us to find."

"She always did like a dramatic reveal," said Hannah.

"She was a menace," said Eli fondly. "Just like the dog."

Biscuit, who had wandered in from the front of the store at the sound of voices, sat down in the doorway with a deep, satisfied sigh, as if he had been expecting this all along.

Eli leaned over and kissed Hannah's forehead, very softly, without thinking, the way a man kisses someone he has known all his life. Then he went still, and pulled back, and looked at her with an expression of such naked surprise that she laughed out loud.

"Sorry," he said.

"Don't be."

"I didn't mean to—"

"I know," said Hannah. "Do it again, and then we'll talk about it."

He did.

# Chapter Twenty-Two: The Easement

The county records office occupied the second floor of a brick building that smelled of radiator dust and carbon paper, and Mr. Dorsey climbed the stairs to it every Thursday with the air of a man visiting an old friend he did not particularly like.

"Two hundred and eleven years of deeds," he said, unlocking the door at the top. "And every one of them is a little bit of a lie."

"Which one is the lie?" asked Hannah.

"Usually the one the seller wrote." He flicked on the lights. Long oak tables stretched under the windows, each one stacked with gray archive boxes labeled in faded ink. "Your grandmother's file is in the back. I pulled everything recorded against 14 Cutter Street after 1961. Including the things that aren't deeds."

Eli had not said much since the morning. He stood at the end of the nearest table with his hands in his coat pockets, looking at the boxes as if they might rearrange themselves if he stared hard enough. He had come, he said, because someone had to carry the heavy ones, and because he had not slept well since the letter.

"Here," said Mr. Dorsey, and lifted a thin folder onto the table. "Recorded March 1990. Instrument type: preservation easement. Grantor, Dorothy Ann Brooks. Grantee, the Cutter Street Preservation Trust." He turned the pages with two fingers. "And co-signed, as trustee, by a Hollis J. Carver."

Hannah sat down slowly.

"What does an easement do?" she asked, though she was afraid she already knew.

"It runs with the land," said Mr. Dorsey. "It's binding on every owner who comes after her, forever, unless the trust agrees to release it. The building can be sold, rented, repaired, painted any color the city allows. But it cannot be demolished. It cannot be converted into anything that changes the front of the building or the back of the lot. And any commercial redevelopment of the block needs the trust's written consent."

"Pike's plan is a redevelopment," said Eli quietly.

"Pike's plan," said Mr. Dorsey, "is a set of drawings that show your grandmother's back wall gone and a parking structure where her oven used to be. If Pike wants to build it, he has to get the trust to sign off. And the trust has two trustees." He tapped the page. "One of them was Dorothy Brooks, who is dead. The other was Hollis Carver. Who is also dead."

There was a long silence in the room.

"Which leaves," said Hannah slowly, "the trust with one trustee."

"Which leaves," said Mr. Dorsey, "Hollis Carver's heir, if he has one, who would be entitled to the seat under the trust deed. Let me see." He turned to the back of the folder and ran his finger down a page of tight, legal type. "Section six. *Upon the death or incapacity of either trustee, the surviving trustee shall appoint a successor, being a son or daughter of the deceased, if such son or daughter is resident in the county.*" He looked up over his glasses. "Mr. Carver, I believe you're the son of the deceased. And I believe you've been resident in the county your entire life."

Eli did not move.

"Dad never told me," he said, very slowly.

"Your father," said Mr. Dorsey gently, "was a man who did not talk about the things that mattered most. I say that with respect. I knew him for thirty years."

Eli walked to the window. He stood with his back to them, his shoulders very straight, looking down at the street where the frost was melting on the parked cars and a man in a red hat was sweeping snow from the steps of the church.

"He knew," he said to the glass. "He knew all along. The letter. The safe. Gran's ledger. He put me on the trust, and he never said a word, because he wanted me to find it myself and decide what it meant."

"Eli," said Hannah.

"I didn't want this." He turned around, and his face was pale and drawn. "I don't want to be the one who decides whether a building gets torn down. I'm an electrician. I fix wiring. I don't want a seat at a table where people's lives get traded like bags of flour."

"Gran wrote that you'd decide when to be a hero," said Hannah.

"That's not the same thing."

"It's exactly the same thing," said Hannah, rising from the chair. "She wrote that you'd stay. She wrote that you had your father's hands. And she left the ledger for you, and the deed, and the trust, and she didn't leave them for Pike, Eli. She left them for you."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he took one hand out of his pocket and pressed the heel of it against his eyes.

"Okay," he said, muffled. "Okay. I'll sign it."

"You don't have to sign it today," said Mr. Dorsey mildly. "But I'd like it signed before Pike's people file the amended plans. They've been very busy this week."

"How do you know that?" asked Hannah.

Mr. Dorsey took off his glasses and polished them slowly on his tie. "Because the city clerk called me on Monday, to ask whether the Cutter Street Preservation Trust had been dissolved," he said. "I told her that was impossible, since nobody had ever bothered to dissolve it. She seemed surprised."

"Someone checked," said Eli.

"Someone checked," agreed Mr. Dorsey, "and found the same thing I did, and then looked very carefully at the calendar. Pike's filings are due on Friday. He wants the easement removed before the trust has a living trustee willing to fight it." He put his glasses back on. "Which means he knows about it. Which means he has known for some time."

Hannah thought of the offer letter on the counter, the one with the neat, confident paragraph about *expedited closing*, and the phrase *the seller acknowledges no encumbrances of record*. She thought of Garrett Pike's smile at the town hall, and the way he had looked at Eli across the room.

"He has known the whole time," she said. "And he didn't tell me."

"Men like Pike never tell you the one thing you need," said Mr. Dorsey. "It's bad for business."

Eli picked up the folder and held it against his chest, the way he might hold a box of tools he had not expected to be so heavy.

"Then we'd better get there first," he said.

They spent the rest of the afternoon in the records office, copying page after page by hand onto yellow legal paper. Mr. Dorsey found the original trust deed, signed in blue ink by a younger, sharper Dorothy Brooks, who had written in the margin, in small, angry capitals: *NO BULLDOZERS ON CUTTER STREET. I MEAN IT.* He found a letter from the state historical society, dated 1991, thanking Dorothy for her *persistence in the face of considerable pressure*, and a faded clipping from the county paper that showed the bakery's front window in 1974, with a row of schoolchildren pressed against the glass and a much younger Hollis Carver standing at the back in a cardigan, grinning like a man who had just been given a secret.

Hannah pressed her finger to the photograph.

"He looks happy," she said.

"He was," said Eli, his voice rough. "He told me once that the happiest day of his life was the day he decided not to sell the store."

When they finally carried the boxes back down the stairs and out into the cold, the sky over the river had turned a deep, bruised violet, and the lights were coming on along Cutter Street one by one. Eli stopped on the sidewalk and looked up at the bakery's sign, the red paint faded almost to pink above the door.

"The Cutter Street Bakery," he read aloud. "Est. 1961."

"Gran painted that herself," said Hannah. "She said the first version was so crooked that the customers thought it was a joke, so she kept it that way on purpose."

"It's not a joke anymore," said Eli. "Not if you're the one who keeps it open."

He took her hand, as easily as breathing, and they walked the last block together in silence, with the folder tucked under his arm and the snow beginning to fall again in thin, glittering lines through the lamplight.

Biscuit met them at the door, sneezing with joy, and Hannah bent down to rub his ears while Eli locked the folder in the safe at the store and came back with two mugs of coffee so bad it was almost a comfort.

"Pike won't like this," said Hannah.

"No," said Eli. "He's going to hate it."

"Good," said Hannah, and for the first time since she had come home, she laughed without any fear at all in it.

# Chapter Twenty-Three: Cracks Along the River

The inspector from Columbus arrived on the second Monday of December in a gray state car, with a clipboard, a hard hat, and a pair of rubber boots so new they squeaked on the frozen ground.

His name was Dale Henner. He was sixty-three years old, thin as a rake, with a neat gray mustache and the quiet, unhurried manner of a man who had spent forty years listening to walls. He had inspected the mill race wall in 1994, when he was thirty-one and had a full head of black hair, and he had been trying to find an excuse to come back ever since.

"I never forget a foundation," he said, shaking Eli's hand at the edge of the boatyard lot. "Your father bought me coffee every time I came through town. Terrible coffee. He'd apologize for it, and I'd say it was the best cup I'd had all year, and he'd laugh at me." He looked at the broken brick and the jagged crack, and his mouth tightened. "Show me the drill marks."

Hannah had never seen Eli so quiet. He led the inspector along the edge of the trench without speaking, pointing out the splintered stone and the raw, bright scars where something sharp had bitten into the old mortar. Dale Henner crouched down, ran his gloved fingers along the crack, and then put his ear almost to the stone, like a doctor listening for a heartbeat.

"Hm," he said.

"What does it mean?" asked Hannah.

"It means somebody was in a hurry." He stood up slowly, his knees cracking. "A boring rig doesn't just go through a foundation like this. It shakes it. You can hear the stone shifting if you know what you're listening for. Somebody drilled into this wall in October and didn't stop when the wall told them to." He looked at Eli. "How long has the bank been moving?"

"We don't know yet," said Eli.

"Then we'll find out." Dale Henner took out a small black notebook and a pencil, and began to write, very slowly. "I'm going to measure it. I'm going to take cores from three points along the bank and send them to the state lab. I'm going to write a report that says exactly what I see, and I'm going to sign my name to it. And then I'm going to drive back to Columbus and sit in my office for a week, waiting for the phone to ring, because I know who's going to call me."

"Pike," said Hannah.

"Pike's lawyers," said Dale Henner, "and then Pike himself, and then the mayor, and then the state representative, who is a friend of Pike's cousin and has never once set foot on a river bank." He smiled grimly. "It's the way it always goes. But this time, they'll have to explain the drill marks."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

They spent the rest of the morning on the bank. The inspector drilled three test holes with a hand auger, marking each sample with a careful number, and the cold wind blew the mud up in sheets across the boatyard lot until everyone's faces were gray.

Biscuit had been banned from the site by Dale Henner, who had taken one look at the dog and said that it was not a sensible place for an animal of such enthusiasm. The dog had sat on the bakery step for an hour in a state of noble, injured dignity. Hannah had brought him a sausage roll to make up for it, and he had accepted the bribe without any sign of forgiveness.

At noon, Walt came down from the library with a flask of tea and a paper bag of cookies, and stood at the edge of the trench with his cap in his hands, watching the inspector work.

"Is it bad?" he asked quietly.

"It's serious," said Eli. "The wall's been cracked for a few months. Maybe longer."

Walt nodded slowly. He was silent for a long moment, looking at the gray river rushing past the boatyard, the water high and brown with the winter rain.

"My wife's buried up on the hill," he said at last. "By the chapel. Her grave's on the bank side. She picked it herself, in 1981. She said she wanted to hear the river when she was dead." His voice was steady, but his hands shook as he turned the cap around. "If that wall goes, they'll move the graves. They always do. But I'd rather not have her moved. I'd rather she stayed where she could hear the water."

Hannah felt the words go through her like cold water. She had not thought about the cemetery. She had not thought about the chapel, or the houses on the high side of the bank, or the children who played along the riverside path in summer. She had been so busy counting windows and bread and the price of the building that she had forgotten the river was there at all.

"It won't go," she said, and her voice sounded stronger than she felt. "Not if we can help it."

Walt looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, very slowly, and gave her one of his cookies.

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

By the end of the afternoon, the inspector had three cores in a plastic case and a page of notes so crowded with figures that Hannah could not read the handwriting. He packed his clipboard into the back of the state car, shook Eli's hand again, and turned to Hannah with an expression of grave, almost paternal kindness.

"Your grandmother came to see me in the autumn of 2019," he said. "Did you know that?"

Hannah shook her head.

"She sat in my office for two hours. She had a box of papers on her knees and a face like thunder. She said Pike had been trying to buy the block for years, and that his people had been poking around the bank, and she wanted to know whether the wall was safe." He smiled faintly. "I told her it was fine, but the mortar was going. I told her the bank would need work within five years. She said she'd find the money. And I think she did, because I never got a call about it."

"She paid for it," said Eli quietly.

"She paid for it," said Dale Henner. "Out of the bakery's account, and a private fund she never told a soul about. She said the wall had been holding Cutter Street up for a century, and she was not going to let it fall down on her watch." He opened the door of the car. "And then she wrote to me in March, very politely, to say that she'd heard I was going to be asked to inspect it again, and that she hoped I'd remember what I'd told her. I did."

He climbed into the driver's seat and wound down the window.

"I'll see you in January," he said. "Make sure that dog doesn't eat my samples."

The car pulled away, slowly, across the frozen ground, and Hannah stood on the edge of the boatyard lot with the cold biting through her coat and the gray river roaring behind her, and understood, at last, what her grandmother had been doing for the last five years of her life.

She had been holding the street up, one bolt and one drawer and one quiet phone call at a time.

"She never told me," Hannah said.

"She never told anyone," said Eli. He came up beside her and took her hand, and his fingers were cold and rough and entirely steady. "That's how you know it was real."

Behind them, Biscuit barked once, sharply, from the far side of the fence, where he had been sitting in a patch of sunshine and watching the river with his ears pricked up and his tail very still, as though he, too, had heard the wall groan under the weight of the water.

# Chapter Twenty-Four: Chicago, Again

The offer arrived on a Tuesday, in a cream-color envelope with a gold foil crest, and Hannah read it standing up in the kitchen with a tray of cinnamon rolls cooling on the counter behind her.

*Dear Hannah,* it began. *We have followed your career with great admiration. Pike Ridge Development has retained our firm for its Cutter Street launch event, and we would be delighted to offer you the role of Senior Director, Client Experience, effective January. Salary commensurate with your extraordinary track record.*

The letter was signed by Marian Lowell, who had hired Hannah straight out of a community college in Dayton and taught her, over eight years, how to walk into any room in the world and make it believe in something.

Below the signature was a handwritten note in blue ink. *You were always the best of us, kid. Come home to the right kind of work.*

Hannah put the letter down very carefully on the flour-dusted counter, as if it might go off.

"Oh, Marian," she said to the empty room.

Biscuit, who had been sprawled under the table with his chin on his paws, lifted his head and looked at her with the solemn attention of a dog who suspected he was about to be left behind.

She called Jess that evening, from the back step, with the cold coming up through her boots and the lights of the diner glowing softly across the street.

"They want you to run the Pike launch," said her sister flatly, after Hannah had read the letter aloud twice.

"They want me to run a party for a man who is trying to tear down Gran's building."

"And they're offering you what, exactly?"

Hannah named the number. There was a long silence.

"Okay," said Jess finally. "That's a lot of money, Han."

"It's a lot of money."

"Are you going to take it?"

"I don't know," said Hannah, and realized as she said it that it was the truth. "Part of me wants to. It would be so easy. I'd know exactly what I was good at. I'd know exactly who to be in the room. I wouldn't have to wonder if I was going to fail at something that mattered."

"You're not going to fail at the bakery."

"I'm failing at it right now. The oven's older than both of us. The wiring's a disaster. The back taxes are four months overdue, and I've got a pile of invoices on the desk that would make a grown man weep, and I can't tell if the bread's selling because people like it or because they feel sorry for me."

"They like it," said Jess firmly. "I've seen the photos. Mrs. Ortiz posted a picture of your cinnamon rolls with the caption *Please do not ask Hannah for her recipe, she will cry.*"

Hannah laughed in spite of herself.

"Go to Chicago," said Jess. "Just go. Have the meeting. You can say no in person. It's the least you owe Marian, and it's the most you owe yourself."

"What if I say yes?"

"Then you say yes," said Jess, "and you come back and tell me why, and I'll decide whether to forgive you."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

The train to Chicago left Dayton at six in the morning, and Hannah spent most of the journey staring out of the window at the flat, frozen fields that slid past under a pale wash of sky. She had not planned to tell Eli where she was going. She had told herself it was because she did not want him to worry, and she had known, even as she thought it, that it was not the whole truth.

She had texted him from the platform. *Going to Chicago for a meeting. Back Friday.*

His reply had come almost at once. *Okay.*

Then, a minute later: *Be careful on the train. The heating's terrible on the second car, I checked it once. Don't let them give you a cold seat.*

She had laughed, alone on the platform, and then felt a sharp, unexpected pang of something that was not quite homesickness, because she was going to a city she had once thought was home, and it was the first time she had ever wanted to be somewhere else.

Chicago was loud and bright and impatient. The meeting took place on the forty-third floor of a glass tower on Michigan Avenue, in a conference room with a view of the frozen lake and a wall of framed magazine covers bearing the firm's name. Marian had not changed at all. She was still small and sharp and immaculately dressed, with silver earrings the size of coins and a laugh like a crack of ice on a lake.

"Hannah Brooks," she said, and hugged her, hard. "You look tired. Good. It suits you."

They talked for two hours. Marian walked her through the Pike Ridge brief, a glossy document of renderings and projections: a plaza by the river, a farmers' market with a glass roof, a *heritage corridor* of shops with fake brick facades and names like *Dorothy's Table* and *The Cutter Street Parlour*. It was beautiful. It was exactly the kind of thing Hannah had spent eight years making look irresistible, and as she turned the pages, she felt a strange, cold heaviness settle behind her ribs.

"They've kept your grandmother's name," said Marian warmly. "I thought you'd appreciate that."

"They've kept her name on a shop that doesn't exist," said Hannah.

Marian's smile faltered, just slightly. "It's a marketing story, sweetheart. Every development is a story. You know that better than anyone."

"I used to know it," said Hannah. "I'm not sure I believe it anymore."

She closed the brief and set it on the table between them. Outside, the lake was gray and endless, and a single freighter crawled across the horizon like a slow, patient animal.

"Do you know what Pike's doing on Cutter Street?" she asked.

"I know he's buying the block," said Marian carefully. "I know he's got a second town meeting next month and a lot of very eager local partners. I know the old bakery is the last holdout."

"I'm the last holdout," said Hannah. "The bakery's just the building."

"Honey—"

"Did you know he's known about the trust since the summer?" said Hannah. "Did you know he's been trying to get my grandmother's easement removed through a shell company in Columbus? Did you know the city clerk got a phone call on Monday asking whether the Cutter Street Preservation Trust still existed?"

Marian was quiet for a long moment. Then she sat back in her chair and folded her hands.

"No," she said. "I did not know that."

"Then maybe you should ask him," said Hannah, "before you put my name on his party."

The meeting ended with an offer, a handshake, and a promise to think it over until the end of the week. Marian walked her to the elevators, her heels clicking softly on the marble floor.

"You were always the best of us," she said again, as the doors opened. "I meant that."

"I know," said Hannah. "That's the problem."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

She sat on a bench by the river for an hour afterwards, watching the ice break up under the bridge and drift away in gray, glittering plates. She thought about the first time she had walked into the firm's offices, twenty-two years old and terrified, with a suitcase full of borrowed dresses and a head full of ambition. She thought about the galas she had planned, the champagne towers and the dazzling lights, the speeches she had written for people who did not believe a single word of them. She thought about her grandmother standing at the oven at five in the morning, humming badly, with flour on her cheek and a cup of tea going cold on the sill.

She thought about Eli saying *I'd be the one who had to open it.*

Her phone buzzed in her coat pocket. A photo, sent without a message. The inside of the bakery at dusk, the lights low, a tray of rolls on the counter and Biscuit asleep across the doorway, one paw twitching in a dream. Eli's thumb was visible in the corner of the frame, pressed over the lens by accident.

Under it, a second message, arriving a moment later.

*Dog misses you. So do the rolls. So does the oven, but it won't say so.*

Hannah pressed the phone against her chest and closed her eyes. The river went on rushing under the bridge, and for the first time in eight years, she did not know what she wanted to be when she got home.

She only knew that she wanted to go home.

# Chapter Twenty-Five: Ice on the Millpond

She came home on the Friday evening train, in a snowstorm that made the windows rattle, and found Eli waiting at the platform with his hands in his pockets and a red wool scarf wound twice around his neck.

He did not say anything when she stepped down onto the platform. He just looked at her, the way he had looked at her across the cocoa urn, and then he took the heavy bag from her shoulder and slung it over his own.

"You didn't have to meet me," she said.

"I know."

"It's freezing."

"I know that too." He tilted his head, studying her face in the yellow light of the station lamp. "Did you say yes?"

"No," said Hannah. "I said I'd think about it."

"Did you think?"

"I thought about the river," she said. "And the bread. And a dog who sulks when I leave the room." She swallowed. "And you."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he said, very softly, "That's a dangerous list."

"I know."

"Come on," said Eli, and turned towards the parking lot. "I've got a surprise. You'll hate it."

"What is it?"

"The millpond's frozen solid," said Eli, over his shoulder. "And the Ortiz boys have built a bonfire at the edge of it, and Mrs. Ortiz has brought a flask of something she won't name, and I've been told, very firmly, that I'm not allowed to come back to Cutter Street until I've taught you how to skate."

Hannah stopped in the middle of the platform, snow settling on her hair.

"I can't skate."

"Nobody can skate," said Eli. "That's the point."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

The millpond sat at the edge of town, behind the old grain mill and the line of frosted willows, and by seven o'clock the whole village seemed to have made its way down the snowy footpath with thermoses and torches and a battered portable speaker playing old Motown songs. A dozen children were already flying across the ice in mismatched boots, shrieking with delight. A few older couples stood near the bonfire with their hands wrapped around paper cups, laughing at something the vicar had just said.

Eli had brought two pairs of skates from the back of the hardware store, old, scuffed things with leather laces and blades that had been sharpened so often they had gone thin as knives.

"Mine," he said, handing her a pair. "They're a size too big. Don't fall on the blades."

"That's very reassuring."

"It's the only advice I've got."

She sat on a log by the fire and laced them up, badly, while Eli knelt beside her and redid the knots with quick, practiced fingers. When he finished, he looked up at her, and she saw that his face was flushed from the cold and that he was trying very hard not to smile.

"What?" she said.

"You look like a deer that's been told it's going to be on the news."

"That's the cruelest thing you've ever said to me."

"It's the truest." He stood up and held out his hand. "Trust me."

"I don't trust you."

"You trust me with the oven."

"The oven doesn't have a face like yours," said Hannah, and took his hand anyway.

He pulled her onto the ice. She wobbled, flailed, and grabbed at his coat, and he caught her with a low, startled laugh that sent a puff of warm breath into the frozen air.

"Okay," he said. "Ankles. Bend your knees. Pretend you're trying to pick up a dropped spoon."

"That's the worst lesson I've ever had."

"It's the only one that works."

They went around the edge of the pond, slowly, her hands locked in his and her ankles wobbling like a newborn foal. The children whizzed past. The bonfire crackled and popped. The music drifted across the water, a soft, sweet crooning that made the whole scene feel like the inside of an old photograph.

On the third lap, she stopped trying to stay upright and simply let herself lean on him.

"You're doing better," said Eli.

"I'm doing terribly."

"You're still on your feet. That's better than my first winter."

"What happened in your first winter?"

"I broke my wrist and my mother laughed so hard she had to sit down on the ice." He said it lightly, the way people speak about things that still hurt a little. "Then she left in the spring, when I was nine, and I didn't see her again until I was twenty-two. She sent a postcard from Florida once. It said *sorry about the wrist*."

Hannah was quiet for a moment, her hands tight on his.

"I didn't know that," she said.

"Nobody does, really. Dad knew. Gran knew, I think. She used to bring me soup and tell me that some people only know how to leave, and that it isn't a judgment on the people they leave behind." He glanced at her, and his face was unguarded in the firelight. "I've spent a lot of my life trying to prove that I wouldn't do the same. Staying. Fixing the things that were broken. Not letting anybody down."

"You haven't let anybody down," said Hannah.

"I let you down. Last month."

"You let me down by being right about the oven," said Hannah. "And I let you down by being scared. We're even."

He laughed, quietly. "Is that how it works?"

"That's exactly how it works."

They had stopped moving. The ice creaked faintly under their feet. Across the pond, the bonfire had burned down to a glowing red heart, and the children were gathering around Mrs. Ortiz as she passed out cups of something hot and fragrant that smelled of cinnamon and whisky. The sky above the willows was crowded with stars.

"Hannah," said Eli.

"Yes?"

"I want to kiss you," he said. "I've wanted to kiss you since the day you dropped that spoon in the kitchen, and I'd like to be very clear that I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But if you want to keep skating, I'll keep skating. I'll make you a fool on the ice for the rest of the night. I'd like that, too."

She looked up at him. There was snow in his hair and a smudge of soot on his cheek from the bonfire, and his eyes were dark and steady and very, very afraid.

"Kiss me," said Hannah, "or I'll fall over on purpose and make you carry me home."

He kissed her.

It was cold and clumsy and it lasted much longer than either of them had planned, because her skates slid out from under her in the middle of it and he caught her, and then they were both laughing against each other's mouths and holding on to each other as if the ice might open up and swallow them. When they finally drew apart, his breath came out in a rough, uneven cloud, and his hands were still pressed to her back, warm through her coat.

"Well," he said, dazed. "That was not the plan."

"What was the plan?"

"Teach you to skate. Make you laugh. Don't fall on the blades."

"I fell on the blades."

"You did." He pressed his forehead to hers. "I'm very glad you did."

Across the ice, somebody let out a long, delighted wolf whistle. Mrs. Ortiz's voice carried clearly over the water. "About time, you two! Now get off my millpond before you freeze to it!"

\* \* \*
:::

They walked back to Cutter Street together afterwards, with their skates slung over their shoulders and their gloved hands tangled together, while the snow came down in soft, slow flakes that made the streetlamps look like fairy lights. Biscuit was waiting on the bakery step, wrapped in the red ribbon Tom Kowalski had sent, and when he saw them coming he rose to his feet and wagged so hard his whole back end swung side to side.

"He's been very patient," said Eli.

"He's been sulking," said Hannah. "There's a difference."

"He's been waiting for you to come home. He's not the only one."

She stopped on the sidewalk and looked at him, and he looked back, and for once neither of them found anything to say that needed saying.

She kissed him again, on the step, under the faded red sign of *The Cutter Street Bakery*, and the dog barked once, happily, as if he had been waiting his whole life for someone to finally get on with it.

# Chapter Twenty-Six: The Bread Club

The idea arrived, as all of Mrs. Ortiz's ideas did, fully formed and already in motion.

"Saturdays," she announced, marching into the bakery at seven in the morning with a clipboard under one arm and a pair of enormous reading glasses perched on her nose. "Every Saturday, one hundred loaves, reserved in advance, collected at the back door before the Sunday crowd gets up. Twenty dollars a month. Two loaves a week, a dozen rolls, and one surprise every month that nobody is allowed to guess."

Hannah, who was elbow-deep in rye dough and had not yet had her coffee, stared at her.

"Who's going to bake a hundred loaves?"

"You are," said Mrs. Ortiz. "And Eli, when he's not fixing the freezer. And Tom Kowalski, who has been bragging about his sourdough since Thanksgiving and needs a sense of purpose in his life. And me, because I have decided to be your bookkeeper."

"You can't bake."

"I can count," said Mrs. Ortiz, with dignity. "And I can frighten people into paying on time. That's more important than baking."

Eli, who had come in through the back door with a box of wire and an expression of mild dread, paused in the doorway and looked from one woman to the other.

"Is this about the freezer?" he asked.

"It's about the future," said Mrs. Ortiz. "Sit down. You're going to be on the committee."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

By ten o'clock, the bakery's kitchen table was covered with sign-up sheets, and the phone had not stopped ringing.

The vicar called, asking whether the church could be a collection point for the Saturday loaves. Jess called from Dayton, demanding to know whether the surprise monthly item could be a cinnamon roll shaped like a dog. The woman who ran the library called to ask whether she could have twelve loaves for the reading group, and whether the bread would still be warm if she came at eleven. The woman who ran the paper shop called to say that she did not want bread, but she had a shelf in her window that was empty, and would Hannah like to fill it with a basket of rolls, with a sign that said *Dorothy's Recipe*, and would she please not tell anyone she had been crying about it.

Hannah put the phone down, very carefully, and wiped her eyes on the back of her wrist.

"What's wrong?" asked Eli, from the freezer.

"Nothing," said Hannah. "Everyone's just being nice."

"Everyone's been nice for weeks." He climbed out of the freezer, pulling his gloves off with his teeth. "It's a terrible thing, isn't it? People being kind to you. You don't know what to do with it."

"I've never been very good at being looked after."

"I noticed." He crossed the kitchen in three long strides and put his arms around her from behind, resting his chin on her shoulder. He smelled of cold metal and wet concrete and the faintly sweet scent of the coffee she had burned that morning. "You're getting better, though."

"Am I?"

"You let the vicar call you 'my dear' last week without hitting him over the head with a rolling pin."

"That was a very close thing."

"I was watching. I had money on the rolling pin."

She laughed, and turned in his arms, and the morning sun came through the window and lay across the flour-dusted tiles like a warm, gold blanket.

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

The first Saturday, they sold out in forty minutes.

The second, they sold out in twenty-five, and somebody from the regional paper came to take photographs, and the photographs ended up on the front page of the Sunday supplement under the headline *Cutter Street Rises*. Mrs. Ortiz cut it out with a pair of scissors and pinned it to the corkboard by the till, underlined in red marker, with a note in her big, curling hand: *SEE? I TOLD YOU SO.*

By the third Saturday, the Bread Club had two hundred and twelve members, a waiting list of forty-one, a monthly newsletter written by the vicar's wife, and a cinnamon roll shaped like a dog that Biscuit had tried to steal twice.

Hannah had not expected any of it. She had expected a few neighbors, a few sympathetic customers, a handful of people who came in out of pity and left out of habit. She had not expected the bakery's back room to fill with the warm, noisy bustle of people carrying bags and boxes and asking after her grandmother, or the way the old men on the bench outside would tip their caps when she walked past, or the children who pressed their noses to the glass and begged for a glimpse of the enormous, golden-brown loaves on the racks.

She had not expected to feel, every single morning, that she was exactly where she was supposed to be.

"It's working," said Eli, on the back step, one Saturday afternoon, with his jacket open and his face turned up to the thin, pale sunshine. "The bread's paying for the wiring. The wiring's keeping the oven running. The oven's making the bread. It's a beautiful, stupid circle."

"It's paying for about a third of the back taxes," said Hannah, who had the figures open on her lap. "The rest is still four months overdue."

"That's a third more than we had last month."

"I know."

"You don't sound very happy about it."

"I'm happy," she said. "I'm just counting."

He sat down beside her, and bumped her shoulder with his, and they watched a flock of starlings wheel above the roofs of the hardware store and the diner, black against the blue.

"Pike's amended filing came through on Wednesday," said Eli. "Did you see?"

"I saw."

"It's the same drawings. Same parking structure. He's just moved the trust's name to the bottom of the page, in very small type, hoping nobody will notice."

"Mr. Dorsey noticed."

"Mr. Dorsey notices everything. It's why I'm starting to like him." Eli rubbed the back of his neck. "There's a hearing on the sixteenth. The council wants to hear from the trust. Which means me."

"You don't have to—"

"I know. You keep saying that." He looked at her, and his face was steady. "I want to do it. I just wish I wasn't so scared I'm going to be sick in front of the mayor."

"You won't be sick."

"You don't know that."

"I've seen you fix a boiler with a broken wrist," said Hannah. "I've seen you tell a developer's assistant to put her phone in her bag and go home. You're not going to be sick." She took his hand. "But if you are, I'll hold your hair back and bring you a cinnamon roll with a dog on it."

He laughed, and the nervous tension went out of his shoulders, and he pulled her into his side and kissed the top of her head, the way he had on the first night, as if it were the most natural thing in the world.

Behind them, the back door opened, and Mrs. Ortiz's voice rang out across the yard.

"Hannah! There's a woman here from the Pike office. She says she's got an invitation for you. She also says she'd like a cup of coffee, and I told her the coffee here is terrible, and she said she'd take her chances."

Hannah felt Eli go very still beside her.

"Tell her I'm busy," said Hannah.

"I did," said Mrs. Ortiz. "She said she'll wait."

Eli sighed, heavily, and stood up, and held out his hand to help her to her feet.

"Come on," he said quietly. "Let's go see what Pike wants to offer you this time."

# Chapter Twenty-Seven: Garrett Pike, Off the Record

Garrett Pike asked to meet her for lunch, and Hannah, who had spent a month refusing every invitation that came from his office, was surprised to find that she wanted to say yes.

"It's not a negotiation," the note said. It was written on heavy cream paper, in a hand so neat it looked typed. *I would like to speak to you without lawyers, without assistants, and without a camera crew from the regional paper. You can name the place. I'll be the one who arrives early.*

Hannah read it twice and showed it to Eli, who read it once and said, "Don't go alone."

"I'm not going alone. I'm going to the diner. Mrs. Ortiz will be at the next table pretending she's reading the paper."

"That's not comforting."

"It's the most comforting thing in this town," said Hannah, and folded the note into her pocket.

\* \* \*

The diner was half empty at one o'clock on a Thursday, and Garrett Pike was sitting in the back booth by the window with a cup of black coffee in front of him and a plate of untouched toast. He wore a camel overcoat, a navy scarf, and the same faint, polite expression she had seen at the council hearing, but without the lawyers, and without the smile. He stood as she approached, and waited until she was seated before he sat down.

"Thank you for coming," he said.

"You said you'd be early."

"I was. I've been here since twelve." He looked at the cup in front of him as though he had forgotten it was there. "Mrs. Ortiz has been very kind about the coffee. She said it was terrible and then brought me a second one."

"That's her way of telling you she's watching."

"I know," said Pike, and almost smiled. "She's been watching me since I arrived in this town. She once told my assistant that I walked like a man who had never been told no in his life, and that she intended to change that before I died."

Hannah did not respond. She waited.

Pike turned his cup a quarter turn on its saucer.

"My father," he said, "was a very successful man. He built three shopping malls in Ohio and two hospitals in Kentucky. He was also, I'm told, a rather unpleasant one. He bought a great many things because he could, and he was never once in his life asked to put them back."

"Is that why you're doing this?"

"I'm doing this," said Pike carefully, "because the Cutter Street block is worth more dead than alive. I don't say that with any pleasure. I say it because it's true, and I have been paid to know what things are worth since I was twenty-three."

"And my grandmother?"

Pike was quiet for a long moment.

"Your grandmother," he said, "was the only person in twenty years who ever told me something I didn't want to hear, and then made me feel like a fool for not having heard it sooner. She wrote me a letter in March. It was three paragraphs long. She said I could buy every building on Cutter Street and still not own the thing that made it worth buying. She said I had mistaken a street for a set of prices, and a town for a line on a spreadsheet, and that I would be happier, and richer in every way that counted, if I learned to ask people what they wanted rather than telling them what they were worth."

"She wasn't being sentimental," said Hannah.

"No," said Pike. "She was being accurate. It was infuriating."

The waitress came over with a pot of fresh coffee, and Pike poured a cup for Hannah without asking, the way someone who had once been a waiter might do. She did not drink it.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked.

"Because I'm tired," said Pike simply. "And because I think you're the only person in this county who won't use it against me."

"You had Bridget Sloane digging into the trust records."

"I did. That was a mistake. I thought the easement was a technicality that a good lawyer could get around." He looked out of the window, at the frosted street and the bakery's faded red letters glinting in the thin winter light. "Then I met your Mr. Dorsey, who has the most irritating courtroom manner I've ever encountered, and I realized that the easement was not a technicality at all. It was a promise. "And the drill marks on the river wall?"

"That was a survey crew," he said quietly. "Not a demolition crew. They were told to take samples. I didn't know they'd gone into the foundation. I have since spoken to the man in charge, who will not be working for me again." He paused. "I'm not proud of it. But I'm not going to pretend I didn't know the wall was old."

"The inspector's coming back in January."

"I know," said Pike. "I've already spoken to the state representative. He thinks I'm very unreasonable. I've asked him to read the report himself."

Hannah stared at him.

"You're withdrawing," she said slowly.

"I'm considering withdrawing," said Pike, "which is a very different thing, and which my lawyers will be furious to hear me say out loud. But I've spent three years trying to make this block into something it was never going to be. I think it's time I let it be what it is."

\* \* \*

They sat in silence for a while. Outside, the frost on the window was beginning to melt, the glass streaming with thin silver lines, and the lights of the hardware store glowed warmly across the road.

"Do you have children?" asked Hannah.

Pike looked at her, startled.

"No," he said. "I was married once. She left me for a man who fixed boats. She said I didn't know how to listen to anybody who didn't want to be bought." He gave a small, dry laugh. "She was right. I've been trying to prove her wrong for twelve years, and I've been failing rather spectacularly."

"Then maybe you should stop trying to prove anything," said Hannah. "And just ask people what they want."

Pike was quiet. Then he nodded, very slowly, the way a man might nod at a verdict he had not expected to hear.

"What do you want, Ms. Brooks?"

Hannah thought about it. She thought about the photograph in the archive, and Manny Ortiz singing in the back room, and the crocuses on the chapel hill, and Walt's wife buried above the river so she could hear the water. She thought about the dog in the mud and Eli's hands on the cold stone of the wall.

"I want the bakery to stay open," she said. "I want the wall to be fixed, and the river to stay where it is, and the graves on the hill to stay where people put them. I want the market on Saturdays, and the Bread Club, and a street full of people who are still arguing about the price of the rolls in twenty years. I want my grandmother's name on the window, and my own on the lease, and the dog to stop sneezing at the inspector." She took a breath. "And I want you to stop trying to buy us."

Pike was silent for a long time.

Then he reached into the inside pocket of his camel coat and took out a folded sheet of paper, thin and yellowed and worn soft at the creases. He placed it on the table between them, carefully, the way he might have placed a card on a poker table.

It was in her grandmother's handwriting. Hannah recognized it at once.

*Garrett,* it read. *You are not a bad man. You are a lonely one, which is worse, because it is so much easier to fix. Go home. Put down the spreadsheet. Ask somebody what they want for supper. And then, for heaven's sake, eat it. — D.B.*

Hannah looked up.

"I read it every Sunday," said Garrett Pike quietly. "I've never once been brave enough to take her advice."

He stood up, buttoned his camel coat, and dropped a banknote on the table, far more than the coffee had cost.

"Tell Mr. Carver I'll be in touch," he said. "Tell him I'd like to see the wall for myself, when the inspector's finished. I'd like to stand on the bank and listen to the water, the way she did." He hesitated at the door, and for the first time since she had met him, he looked very much like an ordinary, tired man. "I don't think I've ever stood still long enough to hear it."

The bell over the door rang softly, and he was gone.

Mrs. Ortiz came out from behind the counter, moving very quickly for a woman of her age, and sat down in the booth opposite Hannah with her eyes narrowed and her arms folded across her chest.

"Well," she said, "what did he want?"

Hannah looked down at the letter on the table. Then she picked it up, carefully, and slid it into her pocket.

"He wanted to ask me something," she said. "And I think I answered it."

"What did you say?"

"I said I wanted to stay," said Hannah, and felt the warmth of it spread slowly through her chest, like the first breath of an oven that had finally caught.

# Chapter Twenty-Eight: A Sunday Dinner

The invitation came in Mrs. Ortiz's handwriting, on a card decorated with a small, slightly lopsided loaf of bread, and it read: *Sunday, one o'clock. Hardware store back room. Bring nothing. Eli is cooking. God help us all.*

"He's cooking?" said Hannah, when she showed it to him across the counter on Friday morning.

"I'm cooking," said Eli, with the air of a man who had been volunteered for a firing squad. "It's a roast. I've watched my father make it nine hundred times. How hard can it be?"

"Eli, you burned the toast on Tuesday."

"The toast was a learning experience."

"The toast was a crime against bread."

"The toast," said Eli, with great dignity, "is in a museum now, where it belongs."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

The back room of the hardware store had been transformed. Someone, probably Mrs. Ortiz, had strung a line of fairy lights across the ceiling beams, and the old oak table had been dragged out from under a tower of paint tins and covered with a checked cloth. There were mismatched chairs from the diner, a bench from the library, and a stool that Walt had carried over in his arms with the air of a man bringing the Crown Jewels.

At one o'clock precisely, Mrs. Ortiz arrived in a red coat, carrying a bottle of wine in each hand and a tray of enchiladas balanced on top of her handbag. She took one look at the kitchen, where Eli was standing over a roasting tin with a dish towels over one shoulder and a face of grim determination, and put both bottles down on the table with a heavy thump.

"Well," she said. "It's on fire."

"It's not on fire," said Eli.

"There is smoke coming out of the oven, mijo."

"That's the fat," said Eli. "It's supposed to smoke."

"Your father," said Mrs. Ortiz, "never let the fat smoke. He said it was a sin."

Eli opened the oven door, released a cloud of blue smoke that made everybody cough, and peered inside with the expression of a man reading a particularly hostile letter.

"Right," he said, "that's a bit of a problem."

\* \* \*
:::

Walt arrived at quarter past one, with his pigeons, as he put it, although he had left them outside. He sat down at the table, took off his cap, and declared the smell of smoke to be the finest aroma he had encountered since the Winter Harvest of 1982.

Edna Price came up the lane with a basket of winter greens from her garden plot, and a jar of honey that she set on the table with an air of having been very generous and wanting it noticed. She looked at the roast, then at Eli, then at Hannah, and said, "You'll want to carve it thinly. It's the only way to hide the charcoal."

"Thank you, Edna," said Eli, weakly.

"You're welcome," said Edna. "I always said your father should have been a chef. He never had the patience."

Tom Kowalski arrived with Biscuit, a tray of rolls, and a bottle of cider he had brewed himself in his garage and which he would not allow anyone to call anything but "a fine drink." The vicar came last, in his second-best sweater, carrying a box of mince pies and a bunch of holly that he had clearly stolen from the churchyard hedge.

By half past one, the back room was full of noise and steam and laughter, and the roast, rescued from the worst of the smoke by a combination of Mrs. Ortiz's brisk supervision and Hannah's better knife skills, had been carved and arranged on a platter with enough greens and honey and rosemary to disguise a multitude of sins.

"It's very good," said Walt, after his first mouthful, and then, after a second mouthful, with immense surprise, "It's actually very good, Eli."

"It's a miracle," said Mrs. Ortiz.

"It's the honey," said Eli, flushing with pleasure.

"It's the stubbornness," said Hannah, and kissed him on the cheek, in front of everybody, and felt him go warm and pink under her hand.

\* \* \*

After the meal, when the plates had been cleared and the cider was flowing and the vicar had begun, very quietly, to hum a hymn, Walt got up from his stool and tapped his glass with a fork.

"I'd like to say something," he said.

The room went quiet.

Walt looked around at the faces gathered in the warm glow of the fairy lights: the vicar, Edna, Tom with the dog asleep at his feet, Mrs. Ortiz with her arms folded, Eli standing by the stove with his dish towels, Hannah beside him with her hand on his arm.

"My wife Margaret," said Walt, "used to say that the best meals in the world were the ones where nobody could remember who cooked them, because everybody was too busy talking." He cleared his throat, a little gruffly. "I think that's what happened here today. Eli burned the fat. Hannah saved the roast. And the rest of us stopped being strangers somewhere between the honey and the second bottle of wine."

"Here, here," said the vicar, and raised his cider.

"I'm a lonely old man with a library and a bench and too many pigeons," said Walt. "I didn't think I'd ever have a Sunday dinner again. And now I've had two in one month." He lifted his glass, and his eyes were suspiciously bright. "To Hannah, who came home. To Eli, who stayed. And to Dorothy, who knew they would."

"To Dorothy," said the whole room, and drank.

Hannah found, to her surprise, that her eyes were stinging. She looked down at her glass and blinked hard, and felt Eli's hand slide into hers under the table, warm and rough and steady.

"Don't cry," he murmured.

"I'm not crying. The fairy lights are in my eyes."

"The fairy lights are on the ceiling," said Eli.

"Then it's the cider," said Hannah, and he laughed, very softly, and squeezed her fingers.

\* \* \*
:::

Later, after the others had gone and the dishes were stacked by the sink, Eli and Hannah sat on the bench in the back room with the fairy lights still glowing above them, and the smell of rosemary and cider lingering in the cold air.

"It was a good dinner," said Eli.

"It was a terrible dinner," said Hannah. "Your roast nearly set the building on fire."

"It was a good dinner," Eli insisted, and put his head on her shoulder. "Nobody left early. Walt told a story about a goat. Edna said nice things about me, which she's never done in thirty years, and then said she was only being polite. The vicar nearly fell off his stool. And Mrs. Ortiz didn't once tell me I was doing it wrong."

"She told you that the fat was smoking."

"That's not the same thing," said Eli, "and you know it."

Hannah laughed, and leaned her head against his, and let the warmth of the room settle over her like a blanket.

"Do you know what I thought," she said, "when I first came home? I thought I'd spend my whole life alone in this building, with the bread and the ledger and nobody to share the cake with. I thought I'd become my grandmother, the terrifying woman in the bakery who knew everybody's business and never let anyone in."

"And now?"

"Now I think she was never alone," said Hannah quietly. "She had Walt and Edna and Mrs. Ortiz and Manny and the vicar and a whole street full of people who came in for a loaf and stayed to talk. She just didn't tell anybody how much she needed them."

Eli was quiet for a moment.

"Then we'd better tell them," he said.

"Tell them what?"

"That we need them," said Eli. "That we're not very good at this. That the fat smokes and the starter bites and I still can't carve a roast without a fight. And that we'd like them to come back next Sunday, and bring something terrible to eat."

"Next Sunday," said Hannah.

"Next Sunday," said Eli, and kissed her, slowly, under the fairy lights, in the back room of the hardware store, while the dog snored at their feet and the first cold stars came out above the river.

# Chapter Twenty-Nine: The Offer Under the Door

The woman from the Pike office was not Bridget Sloane.

She was younger, with a neat gray coat, a leather portfolio tucked under one arm, and the bright, careful smile of someone who had been told to be charming and was not sure how much charm would be enough. She introduced herself as Caroline Wu, Associate Counsel, and she sat down at the corner table with the air of a woman who expected to be interrupted.

Mrs. Ortiz set a cup of coffee in front of her and said, "Drink that. It's terrible. You'll feel better for it."

Caroline Wu drank it, winced, and set the cup down. "Thank you," she said. "I'll be brief."

"Brief is the only thing we do well on Cutter Street," said Eli, from the doorway, where he had planted himself with his arms folded.

Caroline Wu glanced at him, and something flickered across her face that might have been respect. "Mr. Carver. I'm aware you've been appointed to the trust."

"I've been asked to consider it," said Eli.

"Then I'd like to make Mr. Pike's position clear." She opened the portfolio and laid a single document on the table, face up. It was thick, glossy, and marked in red with the words *CONFIDENTIAL — FOR THE OWNER OF RECORD ONLY*. "Mr. Pike is prepared to purchase 14 Cutter Street for four hundred and eighty thousand dollars, cash, with a closing date in January. He's also prepared to pay the back taxes in full, to cover the cost of the wiring and structural repairs, and to offer a lifetime lease on the ground-floor kitchen for any member of the Brooks family who wishes to continue baking there."

Hannah stared at the figure.

It was more money than she had seen in her life. It was more than her grandmother had ever had in the bank at one time. It was enough to repay the taxes, fix the oven, hire a proper electrician, and buy Eli a new van with a heater that actually worked.

"A lifetime lease," said Mrs. Ortiz slowly, "for a kitchen Mr. Pike will own."

"Yes."

"And what happens to the front of the shop?"

"That's subject to the redevelopment plan," said Caroline Wu. "Which, I should add, includes a heritage corridor and a number of very desirable commercial spaces. The bakery would be the anchor tenant. There's a sign already drafted."

"*Dorothy's Table*," said Hannah quietly.

"Yes."

"With a parking garage behind it."

"With a parking garage behind it," agreed Caroline Wu, and did not look away.

Eli had not moved from the doorway. His jaw was tight, and his hands were clenched in the sleeves of his jacket.

"Mr. Pike knows about the easement," he said.

"Mr. Pike is aware of the trust," said Caroline Wu. "He believes the easement can be resolved through negotiation. He'd prefer to avoid a public fight. It's expensive for everyone."

"He knew about it in the summer," said Hannah. "He had the clerk check whether it still existed. He sent his assistants around the county records office to see if anybody had ever read the trust deed. He's been trying to get around it since before I came home."

"That's a matter for counsel," said Caroline Wu, very evenly.

"And you're the counsel."

"I'm one of the counsel."

"Then tell Mr. Pike," said Hannah, rising to her feet, "that my grandmother wrote *no bulldozers on Cutter Street* in her own handwriting, in blue ink, and underlined it three times. Tell him she meant it. Tell him I've got the trust deed in a safe in the hardware store, and the trustee is standing right behind you, and he's not for sale."

Caroline Wu was silent for a moment. Then she gathered the document back into her portfolio, slowly, the way a person might pick up a pair of scissors she had accidentally dropped.

"You're making a mistake," she said quietly.

"I've made plenty," said Hannah. "This one's mine."

Caroline Wu stood up and buttoned her gray coat. At the door, she paused, with one gloved hand on the frame, and looked back over her shoulder at Hannah with an expression that was not quite pity and not quite warmth.

"Mr. Pike asked me to say one more thing," she said. "He said to tell you that your grandmother's last letter to him was the most honest thing anyone's ever written to him. He's kept it. He reads it on Sundays."

Then she was gone, and the bell above the door rang softly behind her, and the bakery was very quiet.

\* \* \*
:::

The offer letter was still in the drawer of the desk that night when Hannah opened it.

She had not meant to look. She had come into the office to find the spreadsheet for the Bread Club subscriptions, and the drawer had stuck, and when she yanked it open, the cream envelope from Marian Lowell had slid out on top of a pile of bank statements, the gold foil crest catching the light from the lamp.

She read it again. Then she took a pen from the mug on the desk and, before she could think better of it, wrote a reply on the back of an invoice.

*Marian, thank you. I'd like to talk about January. I can't promise anything yet. I have a family thing going on, and a building that needs me more than I thought. But I'd like to keep the door open. Hannah.*

She stared at the words for a long time.

Then she folded the invoice in half, put it in an envelope, addressed it to Marian Lowell at the firm's Michigan Avenue office, and walked it down to the mailbox on the corner in the freezing dark, without telling a single soul.

It was the first secret she had kept since she came home.

It was a very small one, she told herself, as she dropped the envelope through the slot and heard it land with a soft, final sound. She was only keeping the door open. She was only keeping her options alive, in case Pike won, in case the easement failed, in case the back taxes swallowed the bakery whole.

She walked back up Cutter Street with her hands in her pockets, past the lit windows of the diner and the hardware store, and she did not look across the road at the back room where Eli was working late on the freezer.

She did not look, because she knew what she would see on his face.

And because she knew she would not be able to lie to him if she did.

# Chapter Thirty: The Phone on the Counter

The phone rang at nine-fifteen on Monday morning, while Hannah was upstairs changing the sheets and Eli was downstairs wrestling with the walk-in door.

He picked it up on the fourth ring, wiping his hands on a rag. "Cutter Street Bakery."

"Oh, good, I was hoping to catch someone," said a bright, cool voice that Eli did not recognize. "This is Marian Lowell, from Lowell & Pierce in Chicago. I'm calling for Hannah Brooks. Is she available?"

Eli stood very still, the rag pressed in one fist.

"She's upstairs," he said. "Can I take a message?"

"Oh, please tell her I'm delighted about January. The start date works perfectly. We'll have her badge ready by the second week, and I've already told Pike's people that she'll be running the launch party herself. They're thrilled. Everyone is thrilled." There was a little laugh. "Tell her she made the right decision. She'll see it's the right thing once she's back in the city."

"Right," said Eli.

"And if she has any questions about the relocation package, she can call me directly. I'll be in meetings all week, but my assistant has the number."

"I'll tell her," said Eli.

He hung up. He put the phone back on its cradle with great care, as if it might bite him, and then he stood looking at it for a long time, while the walk-in door hummed behind him and the morning light crept across the flour bins.

Upstairs, Hannah was singing something tuneless and cheerful, in the way she did when she had forgotten that anyone could hear.

Eli picked up the phone again, looked at the number on the screen, and sat down on an upturned crate in the middle of the kitchen. He held the phone in both hands and did not move.

*January.*

*Relocation package.*

*She'll see it's the right thing once she's back in the city.*

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

Hannah came down the stairs with an armful of clean towels and found him sitting on the crate, with his elbows on his knees and his head bowed.

"Eli?"

He did not look up.

"Was that the freezer company?" she asked brightly, dropping the towels on the table. "Because Tom said they'd sent the wrong part again, and I told him that if they sent one more—"

"It was Marian Lowell," said Eli.

The brightness went out of her face all at once, like a light switched off in a room.

"Oh," she said.

"She says hello." He lifted his head. His eyes were dark and very tired. "She says she's delighted about January. She says the start date works. She says she's already told Pike's people you'll be running the launch party yourself, and that everyone is thrilled, and that you made the right decision."

Hannah said nothing.

"Did you make the right decision?" asked Eli quietly.

"I didn't make any decision," said Hannah. "I wrote a letter."

"What did the letter say?"

"That I'd like to talk about January." She crossed her arms over her chest, as if she were cold, though the kitchen was warm from the ovens. "That I couldn't promise anything. That I had a family thing and a building that needed me."

"And what did you say about the building?"

"I said I'd keep the door open."

"You said you'd keep the door open," said Eli, very slowly, "and then you didn't tell me."

"It was a letter, Eli. It was one letter. It doesn't mean anything."

"It means she's already told Pike's people you're coming back." He stood up, and the crate scraped loudly across the tiles. "It means you've been sitting in this office every night, doing the sums and the spreadsheets and telling me the taxes are going to be fine, while you've got a plan in a drawer to get on a train to Chicago and sell the bread and the bakery and your grandmother's name to a man who's been trying to buy this street for three years."

"I'm trying to keep the bakery open!"

"Then keep it open," said Eli. "Don't keep a door in Chicago."

"You don't understand what it's like—"

"I understand exactly what it's like." His voice cracked on the last word, and he stopped, and pressed his fist against his mouth. When he took it away, his face was white. "I understand what it's like to watch someone you care about walk out the door and tell you it's temporary. My mother said that. *Just a few weeks, Eli, I'll be back before the spring.* She had a suitcase by the door when I came down for breakfast, and she'd left a note on the counter that said she was sorry."

Hannah's throat tightened. "Eli—"

"I'm not asking you to stay," he said. "I'm asking you to tell me the truth. That's all. Just once. Not in a letter. Not on the phone to somebody else. To me."

"The truth is that I don't know what I want!" Hannah's voice rose sharply, and the sound bounced off the tiles. "The truth is that I'm terrified. The truth is that every morning I wake up and I think, this is it, this is the bakery, this is the place I always said I'd never come back to, and it's the only place I've ever wanted to be. And then I think, what if I'm wrong? What if the oven breaks, and the taxes swallow it, and I've got nothing left but a dog and a sourdough starter and a man who won't let me take my own coat off without asking whether I'm cold?"

Eli stared at her.

"A man who won't let you take your coat off," he said.

"That's not the point!"

"It's a little bit the point," said Eli, and something in his face cracked open, painfully and unwillingly. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I didn't mean to make a joke of it."

"You always make a joke of it."

"I make a joke of everything I'm afraid of." He took a breath. "I'm afraid of you going back to Chicago, Hannah. I'm afraid of it so much I can't sleep. I'm afraid of you deciding the bakery is too much, and the town is too small, and I'm too stubborn and too boring and too broken to be worth staying for. And I'm especially afraid of finding out from a stranger on the phone that you've already decided."

The kitchen was very quiet. Upstairs, a pipe knocked twice and went still.

"I haven't decided," said Hannah, in a much smaller voice.

"Then tell me what you're going to do."

"I don't know."

"Then tell me you'll think about it," he said. "Tell me it's not already done."

She opened her mouth to answer. She thought of the envelope dropping through the mailbox slot, the soft, final sound it made. She thought of Marian's bright, cool voice on the phone, and the badge that would be ready in the second week, and the apartment in Chicago she had not yet given up, with its cold white tiles and its beautiful view of nothing at all.

She could not make herself say the words.

Eli watched her face, and whatever he saw there made him close his eyes.

"Okay," he said. "Okay."

"Eli—"

"I need to check the freezer." He picked up his toolbox from beside the door, very carefully, as if it weighed a great deal more than it did. "The compressor's still making that noise. Don't wait up."

"Eli, please."

He paused on the threshold, one hand on the frame, the way he had on the first night. He did not turn around.

"Come and find me when you know what you want," he said quietly. "I'll be the one who stayed."

Then the back door closed, and the bell over the front of the shop gave a faint, trembling ring, as if someone had moved too quickly past it on the way out.

Hannah stood alone in the warm kitchen, with the towels on the table and the dough breathing softly under its cloth. She picked up the phone, looked at Marian's number on the screen, and then put it down again without dialing.

Biscuit came in from the front of the shop, looked at her, looked at the back door, and sat down heavily on her foot, as if to make sure she did not follow.

# Chapter Thirty-One: A Letter Not Sent

The apartment above the bakery was small and cold, and Hannah had never once thought of it as home until the night after she told Eli the truth.

She sat at her grandmother's kitchen table with a cup of tea going cold in her hands and a sheet of plain white paper in front of her. The overhead light buzzed. The radiator ticked. Downstairs, through the floorboards, she could hear the faint, reassuring hum of the new walk-in freezer, which Eli had fixed on Tuesday with a grim look of satisfaction and a mouth full of nails.

She had been trying to write to Marian Lowell for an hour.

She had started three times. The first draft had been polite and businesslike, the sort of letter she had written a hundred times for the firm. *Dear Marian, thank you for your generous offer. Unfortunately, I have decided to remain in Ohio.* She had crossed it out before she reached the end of the first line, because it sounded like a rejection letter from a university.

The second draft had been angry. *Marian, you should have asked me. You should have known. You knew me better than anyone, and you still thought I'd sell my grandmother's bakery to Garrett Pike for a relocation package.* She had torn that one into four pieces and dropped them into the trash can.

The third draft had said nothing at all, except a single line in the middle of the page, written very small. *I'm sorry I didn't tell you the truth.* It was not to Marian. It was to herself.

She picked up the pen again and looked at the paper for a long time.

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

Her phone buzzed on the table beside her. It was Jess.

"You're up late," said her sister, without preamble. "Or you never went to bed. I can hear it in your voice."

"I'm trying to write a letter."

"To who?"

"Marian."

"Oh, God," said Jess. "Is it the one about the badge?"

"It's the one about everything," said Hannah, and rubbed her eyes. "I told Eli. About the offer. About the letter I sent last week. I told him I was going to call Marian in the morning and tell her no. And then I didn't call her. I sat in this kitchen and I cried for an hour and then I went back to bed."

Jess was silent for a moment.

"And now?"

"And now I can't write it down," said Hannah. "I keep trying to make it sound like I'm being brave. But I'm not brave, Jess. I'm just scared of being the person who ran away twice."

"You're not that person."

"I was that person for eight years."

"You were a person who needed a reason to stay," said Jess firmly. "And now you've got one. You've got a bakery and a street and a dog who's been caught digging up a river wall, and a man who fixed the freezer for the fourth time this month because he couldn't bear to see you cold." Her voice softened. "You're allowed to be scared, Han. You're just not allowed to be scared and quiet. Say it. Write it. Make her read it."

"What if she's angry?"

"Then she's angry," said Jess. "And you'll survive it. You've survived worse. You survived me."

Hannah laughed in spite of herself.

"Go to bed," said her sister. "Write the letter in the morning. And then tell the man with the freezer that you love him, before he fixes something else and ruins the moment."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

Hannah did not go to bed.

She picked up the pen again, and this time she did not try to make it sound like anything. She simply wrote what was in her head, one line after another, as honestly as she could.

*Marian,*

*I'm not coming to Chicago. I know you've told Pike's people I'm coming, and I know you'll be disappointed, and I know it will look like I'm throwing away the best opportunity of my career. But I'm not throwing it away. I'm choosing something else.*

*I came home to sell my grandmother's building. I had a plan, and a train ticket, and a letter in a drawer. I was going to be sensible and efficient and gone by the spring. And then I walked down the street and met a man who fixes things for people who can't pay him, and a dog who has no shame, and a woman who has been running a diner on Cutter Street for forty years and still thinks the coffee is good enough for strangers. I met the people who bought my grandmother's bread every Saturday for thirty years, and who still say hello when they see me, as if she's just stepped out for a moment.*

*I thought I was the only one who could save that place. I was wrong. The street saved me first.*

*I don't expect you to understand. But you taught me, a long time ago, that the best thing you can do for someone is to tell them the truth. So here it is. I'm staying. I'm sorry I took so long to say it. I hope you find someone better than me to run your launch party. They will be very lucky, and they will probably be more organized than I ever was.*

*With love, and with more gratitude than I know how to say,*

*Hannah*

She read it through once. Then she read it again. Her hands were shaking, and her eyes were stinging, and the paper was damp at the edges where she had pressed too hard with the pen.

It was not the letter she had wanted to write. It was the one she had been afraid to send.

She folded it into an envelope, sealed it, and wrote Marian's name across the front in careful capitals. Then she sat for a long time with her hand resting on the envelope, listening to the freezer hum and the radiator tick and the first gray light beginning to creep up over the roofs of Cutter Street.

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

At six o'clock, she went down the stairs in her coat, with the envelope in one hand and the dog's leash in the other, and walked out into the cold.

Biscuit had been waiting for her by the door. He trotted beside her down the frosty sidewalk, his breath steaming in the air, and did not once try to sniff anything, which she took as a sign of genuine emotional crisis.

She posted the letter in the box on the corner, and heard it land with a soft, final sound, and this time she did not feel the cold weight of a secret settling in her chest. She felt light, and clear, and strangely calm, as though she had finally put down something she had been carrying up a hill for a very long time.

Across the road, the lights were already on in the back of the hardware store.

Hannah stood on the sidewalk for a moment, looking at them. Then she pulled the dog's leash gently, and crossed the road, and went around to the back door, where she knew Eli would be standing with a mug of terrible coffee in his hand, staring at the freezer as though it had personally offended him.

She knocked twice.

The door opened almost at once. He was wearing a red wool scarf over an old gray sweater, and his hair was sticking up at the back, and his face when he saw her was so open and so relieved that she forgot, for a moment, what she had come to say.

"You're up early," said Eli.

"I posted a letter."

He went very still. "To Marian?"

"To Marian," said Hannah. "I told her I'm staying."

Eli stared at her for a long moment. Then he set down the coffee mug on the nearest crate, very carefully, and stepped out into the cold.

"Say it again," he said hoarsely. "Please. I want to hear it in the daylight."

"I'm staying," said Hannah, and the words came out clear and steady and entirely her own. "I'm staying, Eli. I'm staying with the bread and the dog and the freezer that you fix every week because you don't know how to stop. I'm staying with you."

He crossed the yard in three long strides, and did not say anything at all. He simply took her face in both his hands, his palms cold and rough and shaking very slightly, and kissed her, under the gray dawn sky, in full view of the dog, the freezer, and the whole of Cutter Street waking slowly around them.

Biscuit sat down on Hannah's foot with a deep, satisfied sigh and looked up at them both, as if to say that he had known all along, and had been waiting very patiently for them to notice.

# Chapter Thirty-Two: The Night of the Blackout

The storm came in off the river on the Wednesday before Christmas, and by nine o'clock that evening, Cutter Street had lost its power.

It went all at once. One moment the streetlamps were burning gold through the sleet, and the diner's neon sign was humming in the window, and Hannah was standing at the sink with her hands in cold water, thinking about nothing except how tired she was. The next, the kitchen went black. The fridges fell silent. The oven's pilot light gave a faint, lonely hiss and went out, and somewhere far off, a car alarm started up and then stopped, as if it had thought better of it.

"Hannah?" Mrs. Ortiz's voice came through the dark from the front of the shop. "Are you there, dear?"

"I'm here."

"Is it the whole street?"

Hannah walked to the window and looked out. Across the road, the hardware store was dark. The diner was dark. The church was dark. The only light on Cutter Street was the thin, flickering beam of a single flashlight, moving slowly down the middle of the road, and it belonged to a man in a yellow hard hat who was walking towards the river with a clipboard under one arm and his collar turned up against the sleet.

"It's the whole street," said Hannah, and her stomach turned to ice. "And it's the river side."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

Eli got there twenty minutes later, soaked to the skin, with a headlamp strapped over his beanie and a face like thunder.

"Pike's crew cut the feeder line," he said, shaking water from his hair. "They were boring under the back lot for the drainage survey. They didn't bother to check the map, or they checked it and didn't care. Half the grid's down from the bridge to the diner."

"How long?"

"Hours. Maybe till morning." He pulled off his gloves and looked at the dead oven. "The walk-in's going to go in an hour. Maybe less. Once the cold goes, the stock goes."

"We've got three hundred loaves for the shelter in the morning."

"I know."

"The Bread Club is collecting at seven."

"I know, Hannah."

"And I don't have an oven, and the flour's in the back, and the dough's rising for the proof, and if we don't do something in the next hour—"

"We bake them by hand," said Eli.

She stared at him.

"There's a wood-fired oven in the back of the hardware store," he said. "Dad put it in for the old stove restoration. Nobody's used it since the eighties, but it works. I've been checking it every spring. It's cold, it's dusty, and it takes three hours to heat, but it works." He pulled his beanie off and ran a hand over his wet hair. "And the store's got a generator. Not much of one, but enough for the walk-in and the lights."

"Eli, that's—"

"A terrible idea." He was already moving towards the door. "Get the dough. Bring every tray you've got. And wear a coat, because it's going to be freezing."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

The hardware store was cold and smelled of oil and paint thinner and old brick. Eli had lit the generator in the back room, and it clattered and coughed like an old man clearing his throat, but the light it threw was steady and yellow. He had dragged a folding table into the center of the floor and covered it with clean sheets. In the corner, under a dusty tarpaulin, was an enormous black iron oven with a cast-iron door, its brick chimney running up through the ceiling.

"It's bigger than I remembered," said Hannah.

"Dad used to say it was the only thing in the building that never let him down." Eli knelt in front of it and opened the firebox. "Hand me the kindling. And the newspaper. And anything else that burns."

They built the fire together. It took forty minutes and four attempts, and by the end of it they were both black with soot and laughing helplessly, and Biscuit had sneezed twice and fled to the far corner of the store with an expression of profound betrayal. But the kindling caught at last, and the flames climbed up through the grate, and the old iron began to tick and groan as the heat seeped into it.

"It's going," said Eli softly, and sat back on his heels, staring at the fire with something that looked very like wonder.

Hannah sat down on the floor beside him, her back against a stack of paint tins, and watched the light flicker across his face.

"Your father must have loved this," she said.

"He did. He used to bake bread in here on Christmas Eve, when nobody else was awake. He said it was the only time the store belonged to him." Eli stared into the fire. "He didn't have your grandmother's touch. His loaves came out like bricks. But he loved it anyway."

"You've got his hands," said Hannah. "Gran said so."

"She said I had his hands and none of his patience."

"She was right about that too."

He turned, and looked at her across the glowing firebox, and his face in the orange light was very tired and very gentle.

"Hannah," he said.

"I know what I want," she said.

He went still.

She drew a breath. The fire crackled. Somewhere far off, the sleet hammered against the window, and the generator coughed and steadied.

"I want to stay," said Hannah. "I want to stay on Cutter Street. I want the oven, and the freezer, and the dog who won't stop sulking, and the Bread Club, and Mrs. Ortiz telling me my coffee's terrible. I want Gran's ledger in the safe and her handwriting on the wall. I want to see the sign over the door with the right name on it." She swallowed, hard. "And I want you."

Eli did not move.

"I told Marian I'm staying," she said. "I wrote it down and posted it on the corner, and I should have told you to your face before I did. I was afraid that if I said it out loud, you'd look at me the way you're looking at me now. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I should have said it on the first night, or the second, or any night since. You were right. You were always right. And I've been running away from it since I got off that plane."

He let out a breath that was almost a sob.

"I'm not going to Chicago," said Hannah. "I've already told Marian no, and I meant it. Now I'm going to tell Pike's counsel that the building isn't for sale. I'm going to keep the door shut, Eli. Because I don't want it open anymore."

He crossed the floor in three steps and knelt in front of her, his hands on her knees, his face very close to hers. There was soot on his cheekbone and a smudge of ash on his chin, and his eyes were wet and fierce.

"You're sure?" he said.

"I'm sure."

"Because I'm not going to pretend I'm not terrified," he said, low and rough. "I'm going to have nightmares about the freezer and the boiler and the wretched feeder line for the rest of my life. And I'm going to keep fixing things that don't need fixing, and I'm going to burn the toast every single morning, and I'm going to be stubborn about the oven and bossy about the dog and I'm going to make you cold on purpose just to watch you put on your second sweater."

"I know," she said. "I'm counting on it."

He laughed, a broken, helpless sound, and then he kissed her, slowly and with great care, as if he had waited a very long time to be allowed to do it properly. The fire roared behind them. The old iron ticked and groaned. Biscuit came crawling out from behind the paint tins, sniffed the air, sneezed, and lay down across both their feet with a sigh of enormous contentment.

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

They baked until four in the morning.

They shaped the dough by lantern light, and the flour flew up in clouds that made them both cough. They slid trays into the old oven with a wooden paddle, and the heat hit their faces like a wave. Mrs. Ortiz arrived at midnight with a flask of coffee so strong it made Hannah's eyes water, and she took one look at the state of the hardware store and said, "Well, I never," and then rolled up her sleeves and started shaping loaves without another word. Tom Kowalski came at one with a box of eggs and a tray of rolls he had made himself, badly, and nobody had the heart to tell him so. The vicar came at two, carrying a stack of dish towels, and was made to wash every tray twice.

By the time the first gray light crept through the high, dusty windows of the store, there were three hundred loaves cooling on the racks along the back wall, and the smell of baking bread had filled every corner of the building.

Hannah sat down on the floor, with her back against the paint tins and her hands black with soot and flour, and laughed until she cried.

Eli sat down beside her and put his head on her shoulder.

"That," he said, "was the stupidest thing I've ever done."

"It was the best thing you've ever done."

"Same thing," said Eli, and fell asleep.

When the power came back at seven, with a soft electric hum and a hundred little lights flickering on across the street, the first thing Hannah saw was the Bread Club line, already curling around the corner of the church in the frosted dawn, with the vicar's wife at the front holding a clipboard and Mrs. Ortiz at the back with her arms folded, glaring at anyone who tried to cut in.

And on the window of the bakery, somebody had taped a sheet of paper, written in careful capitals in a hand that Hannah knew was Eli's: *TONIGHT WE BAKED THREE HUNDRED LOAVES BY HAND. NOBODY LOOKED AT THE OVEN. THE DOG WAS PERSONALLY INSULTED.*

Hannah laughed so hard she had to sit down on the step.

# Chapter Thirty-Three: The Trustee

The council chamber was packed to the walls by six o'clock, and the radiators could not keep up.

People stood along the back and down both side aisles. The vicar had brought a folding chair from the church and was sitting in it with his coat buttoned to the neck. Mrs. Ortiz had claimed the front row for the diner and was guarding it with her handbag. Tom Kowalski had brought Biscuit, despite a sign on the door forbidding dogs, and had explained to the doorman that the dog was an emotional support animal for the entire town, which was technically true, and the doorman had given up.

Hannah sat in the second row, with her hands in her lap and Jess's arm pressed firmly against hers. Her sister had driven down from Dayton that morning, with her husband and two sleepy children in the back seat, and had hugged Hannah in the parking lot for so long that Hannah's ribs still ached.

"You're going to be fine," Jess had whispered. "You're going to be terrifying."

"I'm going to be sick."

"You're going to be magnificent. Eli's going to be sick. You're going to hold his hand and tell him he's magnificent too."

Eli sat at the front of the room, at a long table beside Mr. Dorsey, with a stack of papers in front of him and a glass of water he had not touched. He wore a dark gray suit that Hannah had never seen before, and it fitted him slightly too well across the shoulders, as if somebody had altered it in a hurry. His hair was still damp from the shower, and he kept pushing it back off his forehead with one hand.

Every few minutes, he glanced across the room at Hannah. And every time he did, he gave her a small, nervous, lopsided smile that made her want to climb over the chairs and hold him.

Across the aisle, Garrett Pike sat with his lawyers, looking immaculate. Beside him, Bridget Sloane was studying her phone with an expression of studied boredom, and she did not look up once.

"Order," said the mayor, tapping the gavel. "Order, please. We have a full agenda. First item: the Pike Ridge Development amended plan for 14 Cutter Street, and the objection of the Cutter Street Preservation Trust."

The room went very quiet.

Garrett Pike's lawyer rose first, a tall, thin man in a navy tie who spoke as if he were reading a menu aloud. He argued that the trust was dormant, that its original trustee had died more than thirty years ago, and that its surviving seat had never been formally filled. He argued that the easement was an historical oddity, unenforceable in practice, and that Mr. Pike's amended plan preserved the building's facade and therefore honored the spirit of the agreement. He argued for twelve minutes and sat down to a murmur of uneasy approval.

Then Mr. Dorsey stood.

"The trust is not dormant," he said, in his dry, precise voice. "The trust has had two trustees since 1990, and since the death of the second, it has had one. That trustee is present in this room." He turned, and every head in the chamber turned with him. "Mr. Eli Carver, son of the late Hollis Carver, was appointed under section six of the trust deed in December. The appointment was witnessed. The records are on file. I have copies for the council."

He passed a stack of papers to the clerk. The clerk passed them to the mayor, who read the first page with a faint frown, as if he had been hoping very hard that it would not be true.

"Mr. Carver," said the mayor. "Do you wish to speak on behalf of the trust?"

Eli stood up.

For a moment, he did not say anything at all. He stood very still at the table, his hands flat on the papers in front of him, looking down at the surface of the wood as if he were trying to read something written there. Hannah saw his throat move as he swallowed. She saw his knuckles go white.

Then he lifted his head, and his eyes went straight across the room to hers.

*Stay with me,* she thought, and did not know whether she had said it aloud.

He took a breath.

"My father was a hardware man," said Eli. His voice was rough at first, then steadier. "He ran the store across the road for forty years. He used to say that a good building is like a good tool. You don't throw it out when it's a little worn. You fix the wiring. You replace the hinge. You keep it in use, because a thing that's still working is worth more than a thing that's shiny and new and empty."

Somebody in the back laughed, quietly.

"He also used to say," Eli went on, "that there's a difference between selling a building and selling the street it stands on. And he never sold either. Not when the bank wanted him to. Not when the developers came in 1994, or in 2003, or three years ago, when they offered him three times what the store was worth." He glanced at the papers. "He was offered a lot of money by people who didn't know what the street was for."

"Mr. Carver," said the mayor, "I'm going to have to ask you to address the question of the trust."

"I am addressing it," said Eli. "The trust deed is very clear. Dorothy Brooks and my father put it in writing in 1990. The building at 14 Cutter Street cannot be demolished. The block cannot be redeveloped without the consent of the trustee. And I, as the trustee, do not consent."

The room erupted.

It was not loud, at first. It was a rustling, rising murmur, like wind moving through long grass. People leaned in to each other, whispering. The vicar stood up in his folding chair and shouted "Hear, hear!" in a voice that carried clear to the back wall. Mrs. Ortiz banged her handbag on the front row. Tom Kowalski put two fingers in his mouth and whistled, and Biscuit barked once, enthusiastically, and was hushed by about forty people at once.

The mayor hammered the gavel, again and again. "Order! Order! Ladies and gentlemen—"

"Mr. Mayor," said Garrett Pike.

The room went silent again.

He rose slowly to his feet. He did not look angry. He did not look anything at all, except tired, and faintly disappointed, like a man who had expected a better game.

"I'd like to say a few words," he said.

"Mr. Pike, the public comment period—"

"I'll be brief." He turned to face the room, and his voice was quiet and perfectly calm. "I've been coming to this town for three years. I've listened to the people on Cutter Street. I've listened to Mrs. Brooks, and to her granddaughter, and to Mr. Carver. I respect them. I respect what they've built. I don't think any of us should be fighting over a bakery." He paused. "But I also think that the people who live here deserve to see something new happen to their town before it's too late."

He looked at Eli, and for the first time that evening, his expression was almost gentle.

"Dorothy Brooks wrote to me in March," he said. "She told me that the building was worth more than my money. She told me to go back to Columbus and find something that actually needed saving." He smiled faintly. "She was the only person in this county who ever spoke to me like that. I kept her letter. I'll keep it for the rest of my life."

Hannah felt her breath catch.

"Then why are you still here?" said Eli quietly.

Pike met his gaze across the room.

"Because I'm not finished," he said. "And neither are you."

He sat down. The room was silent, and the mayor cleared his throat, and then, after a long and visibly uncomfortable pause, he looked down at his papers and said, "The council will defer its decision on the amended plan until its January session. The trust's objection is noted. Mr. Carver, you'll be notified of the date. Mr. Pike, I'd remind you that the council takes its obligations seriously."

"Of course," said Pike, and smiled politely.

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

They met afterwards in the parking lot, in the bitter cold, under a flickering lamp that buzzed like a trapped insect.

Eli was still in his suit. He had loosened his tie and taken off his coat, and his shirt was damp at the collar, and he looked as if he had just run a marathon and then been asked to explain it.

"I think I'm going to throw up," he said.

"You're not going to throw up."

"I might."

"Then I'll hold your hair back," said Hannah, "and tell the mayor you're getting into character."

He laughed, weakly, and then pulled her into his arms, right there in the middle of the parking lot, with the lamp buzzing overhead and Jess's car pulling out of the space beside them, and the sound of voices drifting from the steps of the council chamber. He held her tightly, his face buried in her hair.

"You were brilliant," she said, muffled against his shirt.

"I was terrifying."

"You were both."

"He said he kept her letter," said Eli quietly. "Pike. He said he reads it on Sundays."

"I know. Caroline Wu told me."

"I don't know what to do with that."

"I don't think you're supposed to know," said Hannah. "I think you're supposed to hold on, and see what happens next."

He pulled back, just enough to look at her. His eyes were red-rimmed and very bright.

"What happens next?" he asked.

"Jess is going to drive us to the diner," said Hannah, "and Mrs. Ortiz is going to buy us both a plate of fries and tell us we were terrible, and then we're going to go back to Cutter Street and sleep for twelve hours."

"That sounds like a very good plan."

"It's the only plan I've got."

"It's the best plan anyone's ever had," said Eli, and kissed her, under the buzzing lamp, while the snow began to drift down through the cold dark air, soft and silent and slow.

# Chapter Thirty-Four: What Bridget Knew

Bridget Sloane came to the bakery on the last Saturday before Christmas, after closing, with her coat buttoned wrong and a leather folder pressed against her chest like a shield.

Hannah heard the knock on the front door and nearly ignored it. Then she saw the woman's face through the glass, pale and drawn beneath the streetlamp, and she unlocked the door without a word.

"I'm not here for the bread," said Bridget.

"I didn't think you were."

"I'm not here for Pike, either." Bridget stepped inside and shut the door behind her, very quietly, as if she were afraid the street might hear. "I'm here because I'm tired, Ms. Brooks. And because I don't want to be the person who did this anymore."

Hannah looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded at the chair by the front window and went to put the kettle on.

Bridget sat down heavily and set the folder on the table. It was worn at the corners, and it had been handled so often that the cover had gone soft as cloth. She did not open it. She just laid her hands flat on top of it, the way Eli had laid his hands on the trust deed, as if she needed to feel that it was still there.

"I came to this town six months ago," she said. "Pike hired me to make people comfortable. That was the job. I was supposed to have coffee with the old women and the diner owners and the church committee. I was supposed to listen. I was supposed to make them feel that the redevelopment was their idea."

"And did you?"

"I made them feel nervous," said Bridget. "That was the part Pike liked best. He said nervous people sign things faster." She gave a short, bitter laugh. "I'm very good at it. I could make your grandmother's neighbors believe the water supply was going to be cut off if they didn't sell their houses by Easter. I did it in two other towns. I'm not proud of it."

The kettle began to whistle. Hannah poured the water into two mugs and set one in front of Bridget, who wrapped both hands around it without drinking.

"Why are you telling me this?" asked Hannah.

"Because of your grandmother's letter," said Bridget quietly.

Hannah froze.

"Pike keeps it in his desk," said Bridget. "He takes it out on Sundays. I've seen him do it. He reads it with his glasses on, very slowly, and then he puts it back in the drawer and locks it. He doesn't say anything. He just sits there." She paused. "I asked him once what it said. He told me it was the only letter he'd ever received that didn't want anything from him."

"And that's why you're here?"

"No," said Bridget. "I'm here because of what was in the file."

She opened the folder at last and turned it around so Hannah could see. The first page was a memorandum, typed on heavy paper, dated the previous March. It was headed *Cutter Street Acquisition — Strategy*, and beneath it were columns of figures, timelines, and names.

Hannah's eye went down the page, and stopped on a line near the bottom.

*Priority: obtain seller signature BEFORE beneficiary reviews county file. Easement risk low if seller unaware.*

"That's not what I think it is," said Hannah, very quietly.

"It's exactly what it is," said Bridget. "Pike knew about the trust. He's known since before he bought the first building on the block. His father knew about it too. Hollis's 1989 loan to Dorothy was the first thing the Pike family ever tried to buy on Cutter Street. Pike's father bought the bakery's back debts from a bank in Columbus, hoping to foreclose if the Brooks bakery defaulted. Hollis paid them off before they could. He paid in cash, and he told the Pike lawyer to take a running jump in writing."

Hannah stared at her.

"That's in the file?"

"That's in the file," said Bridget. "And there's a letter in there from Garrett Pike's father to his lawyer, dated the spring of 1990. It says the Carver man was *a stubborn fool who will not be bought, and I admire him for it*. Pike's father never forgot it. I don't think Garrett did either. I think he spent the last three years trying to prove that everybody has a price, just to show that the old man was wrong."

"And your grandmother was the last one who said no," said Bridget softly.

Hannah pressed her hand to her mouth.

Bridget turned the next page. It was a list of properties on Cutter Street, each one with a name beside it and a column of numbers. Most of them had a tick mark. Some had a question mark. One, at the very top, had a large, neat cross through it, and beside it, in Pike's sharp, slanted hand, a single word.

*Brooks.*

"He's been trying to buy you out for three years," said Bridget. "And every time, he got the same answer. And every time he sent back a memo that said, *We need a different angle.*" She tapped the page. "The different angle was you. He thought you'd be easier. He thought you'd come back from Chicago, tired and scared and wanting to be rid of the place. He was right, for a while."

"I almost sold it," said Hannah.

"I know," said Bridget. "I read your letter to Marian Lowell. Pike's assistant had it copied. He thought it was very funny."

Hannah closed her eyes.

"I'm not telling you this to make you feel bad," said Bridget. "I'm telling you because I've been sitting in that office for six months listening to Pike talk about your grandmother like she was a line item. And last Thursday, he told me to call the county clerk and find out whether the trust could be dissolved on a technicality. He wanted to make it go away without a fight." She set her mug down with a quiet clink. "I went to the clerk myself. I found Mr. Dorsey instead. He looked at me the way you'd look at a cockroach in the flour, and then he gave me a copy of the trust deed and told me to read it twice."

"He's good at that," said Hannah faintly.

"He's very good at it." Bridget almost smiled. "I read it. And I thought, *Oh.* And then I thought, *Oh, God*, and I went home and was sick in the sink."

"Why didn't you just leave?"

"Because I'm a coward," said Bridget simply. "And I needed the money. And because I liked being good at something, even if it was the wrong thing. You're not the only one who's ever been good at a bad job, Ms. Brooks."

Hannah looked at the woman across the table, with her wrong-buttoned coat and her soft, worn folder and her eyes red at the rims from lack of sleep, and something tight and angry in her chest began, very slowly, to loosen.

"What do you want me to do with it?" she asked.

"Whatever you think is right." Bridget pushed the folder across the table. "I've made copies of the memo and the Pike letters. I've sent them to Mr. Dorsey, and to the state attorney general's office in Columbus, because I'm not an idiot and I know what a conflict of interest looks like on paper. And I'm leaving town on the twenty-second." She stood up and buttoned her coat properly, for once, her fingers clumsy with cold. "Pike's already got a new person lined up for Cutter Street. She's better than me. She's younger. She'll be very convincing."

"Then why are you telling me?"

"Because your grandmother's letter said something I couldn't stop thinking about," said Bridget. "She wrote that Pike should stop buying things and start asking people what they wanted. She said he'd never be happy until somebody told him no to his face and meant it." She looked at Hannah. "You told him no. In Marian's office, and at the council, and in your own kitchen, I'd guess. I wanted to tell you that it worked."

"Did it?"

"Pike's withdrawn the amended plan," said Bridget. "He filed it an hour ago. He's not going to fight the trust in January. He's going to sell the option on the river land and walk away, and he's going to tell everybody it was his idea." She paused at the door. "He's not a monster, Ms. Brooks. He's just a man who never learned how to hear the word no. I think you might have been the first person who ever said it to him as if they meant it."

She opened the door. The cold air rushed in, along with the sound of the Christmas lights humming across the street.

"Merry Christmas," said Bridget Sloane, and went out into the snow.

\* \* \*
:::

Hannah sat alone in the bakery for a long time, with the folder open in front of her and the kettle going cold beside it.

Then she closed the folder, carried it back to the office, and locked it in the drawer beside Gran's ledger.

When Eli came in through the back door an hour later, stamping snow off his boots and carrying a box of tangerines from the diner, he took one look at her face and set the box down on the table without a word.

"Bridget came," he said. It was not a question.

"She brought the memo," said Hannah. "And the file. Pike's father bought Gran's back debts in 1989. He tried to foreclose on her. Hollis paid them off in cash and told the Pike lawyer to take a running jump."

Eli sat down slowly on the bench beside the door.

"Dad never said," he said.

"He wouldn't have," said Hannah. "He was a Carver. Carvers don't tell people when they've won."

Eli laughed, the sound cracking a little at the edges, and reached for her hand across the table.

"She'd have liked that," he said. "Your grandmother. The way the old man never let anyone have the last word."

"She'd have said he was a stubborn fool."

"And admired him for it," said Eli, and smiled, and did not let go.

# Chapter Thirty-Five: Christmas Eve on Cutter Street

Jess arrived on Christmas Eve with a car full of presents, two sleepy children in matching pajamas, a husband named Dan who carried the turkey as if it were a baby, and an expression on her face that made Hannah suspect she had been crying in the car for most of the drive.

"Don't say anything," said Jess, and walked straight into the bakery and hugged her so hard that Biscuit barked in alarm and ran to hide behind the flour bins.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say something smug."

"I was going to say the turkey's been in the car too long."

"The turkey is fine," said Dan, over the top of a stack of boxes. "The turkey is the only thing in this family that's fine."

The children, Lily and Sam, were already halfway across the shop, pressing their faces to the glass of the display case and shrieking at the gingerbread houses Hannah had built the night before. Eli had carved a tiny wooden sign for each one, shaped like a loaf of bread and painted in gold. Lily had read hers aloud twice and then declared that she was going to live in one when she grew up.

"You can't," said Sam, with the scorn of a seven-year-old who had only just discovered that adults were not always right. "It's made of cookie."

"It's made of gingerbread," said Hannah. "And it's not for living in. It's for eating. But you're welcome to try."

"Auntie Hannah," said Lily solemnly, "can I have the roof?"

"You can have the roof."

"Can I have the dog?"

"The dog isn't for sale."

"The dog is not a gingerbread," said Eli, coming in from the back with a tray of rolls in each hand and a streak of flour on his nose. "The dog is a menace."

"He's our dog," said Hannah.

Eli looked at her across the room. He set the trays down on the counter very carefully, as if he were not sure they would survive the moment, and then he crossed the floor in three long strides and kissed her, in full view of Jess, Dan, two children, and a dog who had come out from behind the flour bins to see what the fuss was about.

"Oh my God," said Jess, from somewhere behind them. "Oh, finally."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

They ate in the back room, at a long table pushed together from three smaller ones, with a checked cloth that Mrs. Ortiz had lent them and a row of candles that Hannah had lit against the gray afternoon light. Mrs. Ortiz came with a casserole and a bottle of sherry and the expression of a woman who had planned this dinner in great detail and was prepared to be disappointed by it. The vicar came for dessert. Tom Kowalski came with Biscuit's red ribbon freshly ironed and a plate of his very own sourdough rolls, which were slightly flattened on one side and which he presented with enormous pride. Everybody ate one. Nobody said a word about the flattened side.

It was loud and warm and a little bit chaotic. Dan told a long, rambling story about a Christmas in 2009 when the power went out and they ate a whole ham by candlelight, and Mrs. Ortiz contradicted every single detail. The vicar sang a verse of something off-key, and Lily stood on her chair and sang it back at him, louder. Sam fell asleep with his head on the table beside his plate, and nobody moved him.

Halfway through the turkey, Jess caught Hannah's eye across the table and tilted her head towards the window, where the lights of Cutter Street were blinking softly in the dark.

"Come outside with me," said Jess. "I need to show you something."

They went out through the back door, into the cold, and stood side by side on the step with their coats pulled tight. The snow had stopped an hour ago, and the sky was clear and deep and crowded with stars. Across the yard, the hardware store's back window glowed the same warm yellow it always did, and a shadow moved inside, carrying a box of something heavy.

"Eli's still working," said Jess.

"He's always working."

"He's a good man, Han."

"I know."

"No, I mean it." Jess turned to look at her, and her face was earnest and a little fierce in the starlight. "When you rang me from Chicago, you sounded like you'd been hit by a bus. You sounded like you'd forgotten what your own name was. And then you called me in October, from this step, and you sounded like somebody had turned on the lights." She swallowed. "I've been waiting eight years for you to sound like that."

Hannah's eyes stung. She looked down at her boots.

"I'm staying," she said. "I told Marian no. I told Pike's people no. I told Mr. Dorsey I'd put my name on the trust, if Eli wants me on it, which he says he does, though he says it like a man asking for a loan. The taxes are paid, mostly. The oven works. The Bread Club has a waiting list of forty, and Mrs. Ortiz has started charging people for the privilege of being on it, which is illegal, probably, but she says it's a community service." She took a shaky breath. "I'm staying, Jess. I'm really, truly staying."

Jess burst into tears.

She did not sob. She simply stood on the back step of the bakery, with her hands over her face and her shoulders shaking, while the cold air curled around her in clouds of white breath. Hannah put her arms around her sister and held on, the way she had when they were small, when Jess had fallen off the swing and split her lip and Hannah had carried her home on her back all the way down Cutter Street, crying harder than she was.

"You idiot," said Jess, muffled against her shoulder. "You absolute, stubborn, glorious idiot. I was going to be so smug."

"You can still be smug."

"I'm going to be unbearable."

"I know."

"I'm going to make you put it on a banner."

"Please don't."

"I'm going to put it on a banner," said Jess, and laughed, wet and furious, into her sister's coat.

\* \* \*
:::

When they came back inside, the room had gone quiet. Dan was sitting in the corner with a glass of sherry, watching the children curled up on the bench with Biscuit's head on Lily's lap. Mrs. Ortiz was dozing over her dessert. The vicar had his eyes closed and was humming to himself.

Eli was standing at the door to the kitchen with a box in his arms, wrapped in brown paper and tied with string. He looked at the two women coming in from the cold, and he looked at Jess's face, and something in his expression softened.

"Everything all right?" he asked quietly.

"Everything's fine," said Hannah. "Better than fine."

He came across the room and set the box on the table. It was heavier than it looked. He pulled the string loose, slowly, and folded back the paper.

Inside was a wooden box, old and scuffed, with a brass latch and a faded label on the lid. Hannah knew the handwriting before she read the words. *For Dorothy. From Hollis. Keep it warm.*

"It was in the back of the hardware store," said Eli. "Under the nail drawers, next to the safe. Dad must have put it there in 1990, after she paid him back. Mr. Dorsey found the receipt for it in the county files. It's been sitting there for thirty-six years, and nobody ever came to collect it."

Hannah lifted the lid.

Inside, nestled in a bed of faded red felt, was a small cast-iron rolling pin, its handle worn smooth by years of use, and a folded piece of card. On the card, in a square, careful hand, was a single line.

*For the bread. For the street. For the girl who'll come back. — H.C.*

Hannah pressed her hand to her mouth.

"He made it," said Eli softly. "The rolling pin. He used to work in metal before he ever ran the store. Gran told me he made it for her the year she bought the bakery, and he said it would outlast the both of them." He smiled, the sort of smile that was half tears. "She gave it back to him when she paid off the loan, I think. He must have kept it, and then he hid it, because he thought the store would be a better place for it than the bakery."

"Because he knew the bakery would be mine someday," said Hannah.

"Because he knew you'd come back," said Eli. "He wrote it on the card. He knew."

She lifted the rolling pin out of its box. It was cold at first, and then, as she held it, warm in her palm, as if it had been waiting all this time to be used.

"I'm going to put it on the shelf," she said, her voice unsteady.

"You're going to use it," said Eli. "Tomorrow morning. On the first loaf of the year."

"Eli, I can't roll out bread with a rolling pin that's older than my mother."

"You can," said Eli, and smiled, and took her hand. "I've seen you do it. You're the toughest woman in Ohio."

From across the room, very softly, Jess said, "Oh, he's going to be insufferable."

"He's already insufferable," said Hannah, and laughed through her tears, and kissed him in front of everyone, while the candles guttered low and the vicar opened his eyes and began, very quietly, to sing.

# Chapter Thirty-Six: A Postcard From Florida

The postcard arrived in the second week of January, in an envelope so thin and creased that Eli nearly missed it among the bills and the seed catalogues.

He found it on the mat inside the hardware store's front door, face down, with his name written across the back in a looping, uneven hand he had not seen in twenty-three years. He stood very still in the cold morning light, holding it between two fingers, and did not turn it over for a long time.

Hannah found him there twenty minutes later, sitting on the bottom step of the stairs with the postcard in his lap and his head bowed.

"Eli?"

He did not look up. He just held the card out to her, and when she took it, she saw that the front was a photograph of a bright blue ocean under a white-hot sun, with a row of palm trees bending in a warm breeze. In the corner, in faded gold letters, were the words *Greetings from Sarasota*.

She turned it over.

The message was short. It had been written in a hurry, the ink smudged in places, the letters sloping heavily to the right.

*Eli. I was sorry then, and I was not brave enough to say it properly. I am trying now. I have been sober for six years and I am living in a small apartment near the water with a cat named Bruce who hates me. I know you don't want to hear from me. I know I don't deserve to be heard. But I wanted you to know that I think about the roof, and the tar, and your father carrying you home over his shoulder. I think about it every day. — Mom.*

Hannah read it twice. Then she sat down on the step beside him and put her hand on his arm.

"Oh, Eli," she said softly.

"She got sober," said Eli, in a voice that was not quite steady. "She says she's been sober for six years. She has a cat called Bruce."

"That sounds like a very good cat."

He laughed, a short, startled sound, and then his shoulders began to shake, and he pressed the back of his hand against his eyes.

"I don't know what to do with it," he said, muffled. "I don't know whether to be angry, or glad, or sick, or just—" He broke off, shaking his head. "I spent my whole life thinking she left because of me. Because I was too loud, or too stubborn, or too much like Dad. And now she says she thinks about the roof."

"She does think about the roof," said Hannah gently. "She just doesn't know how to say the rest of it."

"You don't know that."

"I know what it's like to be the person who ran away," said Hannah, "and to get a letter from someone who loved you anyway, and to be too frightened to read the second page."

He looked at her then, his eyes red-rimmed and bright. "Did you read it?"

"I read it," said Hannah. "I read it on the bus, and I cried for the whole of the journey, and the man next to me gave me a tissue and told me his own mother had left when he was seven. And then I came home and wrote back."

\* \* \*
:::

They sat on the stairs for a long time, with the cold coming up through the floorboards and the sound of Mrs. Ortiz's radio drifting across the road from the diner. Biscuit came and lay across both their feet with a sigh, which Hannah took as an encouraging sign.

"What would your father have said?" asked Hannah eventually.

Eli thought about it, his thumb moving slowly over the edge of the postcard.

"He'd have said she was a terrible judge of nails," he said at last. "And that she'd always been a terrible driver. And that the roof was a disgrace, and it'd been a disgrace since she left, and he was glad she'd noticed." He gave a small, damp laugh. "And then he'd have made me write back. Right there on the step, in the cold, with a pencil. He'd have stood over me like a sergeant and said I wasn't to leave until I'd said something true."

"Then you'd better write something true."

"What if I don't know what's true?"

"Start with what you do know," said Hannah. "You know she left. You know it hurt. You know you spent years trying to make yourself small enough that nobody would have to leave you again. And you know that she came back, in a way, on a postcard with a cat called Bruce." She squeezed his hand. "Start there."

\* \* \*
:::

That afternoon, Eli sat at his father's desk in the back office, with the postcard propped against a jar of pencils and a sheet of the store's cheapest notepaper in front of him. He had written the first line four times. The fifth one, he showed her, and she read it over his shoulder with her hand resting on his back.

*Mom,*

*I got your card. I didn't write back for a long time, because I was angry, and because I didn't want you to see that I still missed you. I did. I missed you every single day until I was twenty-two, and then I stopped letting myself miss you, because it was easier than hoping.*

*I'm glad you're sober. I'm glad you have an apartment near the water and a cat who hates you. I hope Bruce is kind to you when you're not looking.*

*I'm not ready to forgive you yet. I don't know if I ever will. But I've been fixing the roof for twenty years, and I finally understand what Dad was telling me, every spring, when he handed me the tar and told me to stop complaining. He wasn't just fixing the building. He was waiting for somebody to come home and see that it was still standing.*

*If you want to come and see it, you can. Not this year. Maybe next. Bring the cat if you have to. Hannah says he's probably a better judge of character than either of us.*

*Eli.*

Hannah read it twice. When she looked up, Eli was staring at the page, his face very pale and very still.

"Is it all right?" he asked quietly.

"It's perfect," said Hannah. "It's exactly what you meant."

"It's not perfect. It's too long. It's got a joke about a cat."

"It's got a joke about a cat," said Hannah, "and it's from you. That's the only thing she's ever going to need to read."

\* \* \*

He posted it the following morning, on the corner by the bakery, in the same box where Hannah had posted her letter to Chicago. He stood beside it for a long moment, his hand flat against the cold metal, before he finally let the envelope slip through the slot.

"Done," he said.

"Done," said Hannah, and slipped her arm through his.

They walked back down the frosty street together, past the diner and the library and the bench where Walt was sitting with his pigeons, and Mrs. Ortiz waved at them from the window with a dish towels in one hand and a look of enormous, unsubtle satisfaction on her face. At the bakery, the morning's first batch of rye was cooling on the racks, and the faded red sign over the door caught the thin winter sun.

"You know what I'm going to do?" said Eli.

"Tell me."

"I'm going to fix the roof again," he said. "Right now. Before she has a chance to write back, and before I start worrying about whether she's going to like it. I'm going to go up there with a bucket of tar and a bad temper, and I'm going to do it properly. Because that's what he'd want, and because that's what I know how to do."

"And then?"

"And then," said Eli, and looked down at her with a smile that was half tears and half the first real, unguarded hope she had seen in his face since the day she met him, "I'm going to come back down and tell you I'm glad you stayed."

"I'm glad I stayed," said Hannah.

"I know," said Eli. "I could see it in the window."

He kissed her, quickly, on the sidewalk outside the bakery, and then went off across the road with the bucket on one arm and the dog trotting faithfully at his heels, whistling tunelessly, very badly, the way his father used to whistle on the roof.

# Chapter Thirty-Seven: The Name on the Glass

The sign-painter came on the second Tuesday in January, in a van with *Reid & Daughters — Signs, Gilding, Glass* painted in red along both sides, and he climbed up the ladder outside the bakery window at eight o'clock in the morning with a tin of gold leaf, a set of fine brushes, and the expression of a man who had been waiting thirty years for someone to ask him to do something worth doing.

"Old lettering," he said, peering up at the faded words on the glass. "Hand-painted, 1961. Nobody's touched it since. Your grandmother wouldn't let me." He sniffed. "She said she'd rather have a sign that looked tired than one that looked like a lie."

"What's the lie?" asked Hannah, from the sidewalk below.

"*Brooks & Sons*," said Reid. "There were never any sons."

"That's what Gran said. It's the lettering on the inside of the window, from before the sign. She painted it the year she bought the place, and she wouldn't let anyone scrape it off."

"She was right. It's a terrible name." He dipped his brush into the gold leaf and grinned down at her. "But I'll tell you what, miss. It's the best terrible name I've ever painted."

Hannah stood on the cold sidewalk with her hands in her coat pockets and watched him work. The January sun was pale and thin, and the frost still lay in patches along the gutters, and across the road, the hardware store's windows had been freshly washed for the first time in months. In the middle of the window, a small, neat sign had been propped against a bucket of galvanized nails. It read, in Eli's careful capitals: *COMING SOON — DOROTHY'S ROOM. BREAD CLASSES, FRIDAYS, ALL WELCOME.*

Mrs. Ortiz had written the second line. She had also written, in very small letters underneath, *Free for bakers. Don't tell the vicar.*

"Brooks and Carver," said Reid, from the top of the ladder.

Hannah looked up. He had stopped painting. He was staring at the lettering with his head on one side, the tip of his brush hovering just above the glass.

"What did you say?"

"I said *Brooks and Carver*," said Reid, a little embarrassed. "That's what it says on the trust deed. Mr. Dorsey brought the design around on Friday. He wanted it done this week. He said it was a surprise, and I wasn't supposed to tell you." He scratched his jaw with the handle of his brush. "I thought he'd made a mistake, but he said it was supposed to be a joke. Something about two trustees and one dog."

Hannah laughed so suddenly that she had to lean against the lamppost.

"Paint it," she said. "Paint the whole thing. Gold on the cream. Put the dog in if you can."

"The dog's not in the design."

"Then I'll tell him to take it up with the dog."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

Eli was in the back office when she came in, on the phone to the county, with a pencil behind one ear and a stack of papers spread across the desk. He held up one finger without looking around, the way he did when he was concentrating, and Hannah stood in the doorway and watched him.

He had changed since October. She did not think anyone else could see it, not yet, but she could. He stood a little straighter now when people spoke to him. He laughed more, and more easily, and his shoulders no longer hunched when someone raised their voice across a room. He still burned the toast every morning, and he still fixed the walk-in door more often than was strictly necessary. But he had begun, in small, uncertain ways, to ask for help. He had asked Mrs. Ortiz to check his accounts, and Tom Kowalski to look over the wiring, and Mr. Dorsey to sit in on the trust meetings. He had asked her, one night in November, whether she thought he was doing a good job, and when she said yes, he had gone quiet and then said, very low, *Nobody's ever told me that before.*

"Yes," said Eli into the phone. "The trust accepts. No, we don't need the full council to sign. The deed's clear on that. Thank you, Mr. Dorsey. Tell the clerk I said she's very welcome to call me herself next time."

He hung up and swiveled around in his chair and looked at her, and she saw that he was trying not to smile.

"Brooks and Carver," said Hannah.

He went red. "You saw it."

"Reid told me. He was very confused. He thought it was a mistake."

"It's not a mistake." Eli got up and came around the desk and took both her hands, very seriously, as if he were about to propose a business merger. "Mr. Dorsey says it's the only way to make the trust properly symbolic. Two trustees. Two names. One sign. And the dog gets a name on the deed too, which he says is an honors in the county and he's not going to argue about it."

"The dog is not a trustee."

"The dog has been very patient," said Eli. "The dog deserves recognition."

She laughed, and he laughed with her, and then he stopped laughing and simply looked at her, with his thumbs moving slowly over the backs of her hands.

"I signed the papers this morning," he said. "The trust is formally reconstituted. I'm the living trustee, and you're the appointed successor. Dorothy's wish was that the seat go to the family that kept the bakery open, and Mr. Dorsey says the wording is clear enough that nobody's going to argue. The council has a copy. Pike's lawyers have a copy. Caroline Wu sent me a very polite note asking whether we'd like to discuss the river land, and I said no."

"You said no?"

"I said no," said Eli, "and then I said I'd think about it, and then I went and bought you a coffee. Terrible coffee. From the diner."

"Eli, that's not very romantic."

"It's the most romantic thing I've ever done," said Eli. "I went to the diner and asked for a cup of coffee that tastes like it's been boiled in a bucket, and Mrs. Ortiz said I was a fool, and I said I knew, and then I came back here and asked you whether you'd like to be a trustee on a building you're never going to sell."

Hannah stared at him.

"You're asking me to be a trustee," she said.

"I'm asking you to be the trustee," said Eli. "And then I'm going to ask you something else, but I'd like to do it properly, in the right order, with the sign up and the dog watching and no one shouting in the street."

"You can't just say that and leave me hanging."

"I can say it," said Eli, "because you're not going anywhere."

She put her arms around his neck and kissed him, hard and fast, in the doorway of the back office with the window full of gold leaf and the sound of Reid's brushes tapping lightly against the glass outside.

"Yes," she said against his mouth. "The answer's yes. Ask me the other thing."

"I haven't asked you yet."

"Then ask me. I'm not going to wait till spring."

He laughed, helpless and delighted, and pressed his forehead to hers.

"Marry me?" he said. "Not in a hurry. Not on a deadline. Just because you're the most stubborn, impossible, brilliant woman I've ever met, and I'd like to keep you warm on purpose for the rest of my life."

"That's the worst proposal I've ever heard."

"It's the best one I've ever made," said Eli. "Say yes anyway."

"Yes," said Hannah, and somewhere outside, Reid's ladder creaked, and a dog barked, and a voice from the top of the ladder said, quite loudly, "Oh, thank God, can I finish the sign now?"

By noon, the sign was done.

It stood over the bakery door in gold leaf on cream, the letters tall and slightly uneven, with the paint catching the thin January light. *BROOKS & CARVER*, it read, in old-fashioned serif capitals, and beneath it, in smaller letters, *Est. 1961. Bread for Cutter Street.*

Mrs. Ortiz came out of the diner with her arms full of cinnamon rolls and stood on the sidewalk looking up at it for a long time, with her glasses pushed up on her forehead and her mouth working silently.

"It's perfect," she said finally, in a voice that wobbled. "Your grandmother would have hated it."

"She'd have loved it," said Hannah.

"She'd have hated it for about five minutes and then made everyone eat a roll," said Mrs. Ortiz, and handed her one.

Biscuit came out from behind the counter, looked up at the sign, sneezed once, and sat down on the step with the air of a dog who had personally approved every letter.

Eli came out of the back door, wiping his hands on a rag, and stopped beside her, and looked up.

"Well," he said quietly. "That's the name, then."

"That's the name," said Hannah, and took his hand, and did not let go.

# Chapter Thirty-Eight: A Hammer and a Thumb

The first Friday of April, Dorothy's Room opened at the back of the hardware store, and by nine o'clock in the morning there were twenty-two people crowded into a space meant for six.

They had come with flour on their sleeves and skeptical looks on their faces. There were schoolchildren from the elementary school across the river, herded by a harassed teacher who had promised them cinnamon rolls if they behaved. There were two retired nurses who wanted to learn how to make rye. There was a man from the county planning office who said he had come to check the fire exits and stayed to knead a loaf of soda bread so badly that Mrs. Ortiz took it away from him and fed it to Biscuit. There was the vicar, who had brought his own apron, and Tom Kowalski, who had brought his sourdough starter in a jar and insisted on introducing it to everybody.

"This is Mabel the third," he said, holding the jar up to the light like a proud father. "She's two months old and she's very temperamental. Be gentle with her."

"That's the same Mabel," said Hannah, from the front of the room. "Mabel the second died in February. We had a funeral. There was cake."

"This is a different Mabel," said Tom, unrepentant. "She has her own personality."

The room laughed. Hannah laughed with it. She stood at the front, with her sleeves rolled up and her hair tied back in a red bandana, and watched the faces turned towards her, eager and hopeful and a little bit wary, and she felt the old familiar fear rise in her chest, the one that said *you are a fraud, you are going to be found out, you are not as good as she was.*

Then she looked at the back wall, where the old black oven sat cleaned and polished in the corner, its brick chimney running up through the ceiling, and she saw Eli standing in front of it with his arms folded and his shoulders loose and a look on his face that said, very clearly, *You've got this.*

She took a breath.

"My grandmother taught me to bake when I was six," she said. "She stood me on a step stool, put flour in my hair, and told me that bread was just water, flour, salt, and time. Nobody can hurry the time. That's the hardest part." She picked up the bowl in front of her and held it out. "So we're going to start with the time. Everybody come up and look at the dough. Tell me what you see."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

They made soda bread, rye, and a single, enormous sourdough loaf that the planning officer shaped with great seriousness and then sat on by accident. By eleven, the room smelled of warm bread and wood smoke and sugar, and the schoolchildren had flour in their hair and their mouths, and the retired nurses had declared rye to be the most satisfying thing they had done in years.

At noon, Hannah took the old rolling pin from its box on the shelf. It was cast iron, scuffed and worn, its handle polished pale by three generations of hands. The children gathered around as she rolled out a sheet of dough, slowly and carefully, the way Gran had taught her.

"Who made that?" asked a girl with a missing front tooth.

"A man named Hollis Carver," said Hannah. "He was a hardware man. He made this for my grandmother the year she bought the bakery, and she used it every morning for thirty years. Then she gave it back to him, and he kept it for the rest of his life."

"Is it magic?"

"No," said Hannah. "It's just heavy. And it's old. And it's been waiting for someone to use it."

The girl considered this. Then she reached out, very carefully, and touched the handle with one finger.

"It's warm," she said, astonished.

"It's been on the shelf by the oven," said Hannah. "Everything's warm in here."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

Eli was waiting for her in the back of the store when the class ended, sitting on an upturned crate beside the open safe, with a cardboard box at his feet and a look on his face she had never seen before.

"What's that?" said Hannah.

"A hammer," said Eli.

She looked at the box. It was old, and dusty, and it had a red label on the side that read *Carver & Son — Est. 1978*. Inside, nested in a bed of straw, was a claw hammer with a worn wooden handle and a head that had been polished bright by years of use.

"It was his," said Eli. "My father's. He bought it the year he took over the store. He used it to fix every shelf and every drawer and every door on this street, including the front of your grandmother's oven. He kept it in the safe, under the letter, because he said it was the most valuable thing he owned."

Hannah lifted it out of the straw. It was heavier than it looked. The grip fitted her hand as if it had been made for her.

"He gave it to me," said Eli quietly, "in his letter. He said he wanted me to give it to the person who came back. He said she'd know what to do with it."

"What did he say about me?"

"He said you hit your thumb twice when you were eight and didn't cry either time." Eli's voice was thick. "He said you'd be the toughest woman in Ohio. I told you that already, and you said you cried later, in the car."

"I did cry later," said Hannah, staring at the hammer. "I cried for an hour. Gran made me sit in the back of the shop with a bag of frozen peas on my thumb, and I refused to speak to anyone, and I cried until I fell asleep on a sack of cornmeal."

"Then I think he'd have been proud of that too," said Eli.

She put the hammer down on the crate between them, very carefully, and looked at him.

"You're going to propose again," she said.

"I'm not going to propose again. I'm going to ask you something I should have asked in January, when we had the sign up and the dog watching, and I was too scared to say it properly." He took her hand. "I'm asking you to be my partner, in the building and out of it. Not because of the building, though I'll never stop being grateful for it. I'm asking because you're the only person I've ever met who looked at the worst thing my father ever did and said *that's not the end of the story*. And because I want the two of us to be the ones who finish it."

"You already asked me to marry you," said Hannah.

"I did. You said yes. You said it on a Tuesday, in a doorway, with the sign painter on a ladder. I'm not sure that counts."

"It counts."

"Good." He smiled, and his eyes were bright. "Then I'm asking you again. Properly this time. On the crate. With the hammer."

She laughed, helplessly, and pulled him to his feet, and kissed him with the taste of rye bread still on her lips and flour on the front of her apron. Somewhere behind them, the old oven ticked as it cooled, and the sound of children's voices drifted in from the front of the store, where the schoolteacher was calling a head count.

"Yes," said Hannah. "I'll be your partner. I'll be your wife, too, if you're going to be this insistent about it. And if you ever burn the toast again, I'm going to make you eat the crust with the hammer."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me."

"It's the truth," said Hannah, and kissed him again.

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

That evening, when the last of the children had gone and the room was quiet, Hannah sat alone at the back of the store with Mabel the third in her lap, a jar of starter balanced carefully between her knees, while Biscuit snored in the corner beneath the oven.

She had forgotten how quiet it could be, in the back of a hardware store at night. The air smelled of oil and wood and warm, yeasty bread. The safe stood open against the far wall, its brass dial catching the light from the lamp. And on the crate beside her, the old hammer lay in its box, its head bright, its handle smooth and familiar under her fingers.

Eli came in from the front, carrying two mugs of coffee so bad it was almost a comfort.

"Terrible," he said, handing her one.

"Terrible," agreed Hannah, and drank it anyway.

He sat down beside her on the crate, and put his arm around her shoulders, and for a while they simply watched the lamp flicker and listened to the dog breathe.

"Do you ever miss it?" he asked eventually. "Chicago. The city. The person you were there."

Hannah considered this. The question was a fair one, and she had promised herself she would always answer him honestly.

"Sometimes," she said. "I miss the person who never had to be scared, because she never let herself want anything. But I don't miss the way she lived. And I don't miss the way she ate cereal over the sink." She leaned her head against his shoulder. "I think I like this one better. She's got flour in her hair and a dog who hates her and a man who can't make coffee to save his life. She's a lot more trouble. But she knows what she's for."

"And what's she for?"

"She's for staying," said Hannah. "Right here. On Cutter Street, where the bread's warm and the oven's temperamental and the trust deed is hanging on the wall. She's for finishing what Gran started, and what your father started, and what I was too scared to start on my own." She smiled into his coat. "And she's for you."

Eli was quiet for a long moment. Then he bent his head and pressed his lips to her hair.

"That's the best thing anyone's ever told me," he said.

"It's the only thing I've ever wanted to tell you," said Hannah.

Outside, the first spring rain began to fall on Cutter Street, soft and steady, pattering on the gold letters of the sign and rinsing the glass of the bakery window clean. Across the road, the lamp in the hardware store's back room burned on, the same warm yellow it always had, and the dog sighed in his sleep, and the bread in the oven breathed softly under its cloth, waiting for morning.

# Chapter Thirty-Nine: First Snow

The first snow of the new winter came on the second Saturday in November, quietly, in the small hours, while Cutter Street was still asleep.

Hannah woke to the particular silence that only snow could make, a soft, muffled hush that seemed to press in against the windows and make the whole town feel very far away. She lay still for a moment under the quilt, listening, and felt Eli's arm tighten around her waist in his sleep.

"It's snowing," she whispered.

"Mm," said Eli, not opening his eyes. "Don't get up. It's Saturday."

"It's five o'clock on a Saturday. The bread doesn't know it's Saturday."

"The bread can wait. The bread has been waiting for you since 1961." He yawned hugely and pulled her closer. "Just five more minutes. I'll make the coffee. It'll be terrible, but it'll be warm."

She laughed softly and let herself sink back into the warmth of the bed. Downstairs, Biscuit gave a sleepy, indignant woof, as if he had been personally insulted by the very idea of being left out of the conversation, and a moment later, his claws clicked across the floorboards and he flopped down heavily against the bedroom door with a groan.

"Even the dog is awake," said Hannah.

"The dog," said Eli, "is a menace."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

By six o'clock, the bakery was alive with light and heat and the rich, comforting smell of bread. The oven had been burning since four, its old iron door ticking as it warmed, and the racks along the back wall were already filling with golden loaves. Mabel the third, who had become, in the eyes of the town, a minor celebrity, sat in her jar on the windowsill, bubbling contentedly in the warmth from the glass.

Hannah stood at the counter in her flour-dusted apron, shaping rolls by hand with the cast-iron rolling pin beside her, and watched the snow drift softly past the window. The street outside was white and silent. The lamps glowed through the falling flakes. The gold letters of the sign above the door had gathered a thin, glittering line of frost along their tops.

*BROOKS & CARVER*, it read, *Est. 1961*.

Across the road, the hardware store's lights came on, one by one, and then the back door opened and Eli came out into the snow, carrying a bag of rock salt and a shovel over one shoulder. He stopped on the step and looked up at the bakery, and when he saw her in the window, he lifted one hand in a small, unselfconscious wave, as if the whole street were his to greet.

Hannah waved back, and for a moment, she could not see him clearly for the warm, bright blur in her eyes.

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

The line for the Winter Harvest opened at eight, and by nine it stretched past the church and around the corner onto Mill Lane. The Bread Club members had come first, of course, with their reusable bags and their monthly newsletters and their hopeful, slightly anxious faces, eager to see whether the cinnamon roll shaped like a dog had survived its move to the new season. Mrs. Ortiz had stationed herself at the front of the line with a clipboard and a fierce expression, and was handing out tickets to anyone who looked like they might try to cut in line.

"You can have a ticket," she said, to a man in a flat cap. "You cannot have two. Don't argue. I'm too old to argue, and you're too slow to win."

The vicar's wife ran the cocoa stand on the corner, with a new red scarf and an enormous urn. Tom Kowalski had built a small wooden stage on the church steps, from which the high school brass band, now fifteen strong and considerably more in tune than they had been two years ago, was playing something cheerful and only slightly ragged. The children ran between the stalls in their mismatched mittens, their cheeks already red with cold, and the strings of white lights above the street flickered on as the afternoon began to darken.

Jess arrived at ten, with a car full of cousins, a box of Christmas cookies from her own kitchen, and a banner that read, in glittering purple letters, *I TOLD YOU SO*. She hung it across the front of the bakery window herself, standing on a stepladder in the snow, and when Hannah protested, she simply pointed at the crowd below and said, "They like it. Look at them."

They did. Half the town was pointing at the banner and laughing, and Mrs. Ortiz had taken a photograph of it with her phone and sent it to the regional paper with the caption *Cutter Street Stays Put*.

"You're going to have to stop doing that," said Hannah, climbing down from the ladder beside her sister.

"I'm not going to stop doing anything," said Jess cheerfully. "I'm your sister. It's my job."

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

By the afternoon, the bakery had sold out of everything except the last tray of mince pies, which Hannah had set aside for a particular customer. She was waiting for him at the back door, in the warm, bright kitchen, with the snow still falling softly beyond the glass.

Eli came in, stamping the snow from his boots, with a red wool scarf wound twice around his neck and two cups of coffee in his hands.

"Terrible," he said, setting one in front of her.

"I know," said Hannah. "I'm getting used to it."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said."

"It's true."

He sat down on the stool beside her and put his arm around her shoulders, and she leaned into him, tired and warm and happier than she had ever been in her life. Biscuit, who had been lying under the table with his head on Eli's boot, lifted his head, looked at them both, and sighed the long, deep, contented sigh of a dog who had finally been proved right about everything.

"Hannah," said Eli quietly. "There's something in the post."

She looked up. He was holding an envelope, cream-color and dusty, with a postmark she did not recognize and an address written across the front in a tight, careful hand that she knew, at once, belonged to her grandmother.

*Hannah Brooks, The Bakery, Cutter Street — To be opened when she's home.*

Her heart stopped.

"Where did this come from?" she whispered.

"It was in the flour bin," said Eli, his voice very low. "Under the empty sacks. Mrs. Ortiz found it when she was looking for a clean tray. She said it had been wedged in behind the rye bag for years. She thought it might be from Dorothy." He hesitated. "I didn't open it. I thought you should."

Hannah took the envelope from his hand. It was thin, and light, and it rustled softly as she turned it over. The flap had been sealed with a dab of red wax, stamped with the letter *D*.

She broke the seal with her thumb, and drew out a single sheet of paper, folded in half.

*My darling girl,* it began, in the same looping, careful hand that had filled the ledgers and the recipe cards. *If you are reading this, then you came home. I knew you would. Eli will have made you a terrible cup of coffee and you will have decided to forgive him. Good.*

*I have one more thing to tell you, and you must not tell Eli until he has fixed the boiler, or he will want to fix it first. There is a second building. Mill House, on Ashby Road, across the river, the old cider press with the blue door. Your grandfather bought it in 1958 with money he never told anyone about, and he left it to me, and I never told a soul. It has been empty for thirty years. It needs a roof, a new floor, and a great deal of nerve.*

*The key is taped inside the flour bin. Don't tell Garrett Pike. He will want it. He will want it very badly, and he will be very polite about it.*

*I always knew you'd be the one to come back. I just wanted you to be brave enough to stay. Be brave, Hannah. Bake something wonderful. And for heaven's sake, let Eli carry the flour.*

*All my love, always,*

*Gran*

Hannah read the letter twice. Then she folded it carefully and put it back in the envelope and held it against her chest, very tightly, as if she could feel the old woman's heartbeat through the paper.

"Hannah?" said Eli. "What is it?"

She laughed, shakily, and wiped her eyes on the back of her wrist.

"There's a second building," she said.

Eli stared at her.

"Across the river," said Hannah. "On Ashby Road. The old cider press, with the blue door. Gran left it to me. And there's a key taped inside the flour bin, and she says if I tell you before the boiler's fixed, you'll want to fix the boiler first, and she says not to tell Garrett Pike, because he'll be very polite about it."

Eli was silent for a long moment. Then he put his face in his hands and began, helplessly, to laugh.

"Of course there is," he said, muffled. "Of course there's a second building. Of course she didn't tell me. Of course it needs a roof."

"It needs a roof, a floor, and a great deal of nerve."

"We'll start with the roof," said Eli, lifting his head, his eyes wet and bright and utterly delighted. "And you can tell me all about the blue door. And then, when the boiler's finally working, we'll go and look at it together, and I'll tell you everything that's wrong with it, and you'll tell me it's perfect."

"It's going to be perfect," said Hannah.

"It's going to need a roof," said Eli, and kissed her, slowly, in the warm bright kitchen, while the snow fell past the window and the bread breathed softly in the oven, and the dog, who had been waiting all his life for someone to remember he was there, thumped his tail twice against the floor and went back to sleep.

::: {.scenebreak}
\* \* \*
:::

That night, after the last of the crowds had drifted home and the lights along Cutter Street had dimmed to a soft, wintry gold, Hannah stood alone at the bakery window and looked out at the snow.

The street was quiet. The hardware store's back room glowed warm across the road, and somewhere on the corner, the church bell rang once, very softly, for midnight. The sign over the door caught the lamplight and held it, the gold letters steady and bright.

*Brooks & Carver*.

She thought of her grandmother, standing at this window on a winter morning sixty years ago, with flour on her cheek and a rolling pin in her hand, humming badly, waiting for the frost to melt.

She thought of Hollis Carver, writing a card for a rolling pin he had made with his own hands, sure that one day someone would come home to use it.

She thought of Eli, kneeling on the floor of a hardware store with a safe open beside him, and the letter that had taken him years to read.

And she thought, with a small, warm, startled delight, that there was a blue door across the river, and a roof that needed mending, and a building that had been waiting all this time to be saved.

"Hannah?" called Eli softly, from the kitchen. "Come and see. The dough's breathing."

"It's always breathing," said Hannah, smiling at the snow.

"It's breathing like it's been waiting for you," said Eli, from the doorway, his hair damp from the cold and a smudge of flour on his cheek. "Like it knows you're finally home."

She turned from the window and crossed the room to him, and took his hand, and went with him into the warm, bright kitchen, where the bread was rising beneath its cloth, and the dog was asleep by the oven, and the first snow of the winter lay soft and silent over Cutter Street, as if the whole town had been holding its breath, and had only now, at last, let it out.
