## Chapter 1: Two Trucks on Birch Street

The sofa was already on the lawn when Tessa Marsh turned onto Birch Street, and it was cream, and it was exactly the sofa she had driven six hours from Chicago to put in a cottage on the lake.

She braked too hard. The rental truck shuddered and settled, and the dog in the passenger seat lifted his gray muzzle from the armrest and gave her a look of mild reproach. Moose was nine years old, a golden retriever who had long ago stopped expecting anything exciting from a morning. He had belonged to her uncle. Now he belonged to her, along with the truck, the barn, the business, and a stack of paperwork that she had not yet finished reading.

"I know," she told him. "I see it too."

The cottage at number fourteen was a gray-shingled place with white trim and a deep front porch, set back under two old birches that gave the street its name. The lawn ran down to a row of cedars and then to the lake, which was a flat, bright blue in the early light of the last Tuesday in May. Someone had already dragged a dining table onto the porch. Someone else had left a stack of throw pillows on the steps, still in their plastic. And in the middle of the grass, facing the water as if it had been placed there for the view, sat a cream sofa with a curved back and carved walnut feet.

Tessa had one just like it in the back of her truck.

She got out. Moose followed, slower, sniffing the air as though it had been left out for him.

A man was standing at the open rear door of a white pickup parked on the gravel behind the sofa. He had his back to her, and he was tugging at a lamp in a cardboard box with the patient concentration of someone who was not going to be hurried by anybody. He wore a gray button-down with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, khaki pants, and a pair of work boots that had seen some years. His hair was dark and too long for a realtor, and when he turned around, she saw that he had a face that looked as if it was used to being mildly amused.

"Morning," he said. "You're the other one."

"I'm sorry?"

"The other truck." He tipped his head toward her rental, which was parked half on the lawn with its hazards still blinking. "Ray Pruitt said there might be two. He didn't say they'd both show up at six thirty."

"It's seven fifteen," Tessa said.

"I was up early." He set the lamp down on the porch steps, very carefully, and wiped his palms on his pants. "I'm Owen Reyes. I'm the listing agent for the Pruitt estate. I've been trying to get this house on the market since April, and until about ten minutes ago I thought the staging was handled."

"The staging was handled," Tessa said. "By my uncle. Marsh Home Staging. He signed the contract with Nora Pruitt in January, before he got sick." She heard her voice go flat and tried to pull it back. "He died in February. I'm Tessa Marsh. The contract came to me."

Owen did not say anything for a moment. Behind him the lake lapped at the bottom of the lawn, and somewhere up the street a screen door banged shut.

"I'm sorry about Len," he said at last. "He was a good man. He taught me how to tell a fake Stickley from a real one, and he never once charged me for the lesson."

"That sounds like him."

"Is that the same sofa?" Owen asked.

Tessa looked at the sofa on the grass. Then she looked at the sofa in the back of her truck, which she could see through the open roll door, its cream upholstery wrapped in gray moving blankets and its walnut feet poking out at the corners like the legs of a sleeping animal.

"Yes," she said slowly. "It's one of a pair. Len bought them in 2014, from an estate sale in Milwaukee. He never liked to split a matched set."

"Then he had two," Owen said, "and somebody took one out of the barn without asking the other person."

"I didn't take anything from the barn. I've been in Chicago for six weeks. I drove up last night and slept in the truck in the Larkspur Lake Motel lot, because I didn't want to wake anyone at four in the morning." She crossed her arms. "The barn is locked. I have the only key."

"Not the only key," Owen said quietly. "Len gave me one in 2023. For the storage unit on Route 9, the one with the tags on the lamps. I've been using it for two years, and I always put things back." He reached into his pocket, pulled out a ring with two keys and a plastic tag, and held it up so she could see the faded handwriting. Unit 9. "I didn't take a sofa from the barn this morning. I took a sofa from the storage unit, which is mine too, in a way I'm still trying to explain to you."

Moose wandered over to the sofa on the lawn, sniffed one carved foot, and sat down beside it with a heavy sigh, as if he had made a decision and would not be moving again. Tessa thought that was probably the most sensible thing anyone had done since she arrived.

"In a way," she repeated.

"Yes." Owen looked at the cottage, then back at her. Something in his expression shifted from amusement to a wariness that seemed closer to respect. "Tessa, I don't want to fight with you on a Tuesday morning in front of the Halvorsens."

She followed his glance across the yard. In the house next door, a curtain had moved. A woman in a pale blue housecoat stood at the kitchen window with a coffee mug in both hands, watching them with the frank interest of someone who had been waiting all her life for a morning like this.

"Who is that?" Tessa asked.

"Bev Halvorsen. Retired school librarian. Number twelve. She'll have a pie in the oven by noon, and she'll know by lunch that you were in the drive with a rental truck and a dog who won't move." Owen's mouth twitched. "She's also the only person in Larkspur Lake who knows exactly what Len paid for every single lamp in the Birch Street house, because she's the one who told him the price was too high."

Tessa looked at the sofa, the lamp on the steps, the stack of pillows still in their plastic. Then she looked at the cottage, with its wide porch and its gray shingles and its view of the water, the kind of house that buyers from Chicago fell for before they reached the front walk. Len had always said that a staged house sold itself. He had said it in the back of the barn with his reading glasses on his nose and a pencil behind one ear, and she had never once argued, because she had never once seen him wrong about a room.

"Two sofas," she said. "A pair, from the same estate. Both of them in a house with one living room."

"Yes."

"And a listing agent who has been using my uncle's props for two years without telling me."

"I told you, I put them back."

"You were using them," Tessa said. "That's not the same as putting them back."

Owen met her eyes and held them. "No," he agreed. "It isn't."

The screen door at number twelve banged again. Bev Halvorsen had come out onto her porch, a mug in one hand and a dish towel over her shoulder, and she was walking across the lawn toward them with the purposeful pace of a woman who had decided to intervene before anyone else could make the situation worse.

"Oh," Bev said, stopping at the edge of the grass. "I see the two of you have found the sofa."

"We have," Tessa said.

"Good. Then you can stop standing in my neighbor's yard and talk like adults over a cup of coffee at Gus's. Dock Street Diner opens at seven, and Gus has been waiting all spring for someone to argue with." She turned and looked at Tessa with bright, assessing eyes. "You're Len's girl. You have his jaw. Did he ever tell you I was the one who said the Birch Street lamps were overpriced?"

"He mentioned it," Tessa said. It was a lie, but not a big one.

"He never forgave me," Bev said cheerfully. "Best friend a woman ever had. Go on, Owen. Bring the dog. He's been sitting on that sofa like he's been hired."

Moose, who had been listening, thumped his tail twice against the cream upholstery and did not get up.

Owen laughed for the first time that morning. It was low and unguarded, and it changed his whole face, and Tessa was annoyed to notice it.

"Bev," he said, "I think the dog has made his choice."

"Then the sofa stays," Bev said. "And somebody had better decide who gets the other one before the Pruitts come by at noon and see two of them sitting in the yard like a pair of bookends."

Tessa looked at the sofa on the grass, the sofa in her truck, the man with the lamp and the dog with the sofa. She felt the familiar tightening in her chest that came whenever a plan went sideways. She had come to Larkspur Lake to close out a business, sell an inventory, sign the papers, and go home. She had not come to split a house with a stranger who had a key to her uncle's storage unit and a laugh that made her want to argue harder.

"Hold on. I need ten minutes," she said. "I need to check the contract. And I need coffee."

"Gus's," Bev said. "Ten minutes. Bring the sofa if you like. It's the only thing in this yard that seems to know what it's doing."

Owen picked up the lamp from the steps and tucked it under one arm. He hesitated, then held out his other hand, palm up, the way a man might offer a truce to a dog that had not yet decided whether to bite.

"Truce?" he said.

Tessa looked at the hand. She thought about the storage unit, the faded tag on the keys, the way her uncle had written Unit 9 in pencil on a piece of masking tape and stuck it to the inside of his truck door for a decade.

"Not yet," she said. "Show me the contract first."

He nodded as though he had expected nothing else, and he went back to the lamp in his arms, and Tessa went back to her own truck, where the second sofa sat wrapped in its gray blankets, waiting for someone to decide what it was for.

## Chapter 2: The Cream Sofa

Gus Pelletier had painted the Dock Street Diner the color of lemon custard the year his wife left him, and he had never painted over it, because he said the color reminded him that he had survived. He poured Tessa a cup of coffee without asking, set down a plate of cinnamon toast beside it, and looked at Owen with the open suspicion of a man who had seen this kind of morning before.

"Two sofas," Gus said.

"One sofa," Owen said. "The other one is in Tessa's truck."

"Same sofa?"

"Same sofa," Tessa said.

Gus wiped the counter in a slow circle and considered this for a long moment. "Len bought them as a pair," he said finally. "I drove him to the estate sale in Milwaukee in my Ford. Eleven hours round trip, and he talked the whole way about walnut and how the wood moves with the seasons." He shook his head. "He paid too much for both of them. He always paid too much for the good things, and he always got the bargains on the junk nobody else wanted."

"That sounds like him," Tessa said. She wrapped both hands around the mug and let the heat seep into her palms. The diner smelled of bacon and burnt sugar. Through the front window she could see the public pier at the foot of the street, the bait shop beside it, and a dozen small sailboats leaning gently against their moorings in the flat blue water.

Owen sat on the stool beside her and did not touch his coffee. He had put the lamp in the back of his pickup, and his hands looked restless without it.

"Here's what I know," he said. "Ray and Nora Pruitt inherited number fourteen from their aunt, Ada. They want it sold before Labor Day, and they want it to look like the kind of house that makes a Chicago family cry at the front door. Len had the staging contract in January. Nora signed it, and he got a deposit of four thousand dollars, and then he got sick. The Pruitts called me in April because they needed a listing agent who knew the lake, and I told them I'd list it if they kept the staging with Len."

"And then he died."

"And then he died." Owen turned his cup a quarter turn on its saucer. "I called the number on his website in March, and a woman named Connie answered and said she was the office manager, which I didn't know he had. She told me to call back when things settled. So I waited. And in May I got a text from Nora asking whether the sofa was still going into the house, and I went to Unit 9 and took it out, because I assumed that was what Len would have wanted and nobody else was going to do it."

"Connie is my cousin," Tessa said. "She's in Chicago, and she's not an office manager. She's a bookkeeper who answers the phone when she's bored." She put down the mug. "I didn't know there was a storage unit on Route 9."

"Pinecrest Storage. Unit 9 is the last one on the north row. The door has a red paint mark, and the lock is older than both of us." Owen looked at her directly. "Len rented it in 2016, when the barn got too full. He kept the good pieces there, the things he didn't want to leave in the drafty part of the building. Tables, lamps, a Stickley desk, a painting of a sailboat that I still think is the ugliest thing I've ever seen."

"It's Len's favorite," Tessa said, without meaning to.

Owen laughed, quietly. "I know."

Gus came back with the coffee pot and filled both cups without comment, then leaned on the counter with his forearms crossed and watched them the way a referee watches a boxing match that might go either way. "So what are you two going to do about the sofa?" he said.

"We're going to settle it," Tessa said.

"How?"

"By talking about it like adults," she said. "Which is what Bev told us to do."

"Bev has been wrong about things before," Gus said. "She once told me my pie crust was too wet, and then she ate the whole thing."

Owen smiled into his cup. Tessa did not smile, but it was a near thing.

She opened the folder she had brought in from the truck and laid the contract out on the counter between them. It was a single page, a little worn at the corners, with her uncle's handwriting in the margins. Len had written the room names in block capitals. LIVING, KITCHEN, PRIMARY BED, DOCK ROOM. Under DOCK ROOM he had written, in pencil, "use the blue quilt, not the gray, the gray looks like rain."

"He wrote notes on everything," Owen said softly.

"He wrote notes on the back of his grocery lists. I used to find them in the freezer." She ran her finger down the page. The Birch Street job was listed as a full staging for a three-bedroom cottage, with a fee of $6,800, a deposit of $4,000 already paid, and a balance due at the open house. There was a second line in a different ink, added later. Co-listed with O. Reyes, Kestrel Realty. Furniture from storage as needed.

"As needed," she read aloud. "That's you."

"That's me."

"So you're in the contract."

"I'm in the contract as the listing agent. I'm not a stager." He put a finger on the words. "But I've been helping Len move pieces in and out for two years. I did the install at the Ostrander house in April, and the one on Cedar Point Road, and the one at the marina condo last fall. I'm the guy who carries the couch, Tessa. That's what I've been doing. I've been carrying the couch."

"And taking props out of a locked unit on Route 9 without telling me."

"I told Len," Owen said. "He knew. He said it was fine."

Tessa stared at him. "You're telling me my uncle gave you the key and let you use his inventory, and never once thought to mention it to me."

"He mentioned it to me," Owen said carefully, "every time I brought something back. He would say, 'Owen, you're a good kid, and you're going to be the death of my dining room set.' And then he would give me a cup of coffee."

Tessa looked away. She looked at the window, the pier, the sailboats, the water. She thought about her uncle standing in the barn in his canvas coat, sorting through a box of brass hardware and humming an old song she did not know. She thought about the sound of his truck coming down her parents' driveway in Naperville every summer when she was a girl, and the way he always leaned out the window to say something to her mother that made her laugh. She had not known her uncle well, not as an adult. She had visited Larkspur Lake for two weeks each summer until she was fifteen, and then she had gone to Chicago for school, and then she had gone to work, and somewhere in all of that she had let him become a voice on the phone on Sunday nights.

"What happened to your coffee?" Owen asked.

"It's cold."

"Gus, could we get a warm one for the lady?"

"Sure thing," Gus said, and took her cup away without looking at her, which she appreciated more than she could have explained.

"Here's my proposal," Owen said. "We split the house. I'm the listing agent, so I handle the photos, the open house, and the showings. You handle the staging, the install, and whatever Len's notes say about the blue quilt. We both get our names on the sign. The Pruitts pay us both. The sofa stays in the living room, the second one goes to the dock room, and we don't speak about the lamps until Friday."

"The lamps."

"There are eleven lamps in that house," Owen said. "And every one of them is going to be an argument."

"You don't know that."

"I've been in that house," he said. "The dock room has a brass floor lamp with a green glass shade that Len thought was the best thing in the county, and I think it looks like something a dentist would own. I'll tell you now, Tessa, I'm going to fight you on it."

She picked up her new coffee, which was hot now, and tasted it, and found that she had been about to laugh. She did not let herself do it. Instead she closed the folder and slid the contract back into its sleeve.

"The brass lamp stays," she said.

"Ha."

"It stays in the dock room. It has the green shade. It goes in the window where the light comes off the water at four in the afternoon. I've seen the photos Len took of that room, and it is the only lamp in that house that the light actually likes."

Owen stared at her for a moment, then slowly picked up his own cup and took a sip. "You're going to be hard to work with," he said.

"I've been told."

"By whom?"

"By everybody in my life," Tessa said. "Including my uncle, in writing, on the back of a grocery list."

He grinned then, fully, and she caught herself grinning back, and for one awkward second neither of them spoke.

From the door of the kitchen, Gus cleared his throat loudly. "I'm going to need that booth back by nine," he said. "Some of us have pie to serve."

"We'll go," Tessa said, and gathered the folder.

Out front, the morning had grown warm. The sofa was still on the lawn at Birch Street, Moose was still lying beside it with his nose on his paws, and across the street a pickup truck with a Kestrel Realty decal on the door had pulled up behind Owen's, its driver watching them with open curiosity.

"Who's that?" Tessa asked.

"Ray Pruitt," Owen said. "He's early, too."

Ray walked up the drive in a golf shirt and pressed slacks, a leather folder in one hand and a phone in the other. He looked at the sofa on the lawn, then at the second sofa in the back of Tessa's truck, then at the two of them standing side by side in the gravel with their coffee cups still in their hands.

"Oh," Ray said. "This is awkward."

"It's a sofa," Tessa said.

"It's two sofas," Ray said, "and my sister is on the phone from Phoenix, and she wants to know whether her aunt's house is going to look like a catalog or a crime scene."

Owen set his cup down on the hood of Tessa's rental.

"A catalog," he said. "Grant us forty-eight hours."

Ray looked at Tessa. She met his eyes and felt, to her irritation, that she was being asked to vouch for a man she had known for less than two hours.

"Forty-eight hours," she said. "But I'm going to need the lamps."

## Chapter 3: Terms of Truce

The terms were written on a paper placemat from the Dock Street Diner, because Gus had run out of legal pads in 2019 and never bought more.

Tessa wrote them in blue ink. Owen read over her shoulder and corrected her spelling of "Pruitt" twice before he gave up and let her spell it wrong on purpose, just to see whether he would notice again. He did not. He only sighed, took the pen, and added a line in the bottom corner in neat, square print.

*Who pays for the paint?*

"Nobody," Tessa said. "There's no paint. The cottage is already painted."

"Then who pays for the fresh flowers?"

"Bev."

"Bev doesn't pay for anything. She sends the bill to whoever is least likely to argue." He tapped the placemat. "Lay it down. Flowers, twelve dollars a week, split down the middle."

She wrote it down. The placemat was getting crowded. Under the flowers she had listed the sofas, the blue quilt, the gray quilt, the two dining chairs with the cracked rungs, and the lamp situation, which she had labeled in capital letters as THE WAR.

"Be serious," she said.

"I'm extremely serious," Owen said. "I've never been more serious about a lamp in my life."

It was nine forty in the morning. The booths along the front window were filling up with the usual crowd, retired men in windbreakers arguing about the ballgame, two young mothers with strollers parked in the aisle, and a table of women from the Larkspur Lake Garden Club who had been watching Tessa and Owen with interest since they sat down. One of them had already come over to ask whether Tessa was Len Marsh's niece, and whether she planned to keep the barn open, and whether she was going to sell the sofa to the Pruitts or the Fenners, since she had heard the Fenners were coming to see the house on Saturday.

"Who are the Fenners?" Tessa had asked after the woman left.

"Warren and Priscilla," Owen said. "From Oak Park. They have a place on Sunset Point already, and they want something a little closer to the water for the grandkids. She's a pediatric nurse, and he sells insurance, and they are the most reasonable buyers I have had all year. They will look at a house for four minutes and know exactly what they want. Which is good, because they will also walk out in four minutes if they don't."

"And they're coming Saturday for the open house."

"They're coming Saturday for the open house. Ray has them on the list. Nora has them on her calendar in red."

Tessa wrote *Fenners, Saturday, RED* on the placemat and underlined it twice.

"Okay," she said. "Here's my list. The house is a three-bedroom with a living room, a kitchen, a dining room, a primary bedroom with a view of the lake, a second bedroom Len called the Dock Room, and a little bedroom at the back that everybody agrees is a closet with a window. We stage the living room with the cream sofa, the blue quilt on the bed in the primary, the dining table on the porch, and the brass lamp in the Dock Room. That's the plan."

"That's the plan you want," Owen said. "The plan I want is the sofa in the living room, the other sofa on the porch for the photos, the brass lamp in the Dock Room with the green shade, and a rug in the dining room that is not the blue one."

"The blue one is for the dining room."

"The blue one is for the Dock Room. The gray one is for the dining room, because the gray one looks like the inside of a Chicago bank and buyers from Chicago trust banks."

"That's the most ridiculous thing you've ever said."

"I've been saying it for two years. Ask anyone at Kestrel Realty."

Tessa put down her pen. "Owen, I've been in this business for nine years. I was a senior stager at Northline Staging, under Brent Malloy, and before that I was an assistant to four different designers who never once let me pick out a throw pillow. I know what sells a house. Len taught me that when I was twelve, and I didn't realize until last year that he had been testing me on it the whole time."

"And you're going to tell me the blue rug goes in the Dock Room," Owen said.

"I'm going to tell you the blue rug goes in the Dock Room because the light in there is cold and the blue warms it. The gray makes the room look like a waiting room. And the photos on the listing will be taken at four o'clock in the afternoon, when the sun comes off the water. That's the light Len wanted. He wrote it on the contract."

Owen was quiet for a moment, then picked up the placemat and read the margin where Len had penciled a note in his tidy hand. *Shoot at 4. Light off water. Blue quilt, Dock Room, not the gray.*

"He did write that," Owen said.

"He wrote that in January. I saw the original."

"Then I'm wrong about the rug," Owen said, and seemed almost surprised to say it. "But I'm not wrong about the lamp."

"You are about the lamp, too."

"I'll take that under advisement."

Tessa picked up the pen and wrote, in small capital letters at the bottom of the placemat, *BLUE RUG: TESSA. LAMP: OWEN, TO BE DECIDED FRIDAY.* She turned the placemat around and pushed it toward him.

He read it twice, then smiled slowly. "You're not going to give an inch."

"I'm going to give exactly one inch," she said. "On the lamp. If the light fails on Saturday, you get to say I told you so."

"And if it doesn't fail?"

"Then you have to admit that the brass lamp belongs in the Dock Room, and that you've been wrong about it since before you could drive."

"Deal," Owen said, and put out his hand across the table.

She hesitated only a second before she took it. His palm was warm and rough, the palm of a man who carried things for a living, and she was annoyed to notice how quickly he let go.

The Garden Club women were pretending not to watch them. Gus came back to the booth with a pot of coffee and a plate of blueberry pancakes that nobody had ordered, and set them down between their cups as if it were the most natural thing in the world.

"Bev sent those," he said. "She says you two are going to need the carbohydrates. She also says the flowers are her idea and she'll hear no argument about it."

"We already agreed on the flowers," Owen said.

"I know. She wanted you to know she was in charge of them." Gus wiped his hands on his apron. "She also says the Pruitts called her at seven this morning, and she told them you two were fine, and that you'd be done by Friday, and that she was not going to be the one to tell them otherwise. So you'd better be done by Friday."

"We'll be done by Friday," Tessa said.

"Good," Gus said. "Because if I have to hear one more argument about a rug at my counter, I'm going to ask for a cut of the commission."

When he had gone, Owen picked up a fork and cut a corner from the top pancake, then looked at Tessa with an expression she had not seen on him before, a kind of cautious, almost boyish hope.

"Can I tell you something about Len?" he asked.

"You can tell me anything about Len," she said. "I've been wanting someone to."

"He never once in two years asked me whether I was making money on the props. He asked me whether the pieces were getting the light they needed, and whether I'd remembered to write the room on the tag." Owen put the fork down. "I always thought he was just being careful. But I think he was trusting me."

Tessa looked at the pancakes. They were going cold, the butter sliding off in a slow yellow line. She thought about the tag on the inside of her uncle's truck door, the masking tape with *Unit 9* in pencil, the way he had handed her the storage unit key at the funeral lunch in February and said, very quietly, "Don't sell the good lamps to the first person who asks."

"He trusted me too," she said. "He just didn't tell me that until I was already here."

Owen nodded slowly. He did not say anything, and she was grateful for that.

They ate the pancakes. They argued about the flowers again, and then about the dining chairs, and then, when Gus had gone back to the grill and the Garden Club women had left with a wave, about the brass lamp, which neither of them would give up, and which both of them, Tessa realized with a jolt, had already started to picture in the window of the Dock Room with the light coming off the lake.

When they finally stood to go, the placemat was full. Owen folded it in half, then in half again, and tucked it into the pocket of his button-down.

"For the record," he said, "I'm keeping this."

"It's my contract."

"It's a placemat," Owen said. "And you wrote the terms in blue ink, which means it's binding in the state of Wisconsin."

"That isn't how contracts work."

"It is in Larkspur Lake," he said, and held the door for her, and Tessa walked through it into the warm late-May morning with her uncle's blue quilt in her head and a stranger's laugh in her ears, and tried very hard not to think about how much she was going to miss the placemat if Owen did not give it back.

## Chapter 4: Eleven Lamps

The Birch Street cottage had eleven lamps, and by the time the sun was high over the cedars on Thursday, Tessa had counted them all twice.

She counted them again while she unwrapped the second sofa in the living room, because the living room was where the argument had started and she wanted to be sure of her ground. There were two table lamps with pleated shades on the sideboard, a tall floor lamp with a bronze base in the corner by the fireplace, a pair of sconces that Bev had insisted were original to the house, and a green glass banker's lamp on the desk in the second bedroom, which Owen had already claimed for his own office in the listing photos. Upstairs in the primary bedroom there were two bedside lamps with linen shades. In the Dock Room, which had windows on three sides and a view of the water through the cedars, stood the brass floor lamp with the green glass shade. The eleventh was the little lamp in the closet-bedroom at the back, a plain white ceramic thing with a crooked cord that Tessa planned to throw away.

"You can't throw away the closet lamp," Owen said from the doorway. He had a clipboard, a tape measure, and an expression of absolute calm that she found more irritating than any argument.

"It has a cracked base and the cord is frayed."

"It's Len's. He bought it at a yard sale in 2018 for a quarter. He said it was the best quarter he ever spent, because it made a closet look like a reading nook."

"A closet is a closet."

"A closet is a place where people sleep when their in-laws come for the summer," Owen said, "and this one is going to be photographed with the lamp on, because that's what Len wanted. Place it in the corner. Turn it on. Trust the light to do the work."

She could not argue with that, because she had been about to say the same thing about the Dock Room lamp three hours earlier and had been talked out of it by her own conscience.

They had been at it since eight. Owen had come up the drive at seven in a fresh shirt with two cups of coffee from the Dock Street Diner and a bag of Bev's oatmeal cookies, and he had set one cup on the porch rail and the other on the hood of Tessa's rental and said, without preamble, "We'll walk the house, and I'll tell you everything that's wrong with it."

She had walked the house with him, and he had told her. The porch railing was loose on the north end. The primary bathroom had a drip under the vanity that Bev had reported in April and that Ray Pruitt had promised to fix in May. The kitchen floor had a soft spot by the back door, which he had marked with a strip of blue painter's tape. The attic was full of boxes labeled in Ada Pruitt's careful cursive, and Owen had made a note to ask Nora whether she wanted them or whether she would let Tessa haul them to the barn.

"And the lamps," Tessa had said.

"The lamps are fine. Except the brass one."

"There's nothing wrong with the brass one."

"There's something wrong with the shade," Owen said, and lifted it off the stand to show her. The green glass was clouded along one edge, a fine white crazing like a crack in ice. "Len used to say it was the original. I think he was right, and I think the original needs a new bulb, a better socket, and a little less light."

"It needs more light," Tessa said. "That's the whole point. The light off the water comes in at four, and the shade catches it and throws it onto the ceiling. If you put in a dimmer, you lose the effect."

"If you put in a dimmer, you can actually control it," Owen said. "Which is what a buyer wants. Nobody wants to walk into a room that's too bright or too dark. They want to be able to decide for themselves."

"They don't want to decide anything. They want to fall in love with the room in four minutes and make an offer before they've had time to think about the mortgage."

"That's cynical."

"That's Chicago."

"Chicago has a lot of cynicism," he said mildly, "and it sells a lot of houses, and it has never once understood a lake."

She had not expected that, and it stopped her for a moment, and in that moment she felt a small, unexpected ache. Her uncle had said almost the same thing to her once, on a Sunday night call, when she had been complaining about a client who wanted every room in her condo painted gray. *Chicago doesn't understand water, Tess,* he had said. *Water makes people honest. Offer them a view and they stop lying to you.*

"Okay," she said. "The dimmer goes in the Dock Room, and the brass lamp stays where it is. I'll get Dennis from the hardware store to put it in tomorrow."

"Dennis is going to charge us double for a Thursday."

"Then you can tell him he's a crook. You've known him longer than I have."

"That's true," Owen admitted. "Dennis taught me to drive a stick shift in the parking lot of the bait shop. He cried when I hit the bait bucket."

Tessa laughed before she could stop herself. It came out louder than she meant, and Moose, who had been sitting on the porch in a patch of sun, lifted his head and looked at her with an expression of deep, dog-like suspicion.

"Don't," she told the dog.

"He's laughing at you," Owen said.

"He's laughing with me. He knows I'm right about the lamp."

"Moose has never once been right about a lamp in his life."

They set the sofas. This was the easy part, though it was not easy. The living room sofa had to sit across from the fireplace at a distance that made the photo look balanced. The second sofa, the twin, had to go on the porch, facing the lake, for the exterior photos. Owen wanted it turned so the light caught the walnut feet. Tessa wanted it square, because that was how Len had always placed it, and because she could see the sofa's back clearly through the open front door and it looked like the back of a man who was waiting for a bus.

"We'll shoot it both ways," Owen said finally. "And Nora gets to pick."

"Nora will pick the one that looks like a crime scene."

"Nora is a retired ophthalmologist from Phoenix. She'll pick the one that looks like it has a view. You don't know her."

"Neither do you."

"Nobody knows Nora," Owen said. "That's why Ray hired me to tell her what she wants."

The living room came together faster than Tessa had expected. She set the blue quilt on the primary bed and then, after a long look at the room, moved it to the Dock Room, where it went across the old iron bed like it had been waiting there all along. The gray quilt she folded and put in the closet. She laid a cream rug in front of the living room sofa and a rag rug in the kitchen, which Owen said was going to get dirty within an hour and which she said was exactly the point. The dining chairs with the cracked rungs went into the barn for repair. Owen carried them down the back steps two at a time, and she watched him do it and tried not to notice the way his shirt pulled across his shoulders.

She noticed anyway. She noticed the sweat at the back of his neck when he came back up the steps, and the way he rubbed his palm over the cut on his thumb without seeming to feel it. She noticed the way he looked at the Dock Room when he thought she was not watching, as if he were seeing it for the first time.

"You love this house," she said.

He turned, startled. "What?"

"You're looking at it like you're going to miss it."

"I'm going to miss the lamp," Owen said. "I've been looking at that lamp for three years. It's the only thing in Larkspur Lake that's ever gotten the better of me."

"You're doing it again. Changing the subject."

"I'm not changing anything. I'm telling you the truth."

"You're telling me half of it."

He did not answer for a moment. He stepped into the Dock Room and went to the windows, where the late-morning light came in off the water and lay across the floorboards in long, moving ribbons. He put one hand against the glass.

"Len used to sit right here," he said quietly. "Every Thursday, for almost two years. He'd bring a sandwich and a thermos and a yellow legal pad, and he'd sit in that chair and write in his book, and he'd tell me about the people who had lived in every house on this street. He knew all of it. Who had been married, who had been divorced, who had lost a child to the lake in 1974 and never came back to visit the cottage. He used to say the houses remembered everything, and it was our job to make sure the buyers never had to know what they remembered." He took his hand away from the glass. "I miss him, Tessa. I miss him every single Thursday."

She went to stand beside him. She did not touch him. She looked out at the water, which was bright and blue and rippling under a light breeze, and at the cedars, and at a pair of mallards paddling in the shallows near the pier.

"What book?" she asked.

Owen blinked. "What?"

"You said he wrote in his book. What book?"

For a moment he looked as though he might answer. Then something closed in his face, quick and quiet, the way a shutter drops over a window.

"Just his notes," he said. "On the houses. You know how he was."

"I know how he was," she said slowly. "Where are the notes now?"

"In the barn, I'd guess. Or Unit 9." He stepped back and picked up his clipboard. "Look, we've got two hours before the Pruitts come by for the walk-through, and Nora wants a photo of the porch at four. Can we talk about the lamp?"

Tessa looked at him. She thought about the notebook she had not yet found, and the notes she had not yet read, and the Thursday mornings in the Dock Room that she had never known about.

"We can talk about the lamp," she said. "But I want to see the notes."

"You'll see them," Owen said, without looking at her. "I promise."

He went out to the porch to check the railing, and Tessa stood alone in the Dock Room with the blue quilt across the bed and the brass lamp in the window, its green shade catching a last thin ribbon of light from the water. She crossed the room, reached out, and touched the base of the lamp. It was cool and smooth, and there was a small brass tag screwed to the underside of the stand, the kind a shop used to mark a piece for sale. Someone had written on it in pencil.

She tipped the lamp gently toward the light so that she could read the tag.

*O.R. / Dock Rm / DO NOT SELL WITHOUT ASKING.*

She stood very still. Then she put the lamp back the way it had been, exactly, with the shade turned toward the water, and walked out to the porch, where Owen was tightening a screw in the railing with his back to her and the sun on his shoulders.

"Owen," she said.

He turned around. "Yes?"

"Who is O.R.?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he set down the screwdriver.

"That's me," he said.

## Chapter 5: The Brass Tag

"You put your name on my uncle's lamp," Tessa said.

"I put my initials on a tag," Owen said. "There's a difference."

"Not to a judge."

"There's no judge in Larkspur Lake. There's a township board that meets on the second Tuesday, and Joyce Kessler, who runs the zoning office and thinks the Fourth of July parade is a traffic hazard." He picked up the screwdriver again, then set it down, as if he could not decide what to do with his hands. "Tessa, I'm going to tell you what I can. Not everything. Some of it isn't mine to tell."

They were on the porch at Birch Street with the late sun coming low across the lake and the cedars throwing long shadows over the lawn. Moose was asleep on the second sofa, which had been turned, after a fierce twenty-minute debate, so that its walnut feet caught the light. The rag rug in the kitchen had already picked up a streak of muddy paw print. Somewhere down the street a radio was playing a ballgame, and the announcer's voice rose and fell like a tide.

"Start with the tag," Tessa said.

Owen rubbed the back of his neck. "Len did the tags," he said. "Every piece that came out of Unit 9 had one. He used brass ones from a supplier in Racine, so the pieces wouldn't wander off in somebody else's house. On the back he wrote the room, and on the front he wrote the owner's initials, so that whoever moved the piece knew who to call. For the things that belonged to the storage business, he wrote MH. For the things that belonged to somebody else, he wrote the initials of the person who had put up the money."

"And the lamp."

"The lamp was bought in 2023. With my money."

Tessa said nothing. The radio below rose to a roar, a crowd somewhere in a stadium, and fell away.

"I didn't buy it outright," Owen said. "Len needed to upgrade the inventory. He had three hundred pieces in the barn, and about half of them were from estate sales in the nineties, and they were falling apart. He wanted new lamps, new rugs, a few good tables, and a second storage unit so the barn could breathe. He didn't have the cash, because he'd spent it all on the Stickley desk he thought he'd sell for twice what he paid. So I lent him thirty thousand dollars." He looked at her. "It was a loan. He signed a note. He was going to pay me back out of the commissions on the Cedar Point job and the marina condo, and in return I had access to the inventory for my own listings, and a share in what the pieces brought in when they sold."

"A share," Tessa repeated.

"A small one. Not a partnership. I'm not a partner in Marsh Home Staging. I'm a creditor, with a handshake and a piece of paper." Owen's jaw tightened. "Len told me that if anything happened to him, the note would be honored, and the barn would be yours, and you'd decide what to do. He said it that way. *The barn is Tess's. The note is mine to pay, and I'll pay it.* And then he got sick, and I didn't know what the note was worth, because he'd never written down the number, and I didn't want to ask his niece in the hospital parking lot whether she was going to make good on a handshake."

"Did you ask him?"

"I asked him in January. He said, 'Don't worry about the money, kid. It's in the book.'"

"The book," Tessa said. "What book?"

"I don't know," Owen said honestly. "I've never seen it. I don't think anybody has but Len. He kept a record of everything, and he kept it somewhere he didn't tell anyone about. Bev calls it the Ledger, because she read it once over his shoulder, and she's still a little offended that he never let her see the rest of it."

"So you've been using my uncle's inventory for two years, against a loan he never documented, and you didn't think to mention it at the funeral lunch."

"I mentioned it to you in the diner," Owen said. "On the first morning. I said I'd been using the props."

"You said you'd been putting them back!"

"Because I had." He stood, pacing the length of the porch in three long strides and turning back again. "I put back every piece I used, Tessa. I took the Stickley desk to the Ostrander house in April and brought it back the same afternoon. I took the rugs to the marina condo and brought them back with the dust brushed off. Every single piece has a tag, and every tag has the right room on it, and I've got a spreadsheet on my laptop with the date and the address for every time I touched something. I'm not a thief. I'm the guy who carries the couch, and I've been carrying your uncle's couch for two years, and I never once asked him for a dime that wasn't owed."

"Then why does the lamp say *do not sell without asking*?"

Owen stopped pacing.

"Because it's mine," he said finally. "In part. And that's what it says."

"You wrote that?"

"No. Len did. He wrote it the week I gave him the money, and he put it on the lamp because the lamp was the first thing I ever bought him from the estate sale in Racine. A brass floor lamp with a green glass shade that was chipped and crazed and nobody else wanted. I paid forty dollars for it. Len said it was going to be the best lamp in the county." He looked at the window, at the brass stand and the green shade behind the glass. "He said if I ever wanted it back, all I had to do was ask. He said if you ever wanted to sell it, you should ask me first."

Tessa was quiet for a long time. The sun dropped another inch behind the cedars. The shadows lengthened across the grass and climbed the porch steps.

"Why didn't you tell me any of this on the first day?" she said. "When we were standing in the drive."

"Because you looked like you were going to cry," Owen said, "and because I didn't know you. And then you looked like you were going to hit me with a lamp, and that seemed worse."

She laughed, a short, unhappy sound that surprised them both.

"I'm still going to hit you with a lamp," she said.

"That's fair."

"And I don't like that you have a handshake and a note and a shade with crazing on it, and I don't know what any of it is worth."

"Neither do I," he said. "That's what I'm trying to tell you. I don't know, and I'm scared to find out, because if the note is worth more than the business, then you're going to have to sell it to pay me, and you'll hate me for the rest of your life."

"I don't hate you," Tessa said, and was surprised to find it was true. "I'm just angry that my uncle trusted you with things he never trusted me with."

Owen was silent. Then he sat down again, heavily, on the top step, and rested his elbows on his knees.

"He trusted you with the house," he said. "You know that. He didn't ask me to run the barn. He asked me to make sure you had something to come back to, if you ever wanted it. That's the only thing I've been trying to do since February."

"You could have called me."

"I did call you," Owen said. "Twice. In March. You didn't pick up, and you didn't call back. Connie said you were in a meeting with a lawyer about the estate, and that you didn't want to talk about Larkspur Lake until the paperwork was done." He gave her a small, tired look. "So I took the sofa out of Unit 9, because the Pruitts needed a sofa, and because somebody had to do it. And then you showed up in a rental truck, and I thought, well, there goes that."

Tessa sat down beside him on the top step. She did not touch him. The boards were warm under her, still holding the day's heat, and the smell of cut grass and lake water came up from the lawn in a soft, green wave.

"Connie is going to hear about this," she said.

"Connie already heard about it. She called me Wednesday night to ask whether I'd been sleeping in the barn."

Tessa laughed again, and this time it was easier.

"Okay," she said after a while. "Here are my terms."

"You're going to have terms."

"I'm going to have terms." She rested her chin on her knees and looked out at the water. "You stop using the storage unit without telling me, starting now. Every piece that leaves Unit 9 gets logged in a book with the date and the room. You show me the spreadsheet, and I'll show you the Ledger, if I ever find it. And the brass lamp stays in the Dock Room until the Fenners have seen it on Saturday, and then we'll decide what to do about the note."

"And the note?"

"The note is a problem for Monday," Tessa said. "I'm not going to figure out my uncle's bookkeeping on a Friday night in May with a dog on the sofa."

"Agreed," Owen said. "All of it."

"And one more thing."

"Yes?"

"Don't ever tell me the lamp is the only thing in Larkspur Lake that's gotten the better of you again. I don't like the sound of it."

He turned his head and looked at her, and for a moment the amusement was back in his face, but it was softer than before, and there was something under it she did not quite recognize.

"That was a joke, Tessa."

"I know it was a joke," she said. "I just don't want you making jokes about the one thing my uncle left you that I don't understand."

Owen was silent for a long moment. Then he nodded, once.

"Okay," he said. "No more lamp jokes."

"Good."

"Except the one about the bait bucket. That one's sacred."

"I'll allow the bait bucket," Tessa said, and this time she did smile, and he saw it, and something in the set of his shoulders eased.

Along the street, the ballgame ended with a roar from somebody's radio, and a screen door banged, and Bev Halvorsen's voice carried across the yards, calling a cat named Admiral in for the night. Moose woke on the sofa, stretched out his legs with a groan, and jumped down onto the lawn with the air of an animal who had been waiting all evening for his cue.

"Tomorrow is Saturday," Owen said.

"Tomorrow is the open house."

"Tomorrow the Fenners come, and Nora flies in from Phoenix, and Ray tries to sell them the boathouse." He stood and held out a hand to help her up. "Are we going to be ready?"

She took his hand and let him pull her to her feet. "We're going to be ready," she said. "We're going to be the best-staged house on Birch Street, and you're going to tell the Fenners that the lamp is original, and I'm going to tell them it's a gift from my uncle."

"Is it?"

"It is now," Tessa said, and went to find her keys.

## Chapter 6: Open House

By one o'clock on Saturday, there were forty-one cars parked along Birch Street, and Bev Halvorsen had appointed herself keeper of the guest book.

She sat at the little secretary desk in the front hall with a pen on a ribbon and a plate of lemon bars at her elbow, and she greeted every visitor by name, whether she knew them or not. "Good afternoon, Mrs. Dombrowski, you'll want to look at the porch first. The light is wonderful on the water in the afternoon." "Mr. Jacobsen, the bathroom is down the hall, and the drip is being seen to by a professional, so please don't tell anyone I said so." "Oh, hello, darling, you must be the people from Madison, and this is the Dock Room, and the lamp in the window is the most beautiful thing you will see in all of Larkspur Lake."

Tessa, standing at the top of the stairs with a stack of brochures, heard every word and tried not to wince.

"She's going to get us sued," Owen said, passing below her with a tray of lemonade.

"She's going to get us sold."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

The house had come together the way Tessa had hoped. The cream sofa sat in the living room, catching the light from the front windows, and the second sofa had been turned on the porch so that visitors coming up the walk saw it first, facing the lake with its walnut feet glowing. The blue quilt covered the iron bed in the Dock Room, and the brass floor lamp stood in the corner window with its green shade lit from inside, throwing a soft, moving pattern of light across the ceiling whenever the breeze came off the water through the open sash. Owen had been right about the dimmer, though she would never say so out loud. Dennis from the hardware store had put it in on Friday for double his usual rate, and the light now came up slowly, like a sunrise, as if the room itself were waking to greet the afternoon.

In the closet-bedroom at the back, the white ceramic lamp with the crooked cord sat on a small pine table, lit, as Owen had insisted, so that the closet looked like a place where someone might read a novel with a cup of tea. Tessa had not been convinced. Now she had to admit that it worked.

Her phone buzzed on her hip. It was Lucy.

*Did you sell the house yet? Be honest.*

*Not yet. Forty-one cars.*

*Forty-one? Tess, that's a riot. Is there a man involved?*

*There is a man involved. He has a dog problem and a lamp problem and a placemat.*

*That's three problems. Are you going to be okay?*

Tessa looked at the phone for a long moment before she typed the answer.

*I think so.*

Then she put the phone in her pocket and went down the stairs, because a woman in a green silk blouse was standing in the living room with her hand on the back of the cream sofa, looking at it with an expression that Tessa knew very well. It was the look of a buyer who had already decided.

"Is this the original upholstery?" the woman asked.

"It's the original frame," Tessa said. "The upholstery is new, though. My uncle had it done in a cream linen that holds up to kids and dogs."

"It's beautiful," the woman said. "Warren, come look at this."

A tall man in a blue sweater came in from the porch, followed by Owen, who was holding a glass of lemonade in each hand and looked, Tessa thought, like a man who had been asked to referee a custody battle. The tall man glanced at the sofa, then at the fireplace, then at the view through the front windows, and his face softened in a way that Tessa knew meant trouble.

"Priscilla," he said. "This is the one."

"It's the one," Priscilla agreed. "But I want to see the back bedroom first."

"Of course," Owen said, and handed Warren his lemonade. "The back bedroom has the best closet in the county."

Priscilla laughed, and it was a warm, unforced sound, and Tessa liked her at once.

They went up the stairs in a small procession, Priscilla first, then Warren, then Owen, then Tessa, and at the top of the stairs Moose came charging out of the primary bedroom with a tennis ball in his mouth and a look of unholy delight on his face.

Tessa had no idea how he had gotten upstairs. She had shut the primary door at noon and was almost certain she had latched it. She saw him now, bounding down the hall toward them with his ears flat and his tail going like a propeller, and she saw the open door of the Dock Room behind him, where the afternoon sun was pouring through the water-reflected window and a gull had come to rest on the sill.

Moose saw the gull. The gull saw Moose. The gull took off, screaming, straight through the open sash.

"Moose, no," Tessa said.

Moose went after the gull.

He went through the Dock Room door at full speed, skidded on the old floorboards, and launched himself at the window with a happy bark. He did not reach the gull. He did, however, reach the brass floor lamp in the corner, which had been standing on a narrow table that Owen had set against the wall because the floor was too uneven for it to stand straight. The table tipped. The lamp leaned. The green glass shade, which had been crazed along one edge since before Owen was born, swung out on its arm, struck the window frame with a sharp crack, and split down the middle like a shell.

For a moment nobody moved.

Then the lamp came down. It did not fall far, because Owen was already across the room with both hands out, catching the brass stand before it hit the floor and lowering it, gently, with the expression of a man rescuing a child from a well. The shade did not survive. It slid off the arm and hit the floorboards in three large pieces and a scatter of smaller ones, each of them glinting green in the sun.

Moose sat down in the middle of the carpet with the tennis ball in his mouth and looked at the wreckage with an air of enormous, innocent satisfaction.

"Oh, no," Priscilla said. "Oh, no, no, no. Is that the lamp?"

Tessa could not speak. Her throat had closed. She stood in the doorway of the Dock Room and watched the glass lie scattered across the blue quilt and the floorboards, and she thought, very clearly, *Len's shade. Len's lamp. Len's note on the tag.* She could not remember her uncle's voice in that moment, only the sound of a pencil on a masking-tape label.

"It's fine," Owen said.

He was on one knee beside the lamp, his hands on the brass stand, his face perfectly calm. He did not look at Tessa. He looked at Priscilla, and then at Warren, and then, finally, at the window, where the afternoon light was still coming through the empty frame.

"It's absolutely fine," he said. "The shade is antique. It was cracked when we bought it, and the owner always meant to replace it. We'll have a replacement in ten minutes. Please, go look at the closet. You'll love the closet."

"But the lamp—"

"I'll show you the closet," Owen said, and stood, and offered Priscilla his arm.

Priscilla hesitated, then took it, laughing a little, shaking her head. Warren followed them into the hall with a bewildered look over his shoulder. Tessa stayed in the doorway, unable to move.

Owen came back in a moment later, alone.

"Tessa," he said quietly. "Breathe."

"That was Len's shade."

"I know."

"That was the one thing in this house he wanted me to keep."

"I know," Owen said. "And we're going to save the room." He picked up the largest piece of green glass from the floor, turned it over in his hand, and looked at it. "But I need you to go get the lamp from the back bedroom. The white one. The one with the crooked cord."

She stared at him. "The closet lamp?"

"The closet lamp. Bring it here. Plug it into the outlet by the window, the one with the dimmer. Place it on the floor, next to the bed, where the light comes in off the water." He set the glass down carefully on the windowsill. "Len bought that lamp for a quarter. He said it was the best quarter he ever spent. We'll see if he was right."

She did not argue. She could not have argued if she had wanted to. She went down the hall, past the closet, past the open door of the back bedroom where the white ceramic lamp sat on its pine table, and she picked it up with both hands, feeling its weight, feeling the crooked cord swing against her knee. She carried it back to the Dock Room and set it on the floor beside the iron bed, plugged it into the outlet by the window, and turned it on.

The light came up soft and warm. It fell across the blue quilt and the scattered green glass, and across the floorboards, and onto the walls, where it made the room look, for the first time in Tessa's memory, like a place that someone had chosen to live in.

Owen was standing in the doorway, watching her face.

"It works," he said.

"It works," she said, and her voice was not steady.

Below, the front door opened, and Bev's voice rang up the staircase like a church bell. "Priscilla, darling, you must come back down and sign the book! I've been saving you a lemon bar!"

Moose, who had finally finished with his tennis ball, stood up, shook himself off, and trotted out into the hall as if nothing had happened.

Owen crouched down beside Tessa, close enough that she could feel the heat of his shoulder. He picked up one of the green shards and held it up to the light, so that a tiny green glow fell across his face.

"Len would have hated that," he said.

"Len would have laughed at that," Tessa said. "He'd have said the dog had better taste than the rest of us."

Owen looked at her, and she saw that he was trying not to smile and failing.

"Your uncle was a difficult man," he said.

"He was impossible."

"He was right about the lamp."

"He was right about everything," Tessa said. Her eyes were stinging. She blinked hard. "That's what made him impossible."

Owen did not say anything. He only reached out and, very gently, put the green shard back on the sill, where the light caught it, and left his hand resting for a moment on the back of hers.

Below, Priscilla Fenner's laughter rose up the staircase, bright and clear, and then Warren's voice, lower and sure, saying something Tessa could not hear.

Owen took his hand away.

"That," he said, "sounds like a yes."

## Chapter 7: A Yes With Conditions

The Fenners made their offer at a quarter past six, sitting on the porch steps with a cup of Bev's coffee in each of their hands and Moose lying across both their feet like a warm, damp rug.

Warren did most of the talking. He was a careful man, Tessa could tell, and he had the habit of looking at the lake when he was about to say something important, as if the water would hold him to it. "We'd like to make an offer," he said, "at the asking price, with the furniture included. The sofas, the rugs, the dining set, and the lamps."

"All the lamps?" Owen asked.

"All of them," Priscilla said. "Except the one with the broken shade, which we understand is going to be replaced. We'd like that replaced by Monday, and we'd like to be the ones who choose the replacement."

Owen looked at Tessa. Tessa looked at the lake.

"The lamp isn't for sale," she said.

Warren turned his head slowly. "I'm sorry?"

"The brass lamp in the Dock Room. It's not for sale. It belongs to my uncle's estate, and it has a note on it that says it isn't mine to sell without asking the person who put up the money." She glanced at Owen, who had gone very still beside her. "I'm sure you understand."

Priscilla laughed, a little uncertainly. "Is it a family heirloom?"

"It's a forty-dollar estate sale lamp," Owen said. "With a fifty-dollar shade that's now in pieces on the floor of the Dock Room, and a tag that says do not sell without asking. We'll replace the shade. We'll replace the lamp, if you want a new one. But the old one stays in the house until somebody decides what it's worth."

"That's the most honest thing anybody's ever said to me in a sales meeting," Warren said, and then he laughed, a full, surprised laugh, and Priscilla joined him, and Moose lifted his head and thumped his tail twice against the porch.

"I'll tell you what," Priscilla said, when they had recovered. "We'll offer on the house, with the furniture, and we'll leave the lamp out of it. We'll pay the asking price on the sofas and the rugs and the dining set, and we'll let you two decide about the lamp. And we'll buy a new shade, too, if you want, because I'm not having a house with a broken lampshade in the front window on the day we move in."

"That's generous," Tessa said.

"It's not generous," Priscilla said. "It's practical. We have four grandchildren, and one of them is going to break something before the first family holiday, and I'd rather it was a lampshade than the dining table." She set her cup on the step. "Also, I want the closet lamp. The one with the crooked cord. My husband says it's the ugliest thing he's ever seen, and I think it's the most charming, and I want to fight him about it for the next thirty years."

Tessa found herself laughing, a real laugh, the first one all day that did not feel like it might turn into something else.

"The closet lamp was my uncle's," she said. "It cost a quarter."

"Then I'll pay him a dollar for it," Priscilla said. "And he can buy himself a candy bar."

"He can't," Tessa said. "He died in February."

The laughter stopped. Priscilla looked stricken, and Warren put a hand on her knee, and for a moment the porch was quiet except for the lap of water at the bottom of the lawn.

"I'm so sorry," Priscilla said softly.

"It's all right," Tessa said. "He'd have liked the fight."

"He would have," Owen said, and his voice was rough. "He'd have sold you the lamp for a dollar, and then told you the cord was a fire hazard, and then he'd have charged you for the lesson."

Priscilla smiled, then looked at Owen with sudden interest. "You knew him well."

"I carried his couches," Owen said.

"That's what you do," Priscilla said. "Everybody says so. The Realtors on the north shore all say you're the best closer in the county, and nobody can say why."

"Because I carry the couch," Owen said, and this time he smiled, and it reached his eyes. "That's the whole trick. Bring the couch into the right room, and the buyer does the rest."

Warren stood up and offered his hand to Tessa. "We'll talk to Ray tomorrow," he said. "And we'll wait for Monday. If the lamp stays in the house, we'll be happy. If it doesn't, we'll be happy about that too, as long as somebody tells us why."

"It stays until we know what it's worth," Tessa said. "I'll make sure you're the first to know."

"That's fair," Warren said. "Priscilla's going to make us come back on Thursday for the closet anyway."

They walked down the steps together, the tall man and the woman in the green silk blouse, their voices carrying up the drive as they climbed into a silver sedan that had been parked at the far end of the gravel all afternoon. Priscilla waved at the porch. Warren lifted a hand. The car rolled past Bev's hedge and disappeared around the corner onto Front Street.

Moose sighed, rolled onto his back, and waved all four legs at the evening sky.

"Oh," Owen said. "That went better than the lamp."

"The lamp didn't go at all."

"The lamp is in the house," Owen said. "That's the same thing as going well."

Tessa sat down on the top step again. Her legs were trembling, and she could not tell whether it was relief or the aftermath of the afternoon, when the shade had split and she had stood frozen in the doorway while the green glass lay scattered on the blue quilt.

"I can't believe Moose got upstairs," she said.

"I can," Owen said. "He's been up in the barn hayloft since he was a puppy. Len kept a cot up there, and Moose slept on it every night for nine years. Bev says he cried under the blanket for a week after the funeral and wouldn't come out until Gus brought him a hot dog."

Tessa looked at him, startled. "Moose sleeps in the hayloft?"

"Every night for nine years. Nobody ever told him to stop, and Len never had the heart to try." Owen grinned. "Bev had to tell me. I thought he just liked the hay."

"You are the most annoying man I have ever met."

"I know." He leaned back on his elbows, looking up at the sky. "Your uncle used to say the same thing."

They sat together in silence for a while. The light was going, the cedars turning black against a sky the color of apricots. Below at the water, a motorboat puttered toward the marina, its running lights blinking red and green. Somewhere in the cottage behind them, the refrigerator hummed and clicked, and the brass lamp in the Dock Room threw a faint green-gold glow through the empty window.

"Ray called," Owen said at last. "While you were upstairs with Priscilla. He wants us both at his office on Monday at nine. He says the Pruitt sisters have had three calls from Phoenix already, and Nora wants to know who's in charge of the house."

"Who's in charge?"

"Nobody knows," Owen said. "That's the problem. Ray thinks we should both be in the room, so Nora can see the two of us argue in person and decide which one she trusts less."

Tessa groaned and let her head fall back against the porch post.

"Nine o'clock," she said.

"Nine o'clock. Ray says bring the contract. Nora will want to see the blue-ink terms."

"You told him about the placemat?"

"I told him about the placemat," Owen said. "Gus called him at seven this morning. Gus took a picture of the placemat before we left the booth, and by noon he had it printed and framed, with a little sign that says 'Binding in the State of Wisconsin.'"

"He did not."

"He did. Bev's already been in to see it. She says it's the best thing that's ever happened to the Dock Street Diner, and she's going to bring her own pie plates for the sale."

Tessa put her face in her hands and laughed until her ribs hurt.

When she looked up again, Owen was watching her with an expression she could not read. It was tender, and wary, and something in it made her heart move in a way she did not want to examine.

"Tomorrow is Sunday," he said.

"Tomorrow is Sunday," Tessa agreed. "And we have a shade to order, and a storage unit to check, and a lamp to find a new home for, if it has to go."

"We don't have to find it a new home."

"We have to find out what it's worth," she said. "That's the only way I'm going to know whether my uncle was right about it, or whether he was just sentimental."

"He was sentimental," Owen said. "But he was never wrong about lamps."

"We'll see," Tessa said, and stood up, and brushed off the back of her jeans. "Storage unit. Nine o'clock. Bring coffee, and tell me everything you didn't tell me yesterday."

Owen looked up at her from the top step, the last of the apricot light on his face.

"I told you everything I could," he said.

"Then tell me the rest of it tomorrow," Tessa said, and went inside to find Moose a bowl of water, and to stand for a minute in the Dock Room alone, looking at the green shard on the windowsill and the closet lamp glowing on the floor, and trying to remember the exact sound of her uncle's voice saying *Tess, the light always tells the truth*.

## Chapter 8: Unit Nine

Pinecrest Storage sat at the edge of Route 9, between a farm-supply store and a field of sunflowers that had not yet decided whether to bloom. It was a low, corrugated complex of rust-colored doors laid out in four rows, and on Sunday morning it looked like a place where nothing was supposed to happen.

Owen was waiting by the gate with two coffees and a clipboard. He had changed into jeans and a faded green T-shirt that said LARKSPUR LAKE DAYS, 1998, and he looked, Tessa thought, younger than he had the day before, or perhaps simply less guarded.

"You're early," he said.

"You're earlier."

"I came at six," he admitted, and handed her a cup. "I wanted to be sure the unit was still there."

"Was it?"

"Yes," he said. "It's a storage unit. It's always there. That's the problem with storage units."

The north row was the last one before the fence line. Unit 9 had a red paint mark on its door, just as he had said, and the padlock was an old brass one, dulled by years of weather, with a scratch across its face where somebody had tried a screwdriver on it once. Owen fitted his key into the lock, turned it, and lifted the door with both hands. It went up with a long, grinding complaint, and the smell that came out was cedar and dust and the faint sweetness of old wool.

Tessa stepped inside and stopped.

It was not what she had expected. She had imagined a cramped room full of cardboard boxes and a few dusty pieces of furniture pushed against the walls. Instead she was looking at a carefully arranged space, lit by the sunlight that came through the open door, with a clear path down the center and every piece of furniture placed so that a visitor could see it from the entrance. A walnut writing desk stood against the back wall, its brass pulls polished. Two rattan chairs faced each other across a low pine table. A stack of framed prints leaned against the left wall, each one wrapped in tissue and labeled in pencil. On the right, a row of table lamps stood in a neat line on a long shelf, their shades covered in plastic, and beside them hung a row of brass tags, each on its own hook.

"Oh," Tessa said softly.

"Yeah," Owen said behind her.

She walked to the shelf and took down the nearest lamp. It was a ceramic urn with a cream linen shade, very much in the style her uncle had loved, and the tag on its base read *LR, Pruitt-Hale house, Oct 2024, return to U9*. Under that, in a different hand, someone had added in ballpoint: *Finished. O.R.*

"You wrote that," she said.

"I wrote that," Owen said. "After I put it back."

She took down a second lamp, then a third. Each one had a tag. Each tag had a room, a date, and a set of initials. Most of them said MH. A few said O.R., and a few had both sets, the MH crossed out and the O.R. written beneath. The brass floor lamp from the Dock Room had a twin on the shelf, a little smaller, its green shade intact and perfectly clean, and a tag that read *Dock Rm — spare — DO NOT SELL WITHOUT ASKING. MH. Replace shade from Racine.*

"That's the spare," Owen said. "Len bought two in the estate sale. He said one day he'd need the second one for a house with a bigger dock."

"There's no bigger dock on this lake."

"There's the Ostrander place," Owen said. "They have a dock the size of a parking lot. Len used to say he'd give his right arm to stage it."

Tessa turned the spare lamp in her hands. The brass was cool and heavy, and the green glass was smooth and whole, without a single crack. She set it carefully back on the shelf, then looked around the unit again, more slowly this time, and understood what she was seeing.

"You've been keeping everything organized," she said.

"I've been trying."

"Since when?"

"Since the first time I came in here by myself," Owen said. "Len took me in here in 2023 to show me the inventory. He opened the door, and he said, 'Owen, this is everything I have left that's worth a damn, and you're going to be the one who keeps it that way when I'm gone.' And then he handed me a box of tags and a roll of masking tape and walked back out to his truck without another word."

Tessa turned to look at him. He was standing just inside the door, a coffee cup in one hand and the clipboard tucked under his arm, and his face was turned toward the sunlight.

"He never told me that," she said.

"I know."

"Why not?"

Owen was quiet for a moment. "Because I think he wanted you to find it," he said. "Not all at once. He wanted you to walk in here and see the way it was kept, and know that somebody had been looking after it. And then he wanted you to be angry about it, and then he wanted you to decide what to do."

"That's a very Len thing to want."

"It was the only thing he ever wanted from anybody," Owen said quietly.

She walked to the writing desk and ran her hand along its edge. The walnut was smooth and cool, with the faint grain of wood that had been oiled once a season for a very long time. She opened the top drawer. Inside, there was a stack of brown envelopes, each one addressed in her uncle's square capitals. They were labeled with house numbers and street names, some from Larkspur Lake and some from farther away. *Harbor Road 3. Sunset Point 11. Cedar Point 4. Marina Condo, Unit 6. Birch Street, 14.*

She picked up the envelope for Birch Street and turned it over. The flap was not sealed. Inside, there was a single sheet of yellow legal paper, folded in thirds, and a small, flat object wrapped in tissue. She unwrapped the tissue carefully. It was a brass key, old and worn smooth, with a paper tag tied to its ring. The tag said, in pencil, *Birch St. front. Ada gave me this in 1997. She said I'd need it when she was gone and somebody had to let the light in.*

"Oh, Len," Tessa whispered.

Owen crossed the unit and stood beside her. He did not touch her. He looked at the key over her shoulder.

"He had a key to the house," he said. "Ada's key."

"Ada gave him one in 1997," Tessa said. "He kept it all this time, and never told the Pruitts."

"I don't think he needed to tell them," Owen said. "Ada was his friend. She knew he'd be the one who came to open the place up when she passed."

Tessa unfolded the yellow sheet. It was a list in her uncle's hand, dated the year before, with a column of room names down the left and a column of pieces down the right. Beside the Dock Room, he had written *brass lamp (O.R.'s), blue quilt, green chair, DO NOT PUT GRAY ANYWHERE.* Beside the living room, *sofa pair (2), cream, pillows, rag rug, both sofas to keep matched.* At the bottom of the sheet was a line she had to read twice.

*If Tess comes back, let her choose. If she doesn't, Owen keeps the Birch St work. Don't push her. She's got my temper and her mother's sense.*

She sat down hard on the edge of the walnut desk.

Owen did not say anything. He crouched beside her, so that his face was level with hers, and he waited.

"He wrote this last year," she said.

"Yes."

"He knew I was going to come back."

"He hoped you would," Owen said. "He didn't know. He used to say you'd never come back to the lake, and then in the next breath he'd say you'd be here before the first frost, because you couldn't stand to let a house go unsold." Owen's mouth twitched without humor. "He had a funny way of being right about everything and wrong about the important things."

"He wasn't wrong about me," Tessa said. "He just didn't think I'd come back alone."

Owen looked at her, and something moved in his face, quick and unguarded, before he got it back under control.

"Tessa," he said. "I'm going to tell you something, and you're going to be angry, and I want you to let me finish before you decide."

She folded the yellow sheet very carefully and put it back in the envelope with the key.

"Go on," she said.

"I'm not the only one who keeps this place," Owen said. "There's a second box, behind the desk, under the rag rug. It's got the rest of the notes. Len's real records. Not the spreadsheet I keep. Not the tags. The Ledger, the one Bev calls the Ledger. I've never opened it, Tessa. I've only seen the cover."

"Why haven't you opened it?"

"Because it has my name in it," Owen said. "And I wasn't sure you'd want me to be the one to read it first."

Tessa stood up. She walked around the walnut desk, knelt on the concrete floor, lifted the edge of the rag rug, and found a flat cardboard box pushed against the wall. It was sealed with packing tape. On the lid, in her uncle's capitals, were three words.

*FOR TESS. NOT YET.*

She looked up at Owen, who was standing in the doorway now, his shadow long across the floor.

"Not yet," she said.

"He wrote it on the lid two years ago," Owen said. "I asked him what it meant. He said you'd know when it was time."

"I don't know what time it is," Tessa said.

"Neither do I," Owen said. "But I think it's Sunday."

She laughed, a short, damp sound, and sat back on her heels with the box in her lap. Beyond the open door, a truck rumbled along Route 9 toward the lake, and somewhere beyond the sunflowers a dog barked twice and went quiet.

"Grant me the rest of the morning," she said. "Then we'll talk about the box."

"Okay," Owen said.

"And Owen."

"Yes?"

"Don't put the spare lamp back on the shelf yet. I want it in the Dock Room, next to the other one. If the Fenners buy the house, they should see both."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded and went to the shelf, and lifted the spare lamp down with both hands, as carefully as if he were carrying a child, and carried it out into the sunlight.

## Chapter 9: Dock Lights

The public pier at the foot of Dock Street was three hundred feet of weathered planks, with a row of iron lampposts down its spine and a bench at the end where, Owen said, every teenager in Larkspur Lake had once carved their name and then been forced to sand it off by the township.

At nine o'clock on Sunday night the lampposts came on all at once, with a soft electric hum, and the pier turned gold under them. The lake beyond was black and quiet. Beyond the breakwater, the running lights of a single sailboat moved slowly toward the horizon, and the cedars along the shore were dark walls against a sky full of stars.

Moose walked ahead of them with his nose to the boards, sniffing every crack.

"He's looking for something," Tessa said.

"He's looking for Len's bait," Owen said. "Len used to bring him out here every night after closing, and feed him a hot dog from the snack bar, and tell him he was going to be the first golden retriever in Wisconsin to get a fishing license. He never once got the license."

"Was Len a fisherman?"

"Len hated fishing. He said it was a way for men to sit still and pretend they were thinking. He came out here because the light is good at night, and because my father used to sit on this bench and drink beer after he closed the shop, and Len liked to sit with the man he was supposed to hate." Owen stopped at the railing and put his forearms on it. "They were friends. Nobody in this town believes it, but they were."

Tessa leaned beside him. The railing was cool under her arms, and the wood smelled faintly of creosote and lake water. "Tell me about your father."

"He ran Reyes Bait and Tackle, on the land right over there." Owen nodded toward the squat, gray-shingled shop at the foot of the pier, its windows dark and its sign still bolted above the door. "It's a sandwich shop now. Gus's cousin bought it when my dad left, and he never changed the sign because he said it was bad luck to take down a name that people still recognized. My dad left when I was twelve. He went to Duluth with a woman who sold insurance, and he sent a postcard every Christmas for three years, and then he stopped."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. He was a good bait man and a terrible husband. My mother got a job at the hospital laundry and worked nights for eleven years, so I could finish school and play baseball in the summer for the county team. Len was the one who came to the games. He'd sit in the bleachers in his canvas coat with a thermos and yell at the umpire, every single game, and my mother would send him home with a plate of her tamales because he never once went to the grocery store."

Tessa laughed softly. "He never cooked for himself. I used to get calls from Chicago on Sundays, and he'd be eating a can of baked beans and telling me about the weather on the lake."

"He ate beans for thirty years," Owen said. "He said it was the only food that never let him down."

They were quiet for a while. Moose came back down the pier, gave them both a long, reproachful look for ignoring him, and then sat down between their feet with a groan and rested his chin on Tessa's sneaker.

"He's decided," Owen said. "We're his people now."

"We're not his people," Tessa said. "We're the people who forgot to bring him a hot dog."

"That's most of what people are," Owen said.

She looked at him sideways. He was standing with his back to the lampposts, his face half in shadow and half in their gold light, and he was looking at her with a steadiness she was not used to. The men in Chicago did not look at her like that. They looked at her as though she were a problem to be solved, or a reference to be checked, or a woman who would be easy to leave behind when the project was over. Owen Reyes looked at her as though he were trying to memorize her face in case he never got the chance again.

"Why do you do this?" she asked.

"Do what?"

"Carry the couch. Stay in a town where your father left you a sandwich shop and a bad name. Sit on a pier with my uncle's dog." She shrugged, a little helplessly. "You could sell houses in Milwaukee. You could sell them in Chicago, for that matter. You'd make twice the money and nobody would know your mother's name."

Owen was silent for so long she thought he would not answer.

"Because this is where I'm from," he said at last. "And because somebody has to keep the light on for the people who leave. My father didn't. Len did. He kept the barn open for thirty years, and he took in every stray who came up the drive, and he never once asked anybody to pay him back." He looked out at the water. "I've been trying to do the same thing, Tessa. Keep the light on. Keep the good pieces in the right room. Make sure the buyers get the view. It's a stupid thing to want, and I've never been able to explain it to anybody who didn't grow up here."

"I think I understand it," Tessa said quietly.

"Do you?"

"I don't know." She pushed her hair back from her face. The breeze off the lake was cool and smelled of rain, though the sky was clear. "I grew up in Naperville with two parents and a house on a quiet street and a dog that died when I was eight. I was always the one who knew where everything went. I had a room, and I had a plan, and I had a job at a staging company where I worked seventy hours a week for a man who called me his best closer and never once asked whether I was happy. When Len died, I drove up here for the funeral, and I stood in the barn and saw his handwriting on every tag, and I felt like I'd been sleepwalking for ten years."

"And now?"

"And now I'm standing on a pier with the man who's been stealing my uncle's lamps," Tessa said, "and I can't decide whether I want to strangle him or kiss him."

Owen let out a short, startled laugh. "Those are both reasonable responses."

"Are they?"

"They're the two most reasonable things anyone's ever said to me on this pier," he said, "and I've had a lot of people say a lot of things to me on this pier."

She turned to face him fully. He was very close, closer than she had realized, and the lampposts threw his shadow across the planks between them, so that his face was half light and half dark, and she could see the faint stubble along his jaw and a small white scar above one eyebrow she had not noticed before.

"Owen," she said.

"Yes."

"You said you'd tell me everything."

"I said I'd tell you everything I could."

"You didn't tell me about the Ledger."

"I haven't read the Ledger," Owen said, very softly. "I told you that this morning. I've only seen the cover, and the box has your name on it."

"And you didn't tell me about the tag on the lamp."

"I told you about the lamp."

"You told me about the lamp," Tessa agreed. "You didn't tell me why you kept it in your own shop, under a sign that said DO NOT SELL, for two years, without ever once asking my uncle whether he'd rather you just took it out to a buyer and let it go."

He was quiet. She saw a muscle work in his jaw.

"Because I didn't want to sell it," he said at last. "And because it was the only thing Len ever gave me that wasn't a favor. He gave me the lamp when he gave me the key. He said I had to keep one thing that was mine, in case the rest of it went wrong. And I kept it, and I put it in the Dock Room, because the light was better there, and because I used to sit in that room every Thursday with him, and I could see the water, and I could pretend for an hour a week that I was a man with a house and a family and something to lose."

Tessa did not say anything. She could not. Her throat had tightened in a way she did not like, and she turned back to the railing and gripped it with both hands, staring out at the black water.

Owen came up beside her. He did not touch her. He stood close enough that she could feel the warmth of him through the thin cotton of her shirt, and she heard him breathe out, slowly, as though he had been holding something in for a very long time.

"Tessa," he said. "I'm not going to ask you for anything. I know what it looks like. I know I've been a pain in the neck since the moment you pulled into that drive. I know I took your uncle's props and kept them in a unit without asking, and I know I have a note you've never seen, for a loan you didn't know about, against a business you don't want to run. I know that's a lot to hand a woman on a Sunday night."

"It is."

"But I'm not going to pretend I don't want you to stay," Owen said. "Because I do. And I think my uncle wanted you to, and I think Bev wants you to, and I think that dog would sleep on your feet for the rest of his life if you let him. And I'm trying very hard not to let any of that make the decision for you."

Tessa turned her head. She looked at him in the gold light, the scar above his eyebrow, the green T-shirt with the old town festival printed across the chest, the way he held himself very still, as if he were afraid that if he moved, she would walk away.

She stepped closer.

It was a small step, half an inch, perhaps less. She felt the air change between them, the warmth of his shoulder, the faint smell of soap and sunlight on his skin. His eyes went to her mouth and then came back to her eyes, and something in his face opened, slowly and without any defense at all.

She stopped.

"Not yet," she said.

He breathed out, very quietly, and nodded. He did not move back. He did not look hurt. He only looked at her, steadily, as if he had expected exactly this and had made his peace with it before he ever walked out onto the pier.

"Okay," he said.

"It's not that I don't want to." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it. "It's that I don't know what I'm standing on. I don't know whether I'm going to sell this business, or stay in this town, or go back to Chicago and forget any of this ever happened. And I don't want to make a decision about you until I know what I'm deciding about everything else."

"That's the most sensible thing anyone's said to me all summer," Owen said.

"I don't feel sensible."

"Nobody does," he said. "That's why it's called a decision."

Moose stood up, stretched, and looked from one of them to the other with the disgusted patience of a dog who had sat through enough nonsense for one night. Then he trotted down the pier toward the bench at the end, sniffed the boards where the teenagers had carved their names, and lay down with his head on his paws.

"He's waiting for us," Owen said.

"He's waiting for a hot dog."

"He's waiting for you to make up your mind," Owen said, and his mouth curved, just slightly, at one corner. "He's a very patient dog. He's been waiting for two years for somebody to tell him the truth about the cot."

"What truth?"

"That Len never slept on it," Owen said. "He only kept it for the dog."

Tessa laughed in spite of herself, a wet, surprised sound that echoed off the water.

"I want to open the box," she said.

"Now?"

"No. On Friday. After Monday." She wiped her face with the back of her hand and took a breath that shook only a little. "I want to walk into Ray Pruitt's office with my uncle's notes in my head and my own two feet on the floor, and I want to know exactly what I'm fighting for before I find out what I'm fighting against."

"Okay," Owen said. "Friday."

"And Owen."

"Yes?"

"Don't put any more tags on anything until I say so."

"I wouldn't dream of it," he said, and walked down the pier beside her, under the gold lampposts, while the sailboat's running lights slid out of sight beyond the breakwater and the lake lay black and calm around them, waiting, the way her uncle had always said the water waited, for somebody honest enough to look at it properly.

## Chapter 10: Two Proposals

Ray Pruitt's office was on the second floor of a yellow brick building on Front Street, above a hardware store that had been there since before the lake had a name, and it smelled of furniture polish and old paper. Nora Pruitt sat at the head of the conference table in a navy linen suit, a glass of iced tea sweating beside her and a legal pad on the table in front of her, covered in neat, slanted notes. She had flown in from Phoenix that morning. She looked, Tessa thought, like a woman who had spent thirty years telling people their eyes were fine and was now finding that her own patience had a limit.

"Sit down, both of you," Nora said, without looking up.

They sat, one on either side of the table, with an empty chair between them that nobody had mentioned. Tessa had her contract in a plastic sleeve, the placemat folded in her bag, and her uncle's yellow sheet of legal paper pressed flat in the front pocket of the folder. Owen had a laptop, a printed spreadsheet, a stack of listing photos, and an expression of perfect, unbothered composure. He had not slept much. She could tell from the shadows under his eyes, and from the way he had stood on the sidewalk outside the building for ten minutes before coming up the stairs, looking at the traffic on Front Street without seeing any of it.

Ray came in last, with a cup of coffee in each hand and a sheepish look. He set one in front of his sister and the other in front of the empty chair, then sat down beside her.

"Okay," Nora said. "Here's the situation." She picked up her pen. "My aunt Ada died in January. She left the cottage on Birch Street to me and my brother, with a note in her will that says the house is to be sold by the end of the summer, staged the way her friend Len Marsh would have staged it, and never to be listed by anyone who doesn't know the lake. Len was supposed to do the staging. Len is dead. I have two people in front of me who both claim to be the answer to that sentence, and they've been fighting in my aunt's house for a week. Tell me why I shouldn't choose one of you and be done with it."

"Because we can do it better together," Owen said.

"You're going to say that."

"I'm going to say that," Owen agreed, "because it's true, and because you'd be a fool to hire one of us without the other. But I'm going to say it after I show you the numbers."

Nora raised an eyebrow. "Go on."

Owen opened his laptop and turned it toward her. Tessa could see the screen from where she sat: a clean grid of listing photos, a column of comparable sales from the north shore, and a spreadsheet tab labeled *Birch St — Projected Sale*. He walked Nora through it quickly, without notes, his voice calm and precise. The cottage had been priced at $589,000, which was fair for the lot and the view, but a little low for the photographs. Owen wanted to raise the asking price by $25,000 after the staging was finished, and he had three comparable sales from the summer to prove it could be done. The Fenners were already interested. They had walked the house twice. Their offer was contingent on the furniture, the blue quilt, and a new shade on the brass lamp, but they were otherwise prepared to pay full price within thirty days of signing.

"That's the best offer we've had since April," Ray said, looking impressed.

"It's the only offer we've had since April," Nora said dryly. "Keep going."

"The listing needs a full photography package," Owen said. "Twenty-four photos, two drone shots of the lake from the pier, and a video walk-through at four in the afternoon, which is when the light comes off the water. Len wrote that into his notes for every house on this street. He was right. The light is the sale. Nobody in Chicago buys a view they haven't seen in golden hour."

Nora's pen moved across the legal pad. "And the staging?"

Owen was silent for a moment. Then he turned his head and looked at Tessa across the empty chair.

"The staging is hers," he said. "She's the stager. I carry the couch."

Nora looked up. "You said that to the Fenners."

"I said that to the Fenners," Owen agreed, "and I meant it. Tessa has been a senior stager in Chicago for nine years. She knows how to make a room look like somebody lives in it, and she knows which lamp to put in which window, and she knows that the blue quilt is better than the gray in every light except the one that comes off a Chicago bank. Len taught her, and I don't think I could do it half as well."

It was the first time he had called her uncle *Len*, and Tessa realized he had not meant to say it that way, that the word had slipped out in the presence of the Pruitts and the glass of iced tea and the legal pad, as if the man himself were sitting in the empty chair. She saw Ray glance at her, then at Owen, then down at the table.

"Ms. Marsh," Nora said. "Your pitch."

Tessa opened the plastic sleeve and laid the contract on the table. She did not look at the papers. She looked at Nora.

"The staging is a full install for a three-bedroom cottage," she said. "We put the sofas in the living room, where the light from the front windows catches the cream linen, and we put the second sofa on the porch, facing the lake, for the exterior shots. We put the blue quilt in the Dock Room, on the iron bed, because the cold light in that room needs something warm to pull it. We put the brass floor lamp in the window where the sun comes off the water at four. We stage the kitchen with the rag rug, because it's the only thing in the house that looks like it's been used. We put a dining table on the porch, under the birches, set for four. We fix the drip under the vanity, because a buyer will find it in ninety seconds and never forgive the house. And we turn on every lamp in the place at the same time for the walk-through, so that a buyer walks into a room that already feels like it has been loved."

"That's a lot of lamps," Ray said.

"There are eleven," Tessa said. "I counted them twice."

Nora's pen stopped moving.

"Why did you count them twice?"

"Because one of them broke on Saturday," Tessa said, "and I wanted to be sure the others were still there."

She heard Owen let out a long, quiet breath beside her. She did not look at him.

Nora was silent for a long moment. Then she set down her pen and picked up her iced tea, took a sip, and set it back down, precisely, in the ring of condensation it had already left on the table.

"Ray," she said. "Pull up the will."

Ray opened a manila folder, flipped through it, and pulled out a single sheet of paper, several pages thick with legal language. He read a line, frowned, read it again, and set it down.

"It's the same as always," he said. "The house goes to Nora and me. The sale goes to whoever we say. The staging is to be done by Leonard Marsh or by a person he selects." He looked up at Tessa. "And there's a letter."

"A letter?"

"Len sent it to Nora in January," Ray said. "Before he went into the hospital. It came in an envelope with the lake postmark, and it was addressed to Nora personally, and she never opened it until last week, because she thought it was a bill."

Nora pulled a folded sheet of paper from the inside pocket of her jacket. It was yellow, like the sheet in the envelope at Unit 9, and written in the same square capitals.

"He said I was to show this to whoever came to the table," Nora said. "He said they'd know what it meant."

She unfolded it and read aloud, in a clear, precise voice that made the words sound like a legal notice.

"'Nora. The Birch Street staging is in good hands, whether the hands are mine or my niece's or Owen's. There is a debt in the Marsh books that was never written down properly, and it is Owen's to claim and Tess's to settle, and neither of them will like it. Tell them to sit down together and count the lamps. Tell them I said the light always tells the truth. And tell Tess she was right about the blue quilt, and I was wrong, and I'm sorry I never said so.'"

The room was very quiet.

Tessa stared at the yellow paper in Nora's hand. The words blurred. She blinked and they came back into focus, and she saw that her hands had gone cold on the table.

"He was wrong about the quilt," Owen said quietly. "He was never wrong about the quilt."

"He wrote 'I was wrong,'" Tessa said, and her voice did not sound like her own. "He never once in his life said he was wrong about anything."

"He said it in January," Owen said. "When he knew."

Nora folded the letter back along its crease and set it on the table between them, face up.

"I don't care who's right about the quilt," she said. "I don't care who owns the lamp. I care that my aunt's house is sold before Labor Day, for the price the Fenners will pay, to people who are going to love it the way Ada did. And I care that I'm not going to sit at this table for another week listening to two people I don't trust argue over a sofa." She turned to Tessa. "I'm giving you the staging. Exclusively. Full install, every lamp, the drip, the rug, the quilt, the whole thing. You have until Thursday to finish it."

Tessa felt something loosen in her chest, a knot she had not known was there. She nodded. "Thanks."

"Don't thank me yet. I haven't finished." Nora turned to Owen. "You're the listing agent. The asking price goes up $25,000 after the staging is done, not before. You shoot the photos at four o'clock on Thursday, with the lamp on, and you do the drone shot from the pier, and you don't touch a single piece of furniture without asking Ms. Marsh first."

"Yes, ma'am," Owen said.

"And you pay Ms. Marsh the loan," Nora said, "from the commission on this house. Ray and I will hold the difference in escrow until the two of you agree on the number. If you don't agree by Labor Day, I'll have my lawyer in Phoenix call both of you and tell you what the number is, and you won't like it."

Owen's face did not change. But Tessa saw his hands, resting on the closed laptop, go very still.

"Yes, ma'am," he said again.

"Good." Nora picked up her pen and wrote something on the legal pad, then underlined it twice. "Now, I want the two of you to walk out of this building together, and I want you to go to the diner and have a cup of coffee, and I want you to count the lamps again on your way. And when you're done, I want you to tell me, in writing, what you decided about that brass lamp."

"We'll decide," Tessa said.

"You'll decide before Thursday," Nora said. "Because I'm going to be at that house on Thursday at four o'clock, sitting on the second sofa, watching the light come off the water, and I don't want to see either of you trying to sell it out from under the other one. Is that clear?"

"Yes," Tessa said.

"Yes," Owen said.

Nora closed the legal pad. Ray stood up, pushed in his chair, and held the door for them with a rueful look, the look of a man who had just learned that his sister had been right all along.

On the landing, Tessa stopped. The stairwell smelled of floor wax, and a single window above the door let in a bar of white light that lay across the worn treads. Owen stopped beside her. They did not speak for a long moment.

"He wrote 'I was wrong,'" Tessa said again.

"I heard him."

"He wrote 'I'm sorry.'"

"He did."

"He never once said he was sorry about anything in his whole life, Owen. Not to my mother. Not to me. Not to you, when you were twelve and your father left and he came to the games and yelled at the umpire for three years straight." She put her hand against the wall. The plaster was cool. "What did he do to you?"

Owen looked at her steadily, the light falling across his face.

"He taught me to count the lamps," he said. "And he made me promise to tell you the truth when you asked the right question."

"I asked you the right question on Sunday."

"You did."

"And you didn't answer it."

"No," Owen said. "I didn't. Not all of it."

Tessa took her hand off the wall. The white light lay between them on the stairs, and she could hear, faintly, Ray Pruitt's voice rising from the office above, and Nora's voice answering, sharp and certain, the way a woman's voice sounds when she has been waiting her whole life to be the one who decides.

"Then tell me the rest," Tessa said. "Not on a pier. Not in a unit. Right here, on the stairs, where I can see your face."

Owen took a breath, let it out, and did not look away from her.

"Forty cents on the dollar," he said.

## Chapter 11: Forty Cents on the Dollar

"Explain it," Tessa said.

Owen did not sit down. He stood on the landing with one hand resting on the banister and the white light from the window falling across his shoulders, and he told her in the same level voice he had used for the comparable sales, as if the facts themselves were the only thing he could trust.

"In the spring of 2023," he said, "your uncle was short on cash. He'd bought a Stickley desk at an estate sale in Milwaukee that he thought was worth four times what he paid, and it turned out to be a very good reproduction with a fake brass plate. He lost eleven thousand on it. He had a lease coming up on the second storage unit, and the barn roof was going to need work by the fall, and he had two commissions outstanding that wouldn't pay until the next year. So he came to me, because I was the only person in town he trusted with a checkbook, and he asked me to lend him thirty thousand dollars."

"And you did."

"I did. I had the money from selling my condo in Oshkosh, and I'd been sitting on it, not knowing what to do. He wrote me a note on the back of a Gus's menu, and I signed it, and he put it in the Ledger." Owen's jaw tightened. "But the note wasn't the whole thing. He wanted something more than a loan. He wanted somebody who'd carry the inventory and keep it straight. Somebody who'd put his name on the tags for the pieces that were going out on Pinecrest listings, and who'd take a cut when they sold. He said he didn't have the time or the knees for it anymore, and he didn't want to sell the business to a stranger."

"So he gave you forty cents on the dollar."

"He gave me forty cents of every net dollar," Owen said quietly. "Of every sale that came out of the barn or Unit 9, for as long as I kept the books and carried the couch. It was written on the back of the same menu. He signed it, and he dated it, and he said that if he died before he paid the loan back, the forty cents on the dollar would be mine to keep until you decided what you wanted to do with the business. And then he said, 'Tess will do what she wants. She always does. But you'll have something to say about it, because you'll be the one holding the tags.'"

Tessa put her hand against the wall again. The plaster was cool and rough under her palm.

"Forty cents on the dollar," she repeated.

"Of the net. Not the inventory. Not the barn. Not the Birch Street job, which was never in the agreement, because Len didn't sign the staging contract until January of this year." Owen looked away from her for the first time, out the window, where the sun was catching the tops of the parked cars on Front Street. "He put it in writing, Tessa. In his capitals, on the back of a menu from a diner he swore served the worst coffee in the county, with his initials and the date. I never once thought to tell you, because I didn't know what it was worth, and I didn't know whether he'd ever meant for it to be worth anything at all. He was a very sentimental man, and he was almost always wrong about money."

"And the thirty thousand?"

"I don't know if it's been paid. He never said. The Ledger has the numbers, I think. That's what Bev says. She says he used to write down every dollar he ever made, and every dollar he ever owed, in a green cloth notebook, and she read it once when she was helping him balance the books in 2019 and got sick to her stomach about how much he'd lost on the Stickley desk." He turned back to her. "I've been carrying a debt I didn't know the size of, and a share in a business I didn't own, and I've been waiting two years for somebody to tell me to stop. And you're the first person who ever asked me the right question, and I still didn't answer it."

"You said you were a creditor," Tessa said. "On the porch. You said it was a handshake and a piece of paper."

"It is a handshake and a piece of paper," Owen said. "That's the problem. It's not a partnership, and it's not a loan, and it's not a gift. It's Len's idea of what fair looks like, and he wrote it down in a way that only makes sense to a man who has never once in his life filed a tax return on time."

Tessa laughed, a short, broken sound that surprised her.

"That's Len," she said.

"That's Len."

"Forty cents of every net dollar," she said slowly. "Of the business I just inherited. Which I haven't even read the will for, because I've been too busy hauling sofas and fighting over lamps with a man who's been sleeping in a storage unit." She pushed away from the wall. "Did you ever think about just asking me, Owen? In February? At the funeral lunch? When I was standing in the parking lot with a cup of cold coffee, and you were standing six feet away with that menu in your pocket?"

"I did think about it."

"And?"

"And I thought you were going to tell me to go to hell," Owen said. "And I thought you'd be right."

She did not answer. Below them, the front door of the hardware store opened and closed, and a bell rang faintly, and a man's voice called out something about a box of screws. Upstairs, Ray and Nora were still talking in low voices behind the frosted glass of the office door.

"I'm not angry at you," Tessa said finally.

"You should be."

"I am. But I'm not angry about the lamp, or the storage unit, or the tags." She swallowed. "I'm angry that my uncle wrote a deal on the back of a menu and didn't have the decency to tell me about it in the hospital. I'm angry that he left the business to me with a debt in it that he never explained, and a note that says I'll have to decide what to do about a man I met for the first time a week ago. I'm angry that everybody in this town seems to have known my uncle better than I did, and nobody thought to tell me until I was standing on a staircase with a woman from Phoenix reading me his letter."

"Everybody in this town loved him," Owen said. "That's not the same as knowing him."

"No," Tessa said. "It's worse. It's the same thing with more witnesses."

He almost smiled. Then he did not.

"Can I say one thing that's not about the money?" he asked.

"You're going to say it anyway."

"I'm going to say it because it's the only thing I've got left," Owen said. "He was right about you. He wrote that you'd be the one who came back, and he wrote that you'd want to decide everything for yourself, and he wrote that you had his temper and your mother's sense. He was right about all of that. And he was right about the quilt. He was even right about the lamp, though I hated to admit it until last week."

"And what does that mean?"

"It means he trusted you with the decision," Owen said. "He didn't trust me to make it for you. He didn't trust Bev, or Gus, or the Pruitts. He trusted you. He just didn't trust himself to tell you in time."

Tessa looked at him for a long moment. The light from the window was warm on her face, and she could see, in his eyes, a fear she had not expected: not of her anger, but of the silence she might leave behind if she walked away.

"I need coffee," she said.

"Okay."

"Real coffee. Not whatever Gus is pouring this week."

"Gus is pouring the same coffee he's always poured," Owen said. "It's the rest of the town that changed."

They went down the stairs together. At the bottom, Owen held the door open and waited for her to step through, and she did, and the bright noon light hit her full in the face, and for a moment she could not see anything but the glare off the cars and the water beyond.

"Tessa," he said quietly, behind her.

She turned around.

"I've wanted to tell you that for two years," Owen said. "And I didn't want it to be on a staircase. I wanted it to be somewhere you could sit down."

"Then sit down with me," Tessa said. "At Gus's. In the booth by the window. And tell me the rest of it, all of it, and don't make it sound better than it is."

"I won't."

"And don't call me your partner."

"I wasn't going to."

"Good," Tessa said. "Because I haven't decided what I am to you yet, and I'm not going to be told by a dead man's menu."

Owen was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded, once, and something in his face eased that had been clenched since the pier.

"Okay," he said. "Then I'll tell you what he never got around to saying. And you can decide what it's worth."

They walked down Front Street side by side, not touching, past the hardware store and the bank with its brass clock, past a woman walking a pair of beagles and a man on a bicycle who waved at Owen and then looked at Tessa with frank, delighted curiosity. At the corner, the Dock Street Diner sat under its striped awning with its windows open to the warm air, and inside, through the glass, Tessa could see Gus behind the counter, pouring coffee, and Bev in the back booth, lifting a hand as if she had been expecting them all along.

Tessa's phone buzzed in her pocket.

She took it out and looked at the screen. The name on it made her stop walking.

*BRENT MALLOY — LAKESHORE STAGING.*

Owen glanced at the phone, then at her face.

"Who's that?" he asked.

"My old boss," Tessa said, staring at the screen as it buzzed again in her hand. "He's never called me on a Monday in nine years."

"Do you want to answer it?"

She looked up at him, and then at the diner, and then at the long, blue line of the lake beyond the rooftops, where a single white sail was tacking slowly toward the breakwater.

"No," she said. "But I think I have to."

She let it ring twice more, then answered.

"Tessa Marsh," she said.

"Tessa!" said Brent Malloy, in the bright, warm, unmistakable voice of a man who had already decided how the next five minutes were going to go. "Glad I caught you. How's the lake? I hear you've got a situation up there, with a realtor and a dog."

Tessa closed her eyes.

"Word travels fast in Wisconsin," she said.

"It does," Brent agreed cheerfully. "Listen, I want to talk to you about the business. I've got a number, and I think you're going to like it."

## Chapter 12: The Number

Brent Malloy did not believe in small talk, and he did not believe in phone calls that lasted longer than four minutes. He had told Tessa this on her first day at Northline Staging, nine years earlier, when she had been twenty-five and terrified and holding a box of cheap pens. *Say the number, say the reason, and then shut up and let the other person think,* he had said, and she had done exactly that for nine years, and it had made her very good at her job and very bad at her life.

She went into the diner, slid into the booth by the window, and held the phone to her ear while Owen sat across from her with his hands folded on the table and a cup of coffee going cold between them. Bev, two booths over, had stopped pretending to read her newspaper.

"A hundred and sixty-five thousand," Brent said.

Tessa said nothing.

"For the name, the inventory, the barn lease, and your client list. Marsh Home Staging becomes a Northline Staging location. You keep your title, you run the Milwaukee book, and you get a salary, a car, and a share of the north-shore accounts. I've already spoken to the lawyers. The paper is ready. All you have to do is sign it and get on the road."

"A hundred and sixty-five thousand dollars," Tessa repeated.

"Plus the sofa," Brent said, as if he were making a joke, and then, when she did not laugh, "I'm kidding. The sofa stays with the house. I hear you've got two of them now, and I hear one of them has a dog on it."

"Where did you hear that?"

"Tessa, you're in a town of four thousand people, and the Pruitts have a niece in my accounts payable department." He sounded pleased with himself. "I'm not trying to take advantage. I know what your uncle's business was worth, and I know what he owed. I know he had a thing with a local realtor, and I know that realtor is going to want a piece of anything you sell. This is clean. This is you walking away with money in your pocket, and a job you'll be good at, and nobody in Larkspur Lake having to explain anything to anybody."

Owen, across the table, did not move. But Tessa saw his fingers tighten very slightly on the rim of his cup.

"How long do I have to decide?" she asked.

"Friday," Brent said. "The buyer's lawyers want a signature by close of business. I told them you'd call back this afternoon."

"You said the paper was ready."

"It is ready."

"Then you'll wait until Friday," Tessa said, "and I'll call you when I've read it."

There was a short silence on the line. She could hear, faintly, the sound of Brent's office on the other end: a phone ringing, a door closing, the murmur of a voice she recognized as Regina, his assistant of seven years, saying something about a call on line two.

"You've been up there a week," Brent said at last. "You're not really thinking about staying in Wisconsin."

"I'm thinking about reading the contract," Tessa said. "That's all I'm thinking about right now."

"Tessa, you're the best closer I ever hired. I need you in Milwaukee by August. The Blackwood Group is building four lake houses on the north shore, and they want a stager who knows the water. You're the only one who does."

"I know the water," Tessa said. "I've been in it for a week."

Brent laughed, and it was not an unkind sound. "Friday," he said again. "Phone me on Thursday if you want to talk it through. And Tessa? Don't let a man with a dog and a menu make you forget what you're good at."

The line went dead.

She set the phone face down on the table. Her hands were shaking, very slightly, and she put them flat on the cool laminate to stop it.

Owen did not say anything. He only reached across the table and slid his cup of coffee an inch closer to her, as if it were the only thing in the room that could be trusted to stay still.

"How did he know about the menu?" she asked.

"I have no idea," Owen said.

"Did you tell anyone?"

"I told Ray. I told Nora, through Ray. I told Bev, because Bev already knew." He looked at her steadily. "I didn't tell Brent Malloy, Tessa. I've never met him. I don't think I've ever heard his name said out loud."

"Then who told him about the sofa?"

Bev, from two booths over, cleared her throat with great dignity.

"That would be me," she said.

Tessa turned around in her seat. Bev Halvorsen was sitting with her newspaper folded on her knee and a blueberry muffin on a napkin in front of her, and she looked, for once, not at all cheerful.

"You called my boss?" Tessa said.

"I called the Northline office in Chicago on Thursday," Bev said calmly. "I got the number from your uncle's Rolodex, which I borrowed in April and never returned. I told the woman who answered that you were in Larkspur Lake, that you were trying to save your uncle's business, and that a man named Brent Malloy had been calling your uncle every Tuesday since 2019 to offer him money for the good pieces in the barn." She picked up her muffin and broke it in half. "Your uncle always said no. He said Chicago was for people who didn't know how to get their hands dirty. I thought somebody ought to tell you the offer was on the table before you signed anything in a hurry."

"You didn't have to do that."

"I know I didn't." Bev took a bite of the muffin, chewed thoughtfully, and swallowed. "But somebody had to tell you what was happening, and your uncle wasn't going to, and Owen was too proud to say it, and Gus doesn't like phones. So I did it. I'm seventy-one years old, dear. I've earned the right to be the meddling old woman on the block."

Tessa stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed, and the laugh turned into something closer to a sob, and she pressed the back of her wrist against her mouth until it passed.

"You're the most infuriating woman I've ever met," she said.

"Your uncle said the same thing," Bev said cheerfully. "Every Sunday for thirty years. Now eat your muffin. Gus made them fresh, and he's going to be insulted if you don't."

Gus appeared at the end of the booth with a plate and set it down in front of Tessa. It was a blueberry muffin, still warm, with a pat of butter melting on top. He did not say anything. He only put a second plate in front of Owen, and then stood back, arms folded, looking from one of them to the other.

"Eat," he said.

They ate. It was surprisingly good. Tessa had not realized how hungry she was, or how long it had been since she'd eaten anything that had not come out of a vending machine in a Chicago office lobby. The muffin was dense and sweet and tasted like a Wisconsin summer, and when she was halfway through it she looked up and saw Owen watching her, his own muffin forgotten on the plate.

"What?" she said.

"Not a thing," Owen said. "You've got butter on your chin."

She wiped her chin with a napkin, glaring at him. He grinned, and the grin softened into something she was beginning to recognize as tenderness, and she looked away quickly, out the window, at the rooftops and the lake and the sail still moving slowly toward the breakwater.

Her phone buzzed again.

*LUCY: Did you call him back? Tess, a hundred and sixty-five is a lot of money. Don't make me say it.*

She typed with her thumb, slowly.

*What would you do?*

*I'd take the money and move to Milwaukee and find a man who owns a boat that doesn't leak. I'd call my mother. I'd stop answering emails on Sunday.*

*You'd take the money.*

*I'd take the money,* Lucy wrote, *because you have been doing this job since you were twenty-five, and you are the most talented person I know, and you've never once asked yourself whether you liked it. But that's me. What do you want?*

Tessa looked at the question for a long time.

She thought about the barn on Quarry Road, with its red paint and its hayloft full of Len's old cot and Moose's blankets. She thought about the Dock Room with the blue quilt and the brass lamp and the closet lamp burning on the floor, and the view of the water through the cedars. She thought about the tags on the inventory, all those brass tags with her uncle's capitals and Owen's ballpoint, *Finished*, and the way the room in Unit 9 had felt when she walked in, every piece of furniture placed so that a visitor could see it from the door.

She thought about a yellow legal sheet with her name on it. *Leave her to choose.*

*I don't know yet,* she typed. *But I think I want to find out before Friday.*

*Good,* Lucy wrote. *Then don't sign anything until you do. And Tess? Eat something. You sound like you haven't eaten since Tuesday.*

*I just ate a muffin.*

*Good girl. Send me a picture of the dog.*

Tessa put the phone down and looked across the table. Owen had finished his muffin and was watching her with the careful, patient expression of a man who had decided not to ask a question until he was sure it would be welcome.

"Brent wants an answer Friday," she said.

"I heard."

"He thinks I'm going to sell."

"Are you?"

"I don't know," Tessa said. "That's the problem. I've never not known what I was going to do. Not once in nine years. And now I've got a box in a storage unit that says NOT YET, and a dead man's menu in your pocket, and a woman from Phoenix who wants the house sold by Labor Day, and a hundred and sixty-five thousand dollars on the table that would pay off your thirty thousand with money to spare, and I'm sitting in a diner with butter on my chin, trying to figure out which one of those things is the truth."

Owen was quiet for a long moment.

"The money would pay my note," he said at last. "Every dollar of it. And the forty cents on the dollar, if Len meant it the way I think he did, would come out of your hands and into mine, and you'd walk away with the rest and a job in Milwaukee."

"I know."

"You could do that," Owen said. "And nobody in this town would blame you."

"I know that too."

"But I don't think that's what he wanted," Owen said, and his voice was very quiet, "and I don't think it's what you want either. I think you want to know what's in the box. And I think you want to know whether you can stand in the Dock Room at four in the afternoon, with the light off the water and the blue quilt on the bed, and say the house is yours."

Tessa looked at him, then down at her hands, still flat against the laminate.

"Friday," she said. "We open the box on Friday."

"Friday," Owen agreed.

"And you're not going to pretend you didn't just tell me the truth about the note."

"I'm not going to pretend anything," Owen said. "I'm done with that."

Across the diner, Bev Halvorsen lifted her coffee cup in a silent toast. Gus, at the counter, shook his head and muttered something about phones. And somewhere outside, in the warm June air, a dog barked twice, once for Moose and once for the sun, and then was quiet.

Tessa picked up the last bite of her muffin, looked at it for a moment, and ate it.

## Chapter 13: Ask First

On Thursday morning, Tessa wrote three words on the inside cover of her uncle's yellow legal pad, and underlined them twice, and then taped the page to the refrigerator in the Birch Street kitchen where no one could miss it.

*ASK FIRST.*

Owen saw it at seven fifteen, when he came in through the back door with two coffees and a box of Bev's cinnamon rolls, and he stood in front of the refrigerator for a long moment, reading it, before he set the coffees down on the counter.

"Who's it for?" he asked.

"Both of us," Tessa said. "Every piece that moves. Every lamp, every rug, every pillow. If one of us wants to touch something, we ask the other one first, and we wait for the answer."

"That's going to take all day."

"Then we'll start early."

He picked up his coffee, took a sip, and looked at her over the rim of the cup. She was standing at the sink in a faded blue work shirt, her hair pinned up off her neck, her sleeves rolled to the elbow. She had not slept well. She could tell from the shadows under her eyes and from the way she kept touching the edge of the counter, as if to be sure it was still there.

"Did you call Brent?" he asked.

"At six. I told him I'd give him an answer on Monday, and he said Monday was fine as long as I was in Milwaukee by the first of August." She turned off the faucet. "I didn't tell him I was staying. I didn't tell him I was going. I told him I'd read the contract over the weekend, and that I wanted to see the house one more time in the light before I decided anything."

"Is that true?"

"It's the only true thing I've said to Brent Malloy in nine years," Tessa said. "I want to see the house in the light."

Owen nodded slowly, set his cup down, and walked to the refrigerator. He peeled the yellow page carefully away from the magnet, folded it in half, and slid it into the pocket of his button-down, next to the placemat.

"I'm keeping this one too," he said.

"That's my list."

"It's our list," Owen said. "And it's the first sensible thing anybody's written in this house since Len's notes on the grocery list. Ask first. I'll ask you what I want to move, and you'll ask me what you want to move, and we'll stop fighting about lamps." He held out his hand, palm up, the way he had on the first morning. "Can I move the second sofa?"

"Why?"

"Because it's facing the wrong way for the four o'clock light, and Nora is going to sit on it in six hours, and if it's wrong she'll say so in front of the photographer."

"You want to turn the sofa."

"I want to turn the sofa," Owen said. "Can I?"

Tessa looked at his hand. She thought about the placemat, and the yellow page, and the way he had said *ask* the night before on the stairs at Ray's office, as if it were a word that had never once been used in the Marsh family.

"Yes," she said. "Turn it. Two inches to the left. Not one more."

"Thanks."

"And ask me before you touch the rag rug, because I've decided it goes under the dining table after all."

"Can I ask you about the rug now?"

"No," Tessa said. "You can ask me at two."

He laughed, and turned to the living room with a quick shake of his head, and she heard the scrape of the sofa's walnut feet on the floorboards and the soft grunt of effort as he moved it. Moose, who had been sleeping under the kitchen table, lifted his head at the noise and sighed.

By nine o'clock they had asked each other about forty questions. Owen wanted to move the green chair from the porch to the Dock Room, and Tessa said no, but he could move the cushion. Tessa wanted to hang Len's sailboat painting over the fireplace, the ugliest thing in the county, in Owen's opinion, and Owen said she could hang it as long as she let him photograph it from the worst possible angle. They argued briefly about whether the dining table should be set for four or for two, and settled on three, because Bev had announced she was coming for the photographs whether they liked it or not and would expect a place.

At ten thirty, Ray Pruitt called. He wanted to know whether the drone pilot from Madison was going to show up, and whether Nora was going to be on time, and whether the rumor that the Fenners were bringing their own photographer was true. Owen answered every question with a patience Tessa had not seen in him before, and when he hung up, he said, "Nora is going to be early."

"She's always early," Tessa said. "Len told me that. He said she came to every Pruitt family dinner with a clipboard."

"She came to his staging appointments with a clipboard, too," Owen said. "He told me once she was the only client he ever wanted back."

At noon, Bev came up the drive with a pie on each arm. One was cherry and one was blueberry, both cooling under dish towels, and she set them on the porch rail with the satisfaction of a general placing artillery.

"For the photographs," she said. "The cherry is for Nora. She'll pretend she doesn't want it. The blueberry is for you two, because I know you haven't eaten since Tuesday, and Gus says that's not healthy for either of you."

"Gus says a lot of things," Owen said.

"Gus is right about most things, and wrong about the pie crust." Bev lifted her chin toward the living room. "Now, the sofa. It's two inches left, is it?"

"How did you know?"

"Because you were moving it at seven fifteen, and I could hear you swearing from my kitchen," Bev said serenely. "Len used to swear at that sofa every single time he set it down. He said it was the only piece of furniture in the county that talked back."

At one thirty, Priscilla Fenner's silver sedan came up the drive. Warren got out first, in a crisp white shirt and khakis, and Priscilla followed, carrying a small paper box tied with twine. She walked straight past the sofa and the porch and the pie on the rail, and she came to Tessa in the middle of the lawn, and she held out the box.

"Closet lamp," she said. "I'm sorry, I had to ask. I know you said it wasn't for sale, but I bought you a replacement cord. A new one, brown, from the hardware store on Front. Dennis threw in a plug."

Tessa took the box. Inside was a neat coil of brown cord, a plug, and a card in Priscilla's round handwriting.

*For the crooked one. Thanks for letting us in.*

"That's kind," Tessa said.

"It's practical," Priscilla said, "and I want the lamp. I'm going to ask, and I'm going to be polite about it, and then I'm going to wait while you and Owen decide, because I can see you're going to decide together." She looked at the two of them, and her face softened. "Warren and I talked about it all the way from Oak Park. We'd pay six hundred and fifty dollars for the brass lamp in the Dock Room, with the green shade. We know it's not for sale. We'd pay the asking price on the house with the lamp included. We'd even pay for a new shade, and a dimmer, and whatever else it needed. Or we'd leave it exactly where it is, and never touch it, and let you two keep it, if that's what the note says."

Tessa looked at Owen. Owen was standing a few feet away with his hands in his pockets, and he was not looking at her. He was looking at the Dock Room window, where the brass lamp stood unlit in the corner, its green shade catching the noon light off the lake.

"Owen," Tessa said.

He turned.

"Priscilla's asking whether she can buy the lamp."

"I heard."

"Do you want to sell it?"

He was silent for a long moment. Then he said, very carefully, "Do you?"

"I asked you first."

"I know," Owen said. "But I want to hear you say it."

Tessa felt the old tightness come back into her chest, the sense of a plan slipping sideways. She looked at the lamp in the window. She thought about the Ledger, which she had not yet read, and the box marked NOT YET, which she had not yet opened, and the brass tag screwed to the stand that said DO NOT SELL WITHOUT ASKING, in her uncle's capitals and Owen's ballpoint, and the faint, sweet smell of cedar in Unit 9.

"No," she said. "I don't want to sell it. Not today. Not until we know what it is, and what it's supposed to be, and who it's supposed to belong to."

Owen let out a breath, slowly, as though he had been holding it since the morning.

"Then that's my answer too," he said.

Priscilla looked from one of them to the other, and then she laughed, a warm, delighted sound, and clapped her hands once.

"Oh, I like you two," she said. "Warren, did you hear that? They're not going to sell it."

"I heard," Warren said from the porch, where he had been standing with his hands in his pockets, looking at the lake. "Tell them the offer stands, Pris. Six hundred and fifty, whenever they're ready. And tell them we'll wait."

"We'll wait," Priscilla said. "We're very patient people, we just don't like to be told no." She turned back to Tessa and touched her arm lightly. "But I want you to know, if you ever decide, I'd rather have it than anyone. I'd put it in the window of the Dock Room with the light coming off the water, and I'd tell every grandchild who came to visit that it used to belong to a man named Len who bought his lamps for a quarter."

"That would be perfect," Tessa said, and found, to her surprise, that her eyes were wet.

At two o'clock sharp, Owen came back from the Dock Room with a pencil behind one ear, and stood in front of her on the porch steps, and said, "Can I ask about the rag rug?"

Tessa looked at him, and laughed until she had to sit down.

At four o'clock, Nora Pruitt arrived in a white rental car, ten minutes early, with a clipboard under her arm and a pair of sunglasses pushed up into her hair. She stepped onto the lawn, looked at the cream sofa in the living room through the open front door, then at the sofa on the porch with its walnut feet catching the afternoon sun, then at the pie on the rail, and then at Tessa and Owen, standing side by side on the steps with their hands carefully separated by exactly one foot of air.

"Oh," Nora said. "You two look like you've been arguing for a week."

"We have," Tessa said.

"Good," Nora said. "Then you'll be able to tell me what the light is doing."

Owen lifted the camera to his eye, and then, after a glance at Tessa, lowered it and held out the lens cap to her.

"You shoot," he said. "Len always said you had the better eye."

Tessa took the camera. It was heavier than she had expected, and warm from his hand. She lifted it, pressed her cheek to the eyepiece, and found the Dock Room window through the viewfinder: the blue quilt across the iron bed, the brass lamp catching the last gold light off the water, the cedars beyond, and the lake, flat and bright and holding the whole afternoon in its surface like a promise.

"Ask me again," she said, without lowering the camera, "in ten minutes."

"Ask you what?" Owen said.

"Whether you can have the photo," Tessa said, and pressed the shutter.

## Chapter 14: The Hayloft

The barn on Quarry Road was painted the red of an old schoolhouse, and it leaned a little to the east, as if it had been listening to the lake for a century and had decided to lean in and hear better. Its double doors stood shut under a rusted tin roof. Its windows were dark. A row of sugar maples screened the back field from the county road, and beyond them the land fell gently toward the water.

Tessa stood at the bottom of the gravel drive at seven on Friday morning with the key in her hand and did not move.

"You can go in," Owen said from the cab of his pickup. He had parked behind her and was leaning across the bench seat with both hands on the wheel, watching her through the open window. "It doesn't bite."

"I know it doesn't bite."

"Then go in."

"I haven't been inside since the funeral," Tessa said. "I stood in the doorway for five minutes, and then I went back to the motel and cried in the shower for an hour, because everything in there still smelled like him."

"It still smells like him," Owen said quietly. "Cedar and turpentine and burnt coffee. He never once in thirty years drank a cup that wasn't burnt."

Tessa turned the key in the lock. It was stiff, the metal worn bright at the edges, and the door swung inward with a groan into a wall of cool, still air.

The barn was larger inside than it looked from outside. Its floor was wide-planked oak, scrubbed pale by years of sweeping, and along the north wall ran a row of tall shelves stacked with boxes and crates, each one labeled in capital letters. Dining chairs hung from pegs on the east wall, waiting for repair. A workbench sat beneath the high windows, with a vise, a row of planes, a jar of brass screws sorted by size, and a coffee mug half full of pens. A ladder led up to a hayloft that ran along the back wall, its railing worn smooth by years of hands.

Moose trotted in past her, sniffed the workbench, sneezed, and then trotted straight to the foot of the ladder and sat down with his tail thumping slowly against the floorboards.

"He wants to go up," Owen said from the doorway.

"Then he can go up," Tessa said. "I've never been up there."

She climbed the ladder with her hands on the rails and her heart beating harder than it should have, and when she reached the top she stood in the hayloft's soft gray light and looked around.

It was a small room, lined with old quilts and a few pieces of furniture pushed against the walls under the slope of the roof. A narrow iron cot stood beneath the eastern window, made up with a faded gray blanket, and a dog bed, worn down to the stuffing, lay at its foot. On a low pine table beside the cot sat a battered tin lunch box, a pair of reading glasses folded on top of a paperback mystery, and a yellow legal pad with a pencil laid across it.

Moose came up the ladder behind her, hauling himself over the top rung with a grunt, and went straight to the cot. He sniffed the blanket. He sniffed the dog bed. Then he lay down on the floor beside the cot, put his head on the edge of the mattress, and sighed a long, low sigh, as if he had been away for a very long time and had finally come home.

Tessa knelt beside him. She put her hand on his head, and he thumped his tail twice against the floorboards.

"He used to sleep here every night," she said. "Owen said so."

"Every night," Owen said from the top of the ladder. He had climbed up after her, and he stood now with one hand on the railing, looking at the cot. "Len said the dog kept the cot warm, and the cot kept the dog from chewing the furniture downstairs. They had a deal."

"Did Len ever sleep here?"

"Not once," Owen said. "He said he couldn't stand the smell of old wool and pine tar. He slept in the house on Quarry Road, two miles away, with the radio on."

Tessa looked at the cot. The gray blanket had a neat, careful fold along one edge, the kind a man makes with his own hands when he means to keep something safe. She reached out and lifted the corner, and as she did, something slid off the mattress and landed on the floorboards with a soft, heavy thump.

It was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with a length of brown twine. Under the twine, tucked into the fold, was a small slip of paper. She picked up the slip first.

*TESS. IN THE LOFT. UNDER THE COT WHERE THE DOG CAN'T REACH IT. — L.*

She laughed, a short, shaky sound, and wiped her face with the back of her wrist.

"He didn't even trust the dog," she said.

"He trusted the dog with everything," Owen said. "He just didn't trust the dog to leave it alone."

Tessa untied the twine. The oilcloth came away in stiff folds, smelling of cedar and old paper, and underneath it lay a notebook. It was bound in green cloth, its corners worn soft and dark, its spine cracked in two places and mended with tape. On the cover, in her uncle's square capitals, was a single word.

*LEDGER.*

She sat down on the floor beside the cot, her back against its iron frame, and opened it.

The first page was dated in 2009. The handwriting was pure Len, neat and forceful, the pencil pressed hard into the paper. *Marsh Home Staging. Opened in barn, Quarry Rd. Inventory: 212 pieces. Income: $14,310. Expenses: $11,080. Net: $3,230. Not bad for the first year. Tess is 17 and wants to be a designer. Tell her no.*

She turned the page. The entries went on, year after year, in tidy columns and then in looser, more personal notes. A sale to a family in Sunset Point. A broken chair, a dog bite on a delivery man, a set of brass knobs bought for forty cents at an estate sale in Racine. A bad winter, a cold spring, a lost bid on a Milwaukee house that Len had wanted badly. A note from 2014: *Bought the cream sofas in Milwaukee, both of them, for $1,840 the pair. Paid too much. Worth every cent. Tess will love them when she sees them, if she ever sees them.*

She turned forward, through the lean years and the good ones, and came at last to 2023.

*March 9. Stickley desk, estate sale, Milwaukee. Paid $2,300. Sure it's real. Turned out to be a good reproduction with a fake plate. Lost $1,100 on the resale. Stupid. Owen says I'm getting old. He's right.*

*March 14. Need $30,000 for new inventory, second unit on Route 9, roof on the barn. Can't get it from the bank. Owen says he has it from the condo sale. Asked him. He said yes before I finished the sentence. Told him no. He said yes again.*

*March 15. Wrote the note on the back of a Gus's menu, because it was the only paper in the truck. Owen signed it. I signed it. Gus witnessed it and told me I was an idiot, which is the first time anybody in this town has been honest with me in a decade.*

Tessa read the entry twice. Then she read the one below it.

*March 15, later. Gave Owen 40 cents of every net dollar. Of every sale out of the barn and Unit 9, for as long as he keeps the books and carries the couch. Not the inventory, not the barn, not the house. Only the net. Wrote it in the back of this book so there's no mistake. If I die before the loan is paid, he keeps the 40 until Tess decides. She'll decide. She always does. But she'll decide with him in the room, or she'll never forgive me.*

Beside the entry, in pencil, in a different and slightly shakier hand, someone had written a single line.

*He never once asked me for a penny. I'd have paid him in full the first week. He said the 40 was for keeping the light on. —O.*

Tessa put the book down on her knees and stared at the cot, at the faded gray blanket, at the dog's head resting on the edge of the mattress.

"You wrote that," she said.

Owen was sitting on the top rung of the ladder now, his elbows on his knees, his head bowed. He did not look up.

"Yes," he said quietly. "The week he showed it to me. He wanted me to put something in it, so it wasn't just his voice. I didn't know what to write, so I wrote what he told me."

"He told you to write that?"

"He told me to write whatever was true," Owen said. "And I wrote that the 40 was for keeping the light on, because that's what he always said. He said the light had to stay on in the barn, even when nobody was buying, so the people who came up the drive would know somebody was home."

Tessa lifted the book again and turned the pages slowly. The entries grew shorter in the last year. There were fewer sales, and more notes about the house on Quarry Road and the cold in his knees and the way the dog would not let him sleep past five in the morning. And then, near the back, in a hand that was barely legible, she found her own name.

*Dec 22. Tess came up for Christmas, two days. She didn't cry in front of anybody. She stood by the barn for twenty minutes with her hands in her coat pockets, looking at the roof, and I knew she'd be back. She has my hands. She has her mother's sense about money and my temper about everything else. I wish I'd called her more. I wish I'd told her she was the best closer I ever trained, and that she should never once let anyone make her feel like a second-rate stager in a company that never deserved her. I'm telling Owen to tell her, if I can't. I'm telling Nora to tell her, if Owen can't. Somebody has to say it before she gets back on the road.*

Tessa could not see the page anymore. The words had blurred into a pale green smear, and her throat ached with a sound she did not want to make.

She heard the ladder creak. She felt Owen's hand on her shoulder, very light, and then his weight beside her on the floor, his back against the cot, his knee an inch from hers.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Don't."

"I'm sorry I didn't tell you."

"You're sorry you told me now," Tessa said, and her voice broke on the last word, and she let it. "You're sorry you let me find out on a staircase in front of Ray Pruitt. You're sorry you waited for him to tell me, and he didn't, and you thought I'd never know."

"Yes," Owen said quietly. "That's exactly what I'm sorry for."

She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. The hayloft was very quiet. Beyond the walls, the wind moved in the maples, and somewhere down the county road a truck was grinding up the hill toward town. Moose lifted his head from the edge of the mattress and looked at them both, then put it back down with a sigh.

"Read the last page," Owen said.

Tessa turned to it. It was not an entry. It was a single line in pencil, pressed so hard into the paper that the lead had torn the surface in two places.

*Joyce K. says the barn is grandfathered for storage only. No retail, no sales from the door, no parking for customers beyond two cars. Hank Dressler has complained to the township twice. Next time he files, she has to send an inspector. If the inspector finds the Route 9 unit, we're done. Bring the Route 9 inventory moved into the barn before August, Owen. Or get Tess to decide what she wants to do with it, before somebody else decides for her.*

"Joyce Kessler," Tessa said.

"The zoning office," Owen said. "She's been trying to get rid of this barn since 2015. Len used to say she'd have sold the township for a parking lot if anybody offered her a nice enough lunch."

"And Hank Dressler?"

"He runs the marina on Quarry Road, the one that wants the back field for boat storage. He's been trying to buy the barn land for ten years." Owen looked down at his hands. "Len told him to go to hell every time. And every time Hank went to the township and complained about the traffic."

Tessa closed the Ledger and held it against her chest, the green cloth warm from her body, the oilcloth crinkling beneath her fingers.

"Owen," she said.

"Yes?"

"I think I want to read the rest of it. Every page. And then I want to read the box in Unit 9, the one with my name on it, and the notes under the rag rug. And then I want you to tell me exactly what you think I should do, and I want you to be honest, even if it's the worst thing I've ever heard."

He looked up at her, and his face was open and unguarded, and very tired.

"Okay," he said. "But you have to let me sit on the floor for a little while first."

"You're already sitting on the floor."

"I know," Owen said. "I'm trying to decide whether I'm allowed to stay."

Tessa looked at him for a long moment. Then she moved the Ledger into her lap and shifted over, very slightly, so that her shoulder touched his, and she did not say anything at all.

Moose, on the floor between them, sighed in his sleep and stretched one paw out toward the cot.

## Chapter 15: Storm Warning

At six o'clock on Friday evening, Tessa called Brent Malloy from the front porch of the Birch Street cottage, with the Ledger on her knees and the sun going down over the cedars.

"Monday," Brent said, without preamble. "Did you read the contract?"

"I read it twice."

"And?"

"And I'm not going to sign it on Monday," Tessa said. "I'm going to finish the Birch Street staging. The Pruitts have given me the whole install, through Labor Day. I'm going to see the house sold, in the light, the way my uncle wanted. And then I'm going to make a decision about the business that I can live with, which may be yours, and may not."

There was a pause on the line. Then Brent laughed, a little tightly. "You're turning down a hundred and sixty-five thousand dollars to stage a cottage on a lake."

"I'm turning down a hundred and sixty-five thousand dollars to stage a cottage on a lake for a family who loved it, with the lamps I counted twice," Tessa said. "I'm asking you for until the first of August. That's all I want."

"You're going to regret this."

"Maybe," Tessa said. "But I'd regret it more if I didn't find out what I'm capable of when nobody's watching me count."

Brent was silent for a moment. "Tessa," he said at last, and his voice was softer than she had ever heard it. "Your uncle used to call me every Tuesday, you know. For six years. He always said no. And every Tuesday he'd tell me a story about some lamp he'd found at an estate sale, and I'd end up laughing and hanging up without ever getting the answer I wanted." He cleared his throat. "I thought he was a crank. I didn't know he was the only man in the county who could tell a fake from a real."

"He was a crank," Tessa said. "He was also right about everything."

"Yeah," Brent said. "That's what made him so annoying." He paused again. "August first. But I'm not going to ask you twice."

"You won't have to."

"Good," Brent said. "Then I'll tell Regina to hold the Milwaukee office for you until then. And Tess? Eat something. Lucy called me this afternoon to say you're living on blueberry muffins."

The line went dead before she could answer. She sat with the phone in her lap and stared at the lake, and after a while she realized she was laughing, quietly, the way she used to laugh at her uncle's phone calls on Sunday nights, when he told her the weather on the lake was going to turn and she didn't believe him.

Owen came out onto the porch with two bottles of water and a plate of Bev's sugar cookies. He sat down on the top step beside her, not too close, and set the plate between them.

"Brent?" he asked.

"August first."

He nodded slowly. "And what did you tell him?"

"I told him I'm going to finish the house," Tessa said. "And that I'd decide about the rest by August."

"That's not a decision."

"It's the only honest thing I've got right now." She took a cookie, bit into it, and looked at the lake. "Owen, I've been thinking about the forty cents on the dollar."

"I thought you might."

"I want to say something, and I want you to let me finish before you argue with me."

"Okay."

She turned to face him. The sun was low, and the light off the water caught the side of his face, the faint scar above his eyebrow, the small, tired lines around his mouth.

"Len wrote that you were the one who kept the light on," she said. "In the Ledger. He said the forty cents was for that. I've been trying to figure out what it means, and I think it means you did something for him that he couldn't do for himself, and he knew he'd never be able to pay you back in money. So he paid you in the one thing he had left to give, which was a share of a business he never really wanted to lose."

"That's a generous way of putting it."

"It's the only way I can think of," Tessa said, "that doesn't make me want to throw him in the lake."

Owen laughed quietly. "He'd have liked that."

"He'd have liked that a lot." She took a breath. "So here's what I'm going to do. I'm going to honor the forty cents on the dollar. Of the net, as he wrote it, for as long as you keep the books and carry the couch. Not the house. Not the barn. Not the inventory I haven't read yet. Just the net, and only if I keep the business. If I sell it to Brent, you'll get what's due from the sale, and the loan will be paid from the commission on this house, and the note will be closed."

Owen was very still.

"And if you keep it?" he asked.

"Then you're going to be in the room," Tessa said. "Every time. You're going to tell me where the lamps belong, and I'm going to tell you when you're wrong, and neither of us is going to pretend the other one's a dead man's errand boy." She looked at him steadily. "I'm not going to be your partner, Owen. I don't know what I'm going to be to you yet. But I'm not going to let my uncle's menu decide it for me, and I'm not going to let you carry this alone for another two years because you're afraid I'll be angry."

"You're already angry."

"I'm very angry," Tessa said. "I'm angry at Len. I'm angry at Brent. I'm angry at the township and the Pruitts and Gus's pie crust. And I'm not angry at you anymore. I think I stopped on the hayloft floor, when you told me you were trying to decide whether you were allowed to stay."

Owen looked down at his hands. They were resting on his knees, and they were trembling, very slightly, as if he had been holding them still for a long time and had only just now been told he could let go.

"Thanks," he said.

"Don't thank me. Keep the light on."

"I'll keep it on."

"And don't you ever," Tessa said, and her voice wobbled, "not tell me something again because you think you're protecting me. Do you hear me? I'm not your sister, and I'm not your mother, and I'm not one of your Chicago clients who has to be handled with a clipboard. I'm the one who's standing in the middle of this, and I'd rather be hurt by the truth than comforted by a lie."

"I hear you," Owen said.

"Good."

They ate the cookies. They did not say anything for a while. Below at the water, a pair of ducks rose off the shallows and beat their way toward the pier in a low, noisy arc, and somewhere up the street a radio was playing a country song with a fiddle that went on and on.

Then Tessa's phone made a sound she had never heard before.

It was a long, harsh chime, followed by a second and a third, and then a sharp electronic voice that seemed to come from the phone itself and the trees and the sky all at once.

*Severe weather alert. A line of thunderstorms is expected to develop across Larkspur Lake and Kettle County after 9:00 p.m. Saturday. Damaging winds up to 60 miles per hour and heavy rain are possible. Seek shelter in a sturdy structure if thunder is heard. Do not travel unless necessary.*

Owen had his own phone out. He read the message, then looked up at the sky. It was still clear and blue, with a faint golden haze on the horizon, but there was a strange stillness in the air now, a heaviness that had not been there an hour earlier.

"That's the Kettle County line," he said. "It'll be here by ten."

"Are we ready?" Tessa asked.

"For a storm? No," Owen said. "Nobody's ready for a storm. But the Birch Street roof is older than both of us, and Bev says the flashing around the chimney has been leaking since 1998. And the Pruitts will want the porch furniture in the barn before dark."

Tessa stood up. Her legs felt steady, which surprised her.

"Then we move everything that isn't nailed down," she said. "And we check the roof, and we put a bucket under the leak in the Dock Room, and we find out whether Ray Pruitt has a generator."

"He doesn't."

"Then we'd better get Bev's flashlights," Tessa said, and picked up the Ledger from the porch boards and held it against her chest, the green cloth cool and familiar under her palm.

Owen stood up beside her, slowly, and looked at the sky once more, and then at her.

"You're going to be fine," he said.

"You don't know that."

"No," he agreed. "But I know you're going to try, and I'd rather watch you try than watch anybody else win."

She did not know what to say to that, so she said nothing. She went down the porch steps and across the lawn, and the cream sofa sat in the living room behind her, and the sofa on the porch stood with its walnut feet in the grass, and the brass lamp in the Dock Room window caught the last gold light off the lake and held it, steady and bright, as if it had been waiting all summer for exactly this kind of night.

Behind her, Moose came trotting across the grass, barking once at nothing in particular, and then fell in at her heels as if he had been told the plan and approved.

"Good dog," Tessa said. "Stay close."

Above the cedars, the first thin rumble of thunder rolled out across the lake, very far away, like a door being closed in another house.

## Chapter 16: Rain Day

The storm came in across the lake at a quarter past ten on Saturday night, and it came fast.

Tessa heard it before she saw it. A low, rolling hush swept over the water from the west, like a crowd of people taking a breath in unison, and then the cedars along the shore bent all at once, their dark tops tossing as though a giant hand had shaken them. The wind hit the cottage with a force that rattled every window in the frame. The lamp in the Dock Room flickered, dimmed, and went out. For one long second the whole house was dark except for the blue-white flashes of lightning through the front windows, and then the power went with a soft, final thunk somewhere out in the street, and the rain came down all at once, like a wall of water poured from a bucket.

"Got it," Owen said from the kitchen. There was a clatter, a curse, and a small, bright circle of light appeared on the counter where he had set down Bev's big flashlight. "The breaker's fine. It's the whole street. Bev's already texted Gus. He says he's got the generator at the diner, and he'll bring a cord over at midnight if the lines are still down."

"Is Bev okay?"

"Bev is sitting in her kitchen with a lantern and a pot of tea," Owen said. "She says she's been through worse storms than this, and that the Birch Street roof has leaked every spring since her husband died, and she's been waiting twenty-six years for somebody to fix it."

"Somebody is going to fix it," Tessa said, "as soon as the sun comes up and we can see the flashing."

She came into the kitchen with a second flashlight in her hand and Moose at her heels, his nails clicking on the floorboards, his tail low and uncertain. He had been pacing since the first gust hit the porch, and now he came to stand against her legs with his body pressed close, shaking slightly, until she bent down and rubbed his ears.

"He hates thunder," Owen said.

"Len told me that," Tessa said. "He said Moose used to hide under the barn hayloft cot every time a storm came over the lake, and Len would have to sit on the floor with him for an hour until he calmed down."

"That's where the cot came from," Owen said, almost to himself. "Len bought it for the dog."

They checked the house by flashlight, room by room. The front windows were holding. The porch furniture had been dragged into the garage at the back of the lot, and the two sofas were pushed against the inner walls, away from the glass. The primary bedroom was dry. The back bedroom was dry. The attic hatch in the upstairs hall had a thin brown stain around its edges, and when Owen pushed it open, a steady drip came down from the rafters and hit a tin bucket they had left there that afternoon, with a sound like a clock ticking.

"Chimney flashing," Owen said. He held the flashlight up, its beam shaking in the wind-driven rain that came in through a gap in the roof. "Bev was right. It's been leaking for years."

"Then it's going to keep leaking until somebody goes up there with a ladder," Tessa said.

"Not tonight."

"Not tonight," she agreed.

The Dock Room was the worst. Rain was coming through the top of the north window, a slow, steady trickle that ran down the frame and pooled on the sill, and the blue quilt on the iron bed was already damp along one corner. They pulled it off and folded it over the back of the green chair, out of the way. Tessa set a second bucket under the leak. Owen wedged a towel into the gap in the frame. The brass lamp stood in the corner, dark and silent, its green shade catching their flashlight beams in faint, moving sparks.

"It'll be fine," Owen said. "The lamp's been through worse."

"The lamp has never been through a storm in its life," Tessa said. "It came from a Racine estate sale in 2023, and it's spent most of its life in a storage unit on Route 9."

"It's been in a storage unit for three years, and it's come out just fine."

"That's because the barn doesn't leak."

"Tessa," Owen said quietly. "It's a lamp."

She looked at the brass stand, at the crazed glass of the spare shade standing on the dresser where they had set it that afternoon, at the bucket ticking softly under the leak.

"I know it's a lamp," she said. "I just don't want anything else to break tonight."

He crossed the room in the dark and stood beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth of his arm through the sleeve of her shirt. He did not touch her. He only looked at the lamp with her.

"Nobody else is going to break," he said.

They sat on the floor of the Dock Room with their backs against the bed and the flashlight on the carpet beside them, its beam pointed at the ceiling so that the light spread out in a soft gold circle. The rain drummed on the roof. The wind moved through the cedars with a sound like surf. Moose came and lay down across both their legs, heavy and warm, and sighed.

"Bev sent a pie," Owen said after a while. "Blueberry. It's in the kitchen, under a towel. She says it's the only thing we'll be able to eat once the power's out for good, and that Gus is going to be insulted if we don't finish it."

"Gus is always insulted."

"Gus is always right about pie," Owen said. "It's the crust."

Tessa laughed softly. She let her head fall back against the bed frame, and closed her eyes, and listened to the storm. She could hear Owen breathing beside her, slow and even. She could feel the dog's warm weight against her shins. She could smell the damp wool of the quilt, and the cedar on the walls, and the faint sweetness of the blueberry pie drifting in from the kitchen.

"Tell me about your mother," she said.

"My mother?"

"You said she worked nights at the hospital laundry, so you could play ball in the summers. You never told me anything about her after that."

Owen was quiet for a moment.

"She's still there," he said at last. "She's in the laundry room at Larkspur Community, the same job, twenty-six years now. She works the four to midnight shift, and she still sends me home with a plate of tamales every Sunday, even though I'm thirty-seven years old and I can cook my own food." He turned his head toward her in the dark. "She never once cried about my father. Not in front of me. Not in front of anybody. She'd just get up in the morning, and make coffee, and say, 'Owen, the lake is still here, and so are you, so eat something.'"

"She sounds like my uncle."

"She'd have liked him," Owen said. "She'd have hated the placemat, but she'd have liked him."

"And you?" Tessa asked. "Did you ever cry about him?"

"About my father?"

"About anybody."

Owen did not answer right away. The rain drummed harder. Moose sighed again and shifted his weight, and Owen's hand moved, very slightly, on the carpet between them.

"I cried in the barn," he said, very quietly, "the day Len died. In February. I was standing by the workbench with a box of brass screws, and I couldn't stop. And Bev came in, and she didn't say a word. She just sat down on the stool and put her hand on my back, and we stayed there for an hour, and neither of us said anything at all." He swallowed. "I've never told anybody that."

Tessa opened her eyes and turned her head to look at him.

He was sitting with his knees up and his forearms resting on them, his face turned toward the dark window. The flashlight's glow caught the line of his jaw, the curve of his mouth, the scar above his eyebrow. He looked, in that moment, exactly as young as he was, and exactly as tired.

"Owen," she said.

He turned.

She did not think about it. She did not weigh the placemat, or the menu, or the Ledger in its oilcloth on the nightstand, or the box marked NOT YET under the rag rug in Unit 9. She leaned forward, across the damp quilt and the sleeping dog, and she kissed him.

It was not a careful kiss. It was not a planned one. It was the kiss of a woman who had been standing on the edge of something for two weeks and had finally been pushed, by a storm and a dog and a dead man's handwriting, into the water. His mouth was warm and surprised against hers, and for one long moment he did not move at all. Then his hand came up, slowly, to rest against her cheek, his thumb brushing the damp curve of her jaw, and he kissed her back.

It was slow. It was careful. It tasted of rainwater and sugar cookies and something she could only call the lake. When she finally drew back, just an inch, his breath was uneven, and his eyes were dark in the flashlight's glow, and he was looking at her as though she had given him something he had never once expected to be allowed to keep.

"Tessa," he said, very quietly.

"I know," she said. "I know what I said on the pier."

"You said not yet."

"I know."

"And now?"

She looked at him for a long moment. The rain drummed on the roof. Somewhere outside, a branch cracked and fell, and the house shuddered around them, and the brass lamp in the corner stood dark and silent, waiting for the power to come back on.

"Now it's a Saturday night in a storm," Tessa said, "and I don't want to be the only person in this house who doesn't know what she wants."

Owen let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "Then you know what you want."

"I know what I want for tonight," she said. "I don't know what I want for the rest of my life. And I'm not going to pretend I do, just because I'm tired and wet and I've been sleeping on your uncle's floor."

"He's not my uncle," Owen said, with a small, crooked smile.

"He's the closest thing you've got to one," Tessa said. "Don't argue with me. I'm not in the mood to be right about everything."

He reached out then, and took her hand, and laced his fingers through hers. It was a small thing. It was the most natural thing in the world. He did not pull her closer. He only held on, steadily, while the storm moved east over the water and the rain began, very gradually, to slacken against the glass.

"Stay," he said quietly. "Until it's over. Then go to bed, if that's what you want. I'll sleep on the cot in the barn with the dog, if you need me to be somewhere else."

"There isn't a cot in the barn," Tessa said. "It's up in the hayloft, and you're not sleeping on it."

"I'll sleep on the floor, then."

"You'll sleep on the floor of the Dock Room," she said, "next to the lamp your uncle bought for a quarter, with a bucket under the leak and a dog on your feet. And you'll tell me in the morning which side of the bed you want, and I'll tell you whether you're allowed."

He laughed, low and warm, and leaned his head back against the bed frame, still holding her hand. "That's the most reasonable thing anyone's said to me all year," he said.

"I'm told it's a gift," Tessa said.

They stayed on the floor of the Dock Room, side by side, holding hands, until the rain thinned into a soft, steady hush and the wind dropped to a murmur in the cedars. At some point, Tessa fell asleep with her head against his shoulder, and she did not wake until the gray dawn came up over the lake and the first bird of the morning began to sing from the porch railing, as if nothing in the world had ever gone wrong.

When she opened her eyes, the lamp was still dark in the corner, and the bucket had filled to the brim, and Owen was watching her from a few inches away with an expression she had never seen on any man's face before.

"Morning," he said softly.

"Did the power come back?"

"No."

"Did Bev call?"

"Bev called at six," Owen said. "She said the Birch Street roof is going to cost us a fortune, and she's already decided who's paying for it, and it isn't going to be you."

"Who?"

He smiled, slowly, and tucked a strand of damp hair back from her face.

"Ray Pruitt," he said. "And he doesn't know it yet."

Tessa laughed, and sat up, and felt the stiffness of the floor in her back and the warm, heavy ache of a night spent not quite sleeping. Moose lifted his head from the foot of the bed, looked at them both, and let out one long, satisfied sigh, as though he had finally been proved right about something.

"He knew," Owen said.

"He always knew," Tessa said, and stood up, and reached for her shoes.

## Chapter 17: Wet Floor

The damage was worse in daylight.

The storm had torn three shingles off the north slope of the Birch Street roof, snapped a limb from the oldest birch, and sent a sheet of plywood from somebody's garage skidding across two yards and into Bev Halvorsen's hedge. The chimney flashing had lifted at one corner, and the rain had found the gap and poured straight down through the attic into the upstairs hall, across the floorboards, and out under the door of the Dock Room. The carpet in the hall was soaked. The ceiling in the back bedroom had a dark, spreading stain the size of a dinner plate. And the blue quilt, which they had folded over the green chair the night before, had soaked up so much water that it would need to be taken to the dry cleaner in Racine, if it could be saved at all.

Tessa stood in the upstairs hall at seven thirty on Sunday morning, in her socks and a borrowed sweatshirt that said LARKSPUR LAKE DAYS, 1998, and she stared at the ceiling with her hands on her hips.

"It's going to cost a fortune," she said.

"It's going to cost Ray Pruitt a fortune," Owen said from the top of the stairs. He had come up behind her with two coffees from the diner and a roll of blue tarp under one arm, and he set both cups on the hall table without looking at either one. "Bev has already called him. She says she's not going to let him sell this house with a wet carpet and a stained ceiling, and she's been talking to Nora about it since six."

"Nora's here?"

"Nora's in Ray's car, in the driveway. She drove up from the Pruitt house on Front Street at six thirty, in her pajamas, with a bag of muffins and a clipboard. She says she wants to see the damage with her own eyes before she decides whether the house is worth the trouble." Owen glanced down the stairs. "She also says she's been up since four, because the sound of the rain on her roof in Phoenix is nothing like the sound of rain on Birch Street, and she's been thinking about her aunt."

Tessa went down the stairs. Nora Pruitt was standing in the front hall in a camel-colored raincoat, a clipboard in one hand, and she looked up as Tessa came down with the expression of a woman who had not slept at all and did not intend to say so.

"The Dock Room," Nora said.

"It's soaked," Tessa said. "The window leaked, and the quilt is ruined, and the lamp is fine."

"The lamp is fine?"

"The lamp is fine," Tessa said. "The shade on the spare one is fine. We moved both of them out of the water before midnight. The closet lamp is on the porch, under a tarp, with its cord coiled up in a bag. Priscilla's cord. She sent it on Thursday." She took a breath. "The roof is the problem. The flashing has been leaking since 1998, Bev says. Nobody fixed it."

"Ada never let anyone fix it," Nora said. Her voice was flat and tired. "She said the house was perfect the way it was, and the leak was just the lake reminding us whose house it was." She looked up at the ceiling, at the dark stain spreading across the plaster. "She was a stubborn woman."

"So was my uncle," Tessa said.

"I know," Nora said. "He sent me a bill for the leak in 2019. Forty dollars. He said it was for the patch he'd put on the flashing the summer before, when Ada wouldn't call a roofer. He said he'd rather charge me than watch the house fall in."

Tessa almost smiled. "That sounds like him."

"It was him," Nora said. "He was the only person in Larkspur Lake who ever told my aunt no and got away with it." She tucked the clipboard under her arm. "Does Ray know?"

"Ray's in the car," Owen said from the stairs. He had come down behind them with the blue tarp over his shoulder, and his face was pale and tight with the effort of not saying something unkind. "He's been sitting there since six with the engine running and the radio on, and he hasn't said a word about the roof. I think he's afraid of what it's going to cost."

Nora sighed, a long, weary sound, and turned toward the front door.

"Then I'm going to go out there and tell him," she said. "And then he's going to pay for it."

She went out into the wet gray morning with her clipboard tucked under her arm, and Tessa heard her voice rise from the driveway, sharp and clear and utterly unbothered, saying something about the roofer in Racine and the price of a birch tree and the cost of a Pruitt family reputation that did not survive a leaking ceiling. A car door slammed. Ray's voice rose in protest and fell away.

Owen came to stand beside Tessa in the front hall. He put the tarp down on the floor and looked up at her.

"Ask me something," he said.

"What?"

"Whatever. Ask me what I want to do about the roof."

"What do you want to do about the roof?"

He smiled, tiredly. "I want to get up there with a ladder and a bucket of tar and fix it myself, because I'm the stupidest man in the county and I'd rather break my neck than let Ray Pruitt think his aunt's house is falling down." He paused. "But I'm going to ask you first."

"Then don't go up there," Tessa said, "until the roofer from Racine has been here. And don't you dare touch that birch."

"I wasn't going to touch the birch."

"You were going to touch the birch."

"I was going to look at the birch," Owen said. "There's a difference."

She laughed, and it came out wobbly and tired, and she leaned against the wall with her arms crossed and her head tipped back, looking at the ceiling. The stain was still spreading, very slowly, toward the corner.

"Ask first," she said. "That's the rule."

"Yes, ma'am," Owen said, and looked at her in a way that made her cheeks go hot, and she turned away and went to find the coffee.

The Fenners arrived at one o'clock, in the silver sedan, with a fat manila envelope on the back seat and an expression of quiet, determined optimism that Tessa had not seen on anyone since she left Chicago. They walked up the drive hand in hand, looking at the birches, the tarp on the north slope, and the lake, which had calmed overnight into a sheet of pale silver under a sky the color of wet pewter.

"We heard about the storm," Warren said. "Are you all right?"

"The roof is a mess," Owen said. "And the Dock Room is a swamp. But the house is standing."

"That's the only thing we care about," Priscilla said. She looked at the stain on the ceiling through the open door, and then at Tessa, and her face softened. "We're buying a house, not a roof. Roofs can be fixed. Lamps can be replaced. The lake is still here."

"The lake is still here," Tessa repeated.

Priscilla laughed. "Your uncle said that to us," she said. "Years ago, when we bought our place on Sunset Point. Warren asked him whether the leak was going to be a problem, and Len said, 'The lake is still here, and so are you, so buy the damn house.'"

Warren nodded. "He was right about that, too."

"He was right about everything," Tessa said, and felt the old pang in her chest, and let it pass.

They sat at the dining table on the porch, under the birches, with the envelope between them and a pot of Bev's coffee going cold in the middle. The purchase agreement ran to eleven pages. Owen had read every word of it twice, and he went through it now, line by line, with a pen in one hand and a glass of water in the other, while Tessa sat beside him and tried not to watch the clock.

"The furniture is included," Owen said. "The sofas, the rugs, the dining set. The blue quilt is excluded, because it's damaged, and you've agreed to replace it at the Pruitts' expense. The brass lamp is excluded, pending a decision by the sellers' agent and the owner of the lamp."

"The owner of the lamp," Priscilla said, with a small, delighted smile. "I like that."

"And the closet lamp is excluded at your request," Owen said. "Because you want to buy it yourself, from the estate, for a dollar."

"A dollar and a candy bar," Priscilla said.

"Tessa," Owen said quietly.

She looked up.

"Do you want to sign it?"

She had known he would ask. She had known since the storm, since the Dock Room floor and the brass lamp and the dog across her shins, that someone was going to put a pen in her hand and ask her to decide. She looked at the eleven pages, at the blank line near the bottom where the owner's signature would go. She looked at Owen, who was watching her steadily across the table, his face unreadable, his pen laid down quietly beside the agreement.

"Ask me first," Tessa said. "Are you going to sign?"

Owen let out a breath, almost a laugh.

"Yes," he said. "I'm going to sign as the listing agent. Because I'd sign anything you put in front of me, Tessa, if it had your name on it."

"Then I'll sign as the owner of the staging contract," Tessa said. "And we'll hold the lamp in escrow, with Nora's permission, until the township decides what it's worth."

"The township?" Warren asked, frowning.

Tessa glanced at Owen, and then at the envelope in her lap, which she had not yet opened. It was the envelope from the mailbox at the end of the drive, the one she had pulled out on her way up from the barn that morning, and had not had time to read. It had a red county seal in the corner, and the words *Village of Larkspur Lake, Office of Zoning and Land Use* printed in a plain black font. It was addressed to *Marsh Home Staging, Quarry Road, Larkspur Lake, WI*, and it had been postmarked the day before the storm.

She opened it with her thumb and read the first paragraph aloud, slowly, so that Owen could hear it too.

*Re: Notice of Complaint and Scheduled Review, Quarry Road Barn (Parcel 14-07-221). The township has received a written complaint alleging that the parcel is being used for retail sales in violation of its agricultural storage permit. A public hearing before the township board is scheduled for Tuesday, August 4, at 7:00 p.m. at the Larkspur Lake town hall. The property owner or authorized representative is required to attend. Failure to appear may result in revocation of the existing storage permit.*

Tessa put the letter down on the table very carefully, as if it might go off.

"Joyce Kessler," Owen said quietly. "Hank Dressler."

"August fourth," Tessa said. "That's three days after Brent's deadline."

"Yes."

"And the Route 9 unit is the first thing the inspector will find, if the barn is ever opened for a look."

"Yes," Owen said. "That's exactly what Len wrote."

Priscilla and Warren were both silent. Warren looked at the lake. Priscilla looked at Tessa, and then at Owen, and then, slowly, at the dining table, where the eleven-page agreement lay between them like a promise nobody was ready to keep.

"Oh," Priscilla said at last, very gently. "I think you two have a great deal to talk about before you sign anything else."

"We do," Tessa said.

"We'll wait," Warren said. "Please take all the time you need. But Tessa?"

"Yes?"

"The offer stands," he said. "The house, the furniture, and the lamp, when you're ready. And if you ever decide you want to fight the township about the barn, I know a lawyer in Oak Park who loves a good zoning fight. He'd do it for nothing, just to see the look on Joyce Kessler's face."

Tessa looked at the Fenners, and at Owen, and at the letter, and at the blue quilt folded over the green chair in the Dock Room, its corner still damp from the storm, and she felt, for the first time since she had pulled into the drive on the last Tuesday of May, that she was not alone with the decision.

"Thanks," she said. "I think I'm going to need him."

## Chapter 18: Not Yet

The box marked *FOR TESS. NOT YET.* sat on the concrete floor of Unit 9 for three minutes before Tessa could bring herself to touch the tape.

It was Monday morning, and the north row of Pinecrest Storage was warm already, the sun slanting through the open door and laying a bright rectangle across the floor. Owen stood in the doorway with his arms folded and his back to the light, not watching her and not leaving. He had asked once whether she wanted him to go outside, and she had said no, and he had nodded and stayed where he was. She was grateful for that. She did not think she could have done it with anyone else watching.

She took the box in both hands and lifted it into her lap, sitting on the rag rug with her back against the walnut desk. It was lighter than she had expected. The tape was yellowed and brittle, and when she pulled at the edge it came away in one long, crackling strip, releasing the faint, sweet smell of old paper and cedar.

Inside were letters.

Not many. Perhaps a dozen, stacked in order and bound with a rubber band that had long since gone brittle and sticky. Each one was in a plain white envelope, addressed in her uncle's capitals: *TESS MARSH, 2208 N. KEDZIE, CHICAGO*, then *TESS MARSH, NAPERVILLE*, and then, on the most recent, *TESS MARSH, C/O LAKESHORE STAGING, CHICAGO, IL*. The postmarks ran back to 2013. The first one was dated the summer she turned twenty-one.

She slid off the rubber band and held the stack in her lap for a long moment without opening anything.

"Owen," she said.

"Yes?"

"Did you know these were here?"

"I knew the box was here," he said quietly. "I didn't know what was in it. Len never let me look."

"He wrote me a letter every year."

"I know."

She turned to look at him. He was still in the doorway, still with his arms crossed, but his face had gone soft and unguarded, the way it had on the hayloft floor.

"You knew," she said.

"I knew he sent something to you every summer," Owen said. "He'd stand at the mailbox on Quarry Road with his hat in one hand and a stamp in the other, and he'd say, 'Owen, I'm writing the kid again, and she's never going to answer, and I'm going to keep doing it anyway.' He said it like it was a joke. It wasn't."

Tessa looked down at the stack of letters in her lap. She thought about the shoebox in her childhood bedroom in Naperville, where she had kept birthday cards and report cards and a single postcard from the Larkspur Lake Motel with a picture of a loon on the front. She had kept every one of them. She had never once in her life written back to her uncle.

"I didn't answer," she said.

"I know," Owen said. "He didn't expect you to."

She opened the first envelope.

The letter was short, two lines in pencil on a sheet torn from a yellow legal pad. It was dated July 14, 2013.

*Tess. The lake is warm this year. Moose is a puppy and has eaten one of my shoes. Go up and see him. You'll like him better than me. —Uncle Len.*

She laughed, a short, wet sound, and turned the envelope over, and found her own name on the back in her uncle's handwriting, written again in smaller letters, as if he had been afraid the first one would not be enough.

*Still here.*

She opened the next one. And the next. Each letter was a little longer than the last, though none of them was ever more than a page. They spoke of the weather, and the lake, and the barn, and Moose, who had grown into a large, dignified dog and had taken up the habit of sleeping in the hayloft. They spoke of Gus's pies and Bev's sharp tongue and Joyce Kessler's latest letter to the township. They spoke of the Pruitts, and of Ada, and of a woman named Marian who had brought Len a dozen eggs every Tuesday for eleven years and then moved to Arizona without saying goodbye. They spoke of a house on Sunset Point where a little girl had once left a drawing of a cat on the window seat, and Len had never sold that window seat because he didn't want anyone to sit on the cat.

They spoke, over and over, of Tessa.

*You'd hate this sofa. I bought it anyway. It's the same cream as the one you liked in the Northline window, the one you said looked like a cloud that had given up on the sky.*

*You were right about the blue quilt. I was wrong about the blue quilt. Don't tell anybody I said so.*

*Owen says you're the best closer he's ever seen. He's a bad liar, Tess. I'd believe him if I were you.*

*Your mother says you're working too hard. I say she's right, and I'm too old to tell you to slow down, so I'm asking you to do it for me. Eat a real meal. Sit down on a porch. Look at the water for an hour. It won't make you a worse stager. It'll make you a better person, and you'll need that more than you need another sold house.*

Tessa read that one twice, and then she put it face down on her knee and pressed her hand flat over it, and for a long time she did not move at all.

Owen came across the unit then, slowly, and crouched beside her on the rug. He did not touch her. He sat back on his heels with his forearms resting on his knees, and he waited.

"He wrote about you every single time," he said.

"I know." Her voice came out thin and uneven. "He wrote about everyone. The Pruitts. Bev. Moose. Gus's pie crust. He wrote about a window seat for God's sake." She swallowed. "But he never once said he missed me."

"He wrote that in the last one."

"He didn't."

"He did," Owen said. "It's the one at the bottom. He told me about it in January, when he was in the hospital in Milwaukee and couldn't sleep. He said he'd written you a letter that said the only thing he ever wanted you to know, and that it was short, and that he'd been trying for three weeks to make it longer, and couldn't."

Tessa dug through the stack with shaking fingers until she found the last envelope. It was postmarked January 9, 2026. The handwriting was barely recognizable: loose, sloping, pressed hard into the paper, the pencil skidding in places as if the hand that held it had been shaking.

She opened it.

It was only one line.

*Tess. I miss you. Return home or don't, but know that the light stays on here, and it always will. — L.*

The unit was very quiet. Beyond the door, a truck rumbled along Route 9, and somewhere beyond the sunflowers a dog barked twice and fell silent.

Tessa did not cry. She had expected to. She had cried in the shower at the Larkspur Lake Motel for an hour, and at the funeral lunch in February, and on the hayloft floor on Friday. But now, sitting on the rag rug with her uncle's last letter in her hands and Owen's shoulder inches from hers, she felt something she had no word for, a kind of settling, as if a heavy piece of furniture had finally been moved into the right room and set down exactly where it belonged.

"He never said that to me," she said quietly. "Not once. Not on the phone. Not in person. Not even at Christmas, when he drove all the way to Naperville in the snow and sat in my mother's kitchen eating her meatloaf, and I was in the other room answering email."

"He was afraid you'd say no," Owen said.

"I would have said no."

"I know you would have," Owen said, very gently. "That's why he wrote it down."

Tessa turned the letter over in her fingers. The paper was soft and thin with handling, and there was a faint, pale stain in one corner, the kind a tear leaves when it dries before the ink does.

"I'm not going to cry," she said.

"Okay."

"I'm going to get up off this floor, and I'm going to walk out of this unit, and I'm going to put these letters in the glove box of my truck, where I can read them whenever I want. And then I'm going to call Brent Malloy and tell him to stop calling my cousin. And then I'm going to call my mother."

Owen did not say anything for a moment. Then he said, quietly, "Which of those are you doing first?"

"The letters," Tessa said. "Then my mother. Then Brent."

"And the barn?"

"The barn can wait until the township has finished being an idiot."

He laughed, low and surprised, and she felt the sound of it in her own chest, warm and loosening, the way a knot comes undone when you finally find the right end of the string.

"That's the most Len thing you've ever said," he said.

"It's the most Len thing I've ever wanted to be," Tessa said, and then she got up off the floor, a little unsteadily, and stood for a long moment in the bright rectangle of sunlight spilling through the unit door, holding the letters against her chest.

Owen rose beside her. He did not reach for her hand. He only stood close enough that she could feel him there, steady and warm and patient, the way the lake was patient, the way her uncle had always said the water was patient for anyone honest enough to look at it.

"Tessa," he said.

"I'm not going to say yes yet," she said, without turning around. "Not to the house, or the barn, or the forty cents on the dollar. I'm not going to say yes to you, either, not until I've read every one of these and called my mother and figured out what I'm actually going to do with my life."

"Okay."

"But I'm not going to say no."

He was silent for a long time. Then she heard him let out a breath, slowly, as though he had been holding it since the pier.

"That's enough," he said.

"Is it?"

"It's more than I had on Friday," Owen said. "And it's more than I ever thought Len would get out of you." He paused. "He'd have said you were a difficult woman."

"He'd have been right."

"He was always right," Owen said. "That was the problem."

Tessa laughed, and walked out of Unit 9 into the bright June morning with the letters held close, and the lamp on the shelf inside still waiting in the dark, its brass tag turned to the wall, its green shade catching what little light came through the door before it fell away.

## Chapter 19: Rosa's Kitchen

By the third week of June, Tessa had learned how Larkspur Lake woke up in the morning.

It started at five thirty, with the bait shop lights coming on at the foot of the pier and the sound of Gus's grill being scraped clean at the back of the Dock Street Diner. By six, the first pickup trucks were rolling down Front Street with their windows open and their radios playing country stations, and somewhere along Birch Street a screen door banged shut, and Bev Halvorsen's voice carried across two yards, calling a cat named Admiral in for breakfast. By seven, the Garden Club women were in their booth at the diner, and by eight, the roofer from Racine had arrived with a pickup full of shingles and a cheerful, unbothered manner that suggested he had been fixing leaks in Larkspur Lake since the Clinton administration.

His name was Pete Ostrowski. He was sixty years old, with a gray mustache and a belly that did not impede his balance one bit, and he walked across the Birch Street roof on the steep north slope as if it were a sidewalk. He spent four days up there. He replaced the chimney flashing, the three torn shingles, and a rotted section of fascia that Owen had been trying to find a reason to fix since April. He told Tessa, on the second afternoon, sitting on the porch steps with a sandwich in his hand, that the house was in better shape than it looked and worse shape than anybody wanted to admit.

"Your uncle called me in 2019," Pete said, with his mouth full. "He said the flashing was going, and he wanted it done right, and he didn't want Ada to know he'd paid for it. So I came up on a Tuesday, and I fixed it, and Ada came out on the porch with a glass of lemonade and said, 'Pete, I know what you're doing. Go home.'"

"Did you?"

"Went home, sure. Came back the next year and did it again, because your uncle paid cash, and he always had a good lemon bar." Pete grinned. "Now she's gone, and somebody's got to pay me to do it right. Are you going to be the one?"

"I think so," Tessa said.

"Good." He stood up, brushed the crumbs off his shirt, and looked out at the lake. "Because the Birch Street job is the last one in this town that's worth doing right, and I'd hate to see it go to a Chicago outfit that puts vinyl siding on everything."

Tessa laughed. She had not expected to, and she did not stop her laughter when it came.

Lucy Ambrose called on the Wednesday of the roofing week, from a crowded train platform in Chicago, to ask whether she was still alive.

"I'm alive," Tessa said. She was sitting on the porch with her feet up on the rail, Moose asleep beside her, a cup of coffee in her hand and the Ledger open on her knee. "I'm also very sunburned. And I think I've gained five pounds in blueberry muffins."

"Tess, you sound happy."

"I'm not happy. I'm tired. The roof leaked, the township is threatening a hearing, and there's a man in the next room who keeps leaving me notes about the lamps on the fridge."

"That's the most romantic thing I've ever heard."

"It's not romantic. It's a pain in the neck."

"It's romantic," Lucy said, with great certainty. "And I'm coming up for Lake Days. Don't argue. I'm taking the Friday train out of Union Station, and I'm bringing your good shoes, because I know you've been wearing the same pair of work boots since May. I want to meet this realtor. I want to see the dog. And I want to see the placemat, because Brent Malloy called me again on Tuesday to ask whether you'd lost your mind over a hundred and sixty-five thousand dollars and a placemat, and I need to see with my own eyes whether he's right."

"I haven't lost my mind."

"Then bring me a lemon bar," Lucy said. "Gus's, not Bev's. I'm told Bev's are a crime against nature."

Tessa hung up and laughed until Moose lifted his head and gave her a long, injured look.

"Don't," she told him. "It's not your problem."

The Garden Club women were a different matter.

They had been watching the Birch Street job from the beginning, with an interest so intense it bordered on the forensic. They knew every truck that came up the drive, and every pair of shoes Tessa wore to the diner, and the exact hour Owen Reyes had walked her down the sidewalk on the first Saturday in June with his hand not quite touching the small of her back. They knew about the Dock Room, and the storm, and the brass lamp that had been excluded from the sale by mutual agreement. And on the last Thursday in June, sitting in their usual booth at the diner with three pieces of pie and a pot of tea, they decided, in voices that carried easily to the counter, that the Marsh woman had come to Larkspur Lake to steal her uncle's business and was now sleeping with the realtor who had been stealing it for her.

Tessa heard every word from the end of the counter, where she had been waiting for her coffee. She set down her cup very carefully.

"Don't," Bev said, from the booth beside the window.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say something very unkind in a very loud voice," Bev said, "and you'd have been wrong about the sleeping part."

"Bev, the whole town knows he's been in her unit every morning for a month."

"The whole town knows his truck is in her drive," Bev said crisply, "because he's been fixing her roof, and her porch, and her dishwasher, and carrying her uncle's couches out of a barn that's been falling down since 2015. And if you want to tell me what that has to do with the price of a lamp, Ethel, you can tell it to the township board on the fourth of August, when they're deciding whether to shut the barn down."

The Garden Club table went quiet. Ethel Sorensen, who had been the chair of the club since the first Clinton administration, put down her fork and looked at Bev Halvorsen with the expression of a woman who had never once been spoken to in that tone in public.

"Bev," she said stiffly, "we're only talking."

"You're only talking," Bev agreed. "And I'm only telling you that the girl is trying to keep this town standing up, and if you want to sit here and gossip about her while she does it, that's your business, but I'll be writing down your names in the minutes of every hearing between now and Labor Day." She picked up her teacup. "Gus, could we have another pot of tea for the ladies? They look like they need something to do with their mouths."

Gus, behind the counter, grinned into his coffee cup and said nothing.

Tessa picked up her coffee again. Her hands were shaking. She looked over her shoulder at Bev, who winked at her across the diner with the pure, unrepentant delight of a woman who had been waiting thirty years for an excuse to say exactly that.

"Thanks," Tessa said.

"Don't thank me," Bev said. "Credit Len. He taught me how to do it. He said the best way to shut up a gossip is to make her feel like she's been invited to the meeting."

On Friday evening, Owen took her to dinner.

It was not a date, he said, when he came up the drive at six in a clean blue shirt with his hair still damp from the shower, and he stood on the porch steps looking as nervous as she had ever seen him. It was dinner with his mother, who had asked to meet the woman her son had been talking about for three weeks, and who had threatened, via a series of increasingly pointed text messages, to come to Birch Street and find out what was going on herself if he did not bring her over.

"She's going to interrogate you," Owen said.

"Good," Tessa said, grabbing her keys. "I've been waiting all summer for someone to ask me a real question."

Rosa Reyes lived in a small yellow house on the north side of town, two blocks from the hospital, with a porch full of potted geraniums and a vegetable garden so neat it looked like a diagram. She opened the front door before they reached the steps, a small, straight-backed woman in her late sixties with iron-gray hair cut short and a pair of reading glasses hanging from a chain around her neck. She looked Tessa up and down, slowly and thoroughly, the way Tessa had seen Nora Pruitt look at a sofa.

"You're taller than I expected," Rosa said.

"I'm sorry," Tessa said. "I can stand in a hole if you'd prefer."

Rosa's mouth twitched. Then she laughed, a sudden, delighted bark, and stepped back to let them in.

"Owen," she said over her shoulder, "she's got a sense of humor. Good. Your father never had one. I'm going to have to sit down."

Dinner was pork chops, rice, green beans from the garden, and tamales, which Rosa had made that afternoon and stacked on a plate in the middle of the table with a sharp, pointed look at her son. They ate in a small, bright kitchen with a window over the sink that looked out at the back garden and a clothesline strung with dish towels. The radio was playing an old Spanish love song in the corner. Rosa talked, and Owen laughed, and Tessa found herself talking too, about the storage unit and the lamps and the Ledger in its oilcloth, and about Len, who had apparently once sat in this very kitchen and eaten six tamales in a row without saying a word.

"He always said mine were the only ones in the county worth the drive," Rosa said. "He drove all the way from Quarry Road every Sunday for ten years. Owen never once thought to ask why."

"I thought he just liked the drive," Owen said.

"He liked the drive," Rosa said. "And he liked the company. He was a lonely man, Owen. You should have noticed sooner."

Owen went quiet. He looked down at his plate.

Rosa turned her attention back to Tessa, and her face softened in a way that made her look, suddenly, much more like her son.

"So you're the niece," she said.

"I'm the niece."

"With the dog."

"With the dog."

"And the placemat."

"And the placemat," Tessa said. "I'm afraid it's famous now."

Rosa nodded slowly. "Gus framed a copy of it," she said. "Bev told me. She told me the whole thing, including the part where you ate your muffin with butter on your chin, which I thought was very endearing." She took a sip of coffee. "He's never brought a woman to this kitchen, you know. Not once. Not once. Not in all the years he's lived in this town."

"Mama," Owen said.

"I'm talking," Rosa said. "And I'm going to tell her the truth, because somebody has to in this family." She set her cup down on the table, very precisely. "You're the first one who ever came to dinner and didn't want to sell him something."

Tessa blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"Every woman who came to this town wanted something from him," Rosa said. "The houses, the lots, the lake view, the commission. They'd smile at him in the diner and ask him about the market, and then they'd ask him to help them find something cheap in Milwaukee. And Owen would say yes, because he's a good boy and a terrible salesman, and he'd end up with nothing but a dog and a sofa." She reached across the table and patted Tessa's hand, briskly. "You came up that drive with your uncle's lamps in your truck and told him he was wrong about the rug. That's the most honest thing I've heard about my son in ten years."

Owen made a sound like a man trying not to choke on a tamale.

"I'm going to get the dessert," he said, and got up and went to the stove.

Rosa leaned across the table, looked at her son's back, and lowered her voice to a conspiratorial whisper.

"Don't let him get away with it," she said. "He's going to try. He always tries to carry everything himself, even when somebody's standing right there offering to take the other end."

"I know," Tessa whispered back.

"Good girl," Rosa said. "Eat your tamale."

They ate the tamales. They ate a plate of Rosa's pan dulce, sweet and soft and dusted with sugar, and then they sat on the porch under the string of lights while the sun went down behind the hospital and the geraniums glowed red in the dusk. Owen sat on the top step with his elbows on his knees. Tessa sat beside him. Their shoulders touched, and neither of them moved.

"She likes you," Owen said.

"She threatened to take me to the hospital laundry if I broke your heart."

"That means she likes you."

"And she told me you carry everything yourself."

"That's because I do."

"Owen."

"I know," he said, and turned his head to look at her in the last gold light. "I'm going to ask you something, and you can say no."

"Ask."

"Would you come to the barn with me on Monday morning," he said, "and help me move the Route 9 inventory in before Joyce Kessler's inspector comes on the first? All of it, every table and lamp and rug, every piece with an O.R. tag and an MH tag and a note in Len's capitals? I can't do it alone. And I don't want to be the only one who knows where everything goes."

Tessa looked at him for a long moment.

"Ask me again on Monday," she said. "Ask me at seven, in the drive, with the dog in the truck. And I'll tell you whether I'm coming."

He laughed, very softly, and reached across the step and took her hand.

"I'll ask you at seven," he said.

"Good," Tessa said. "Bring coffee."

## Chapter 20: The Route 9 Move

At seven o'clock on Monday morning, Owen was standing in the drive on Birch Street with two coffees, a clipboard, and an expression of nervous hope that made Tessa want to laugh and hug him in the same breath.

"Oh?" he said.

"I'm coming," Tessa said, climbing into the cab of his pickup with the dog in the back seat and a box of Bev's cinnamon rolls on her lap. "But you're going to ask me every single time you want to pick up a lamp."

"That's fair."

"And you're going to tell me the truth when I ask whether the lamp is heavy."

"It's always heavy," Owen said. "That's why I ask you."

They drove out Route 9 in the early morning light, past the farm-supply store and the field of sunflowers, which had finally made up its mind and turned the whole hillside gold. Pinecrest Storage opened its gate for them at seven thirty. The manager, a thin, sun-darkened man named Ferris who had worked the north row for twenty years, came out of the office with a coffee mug in one hand and a clipboard in the other, and looked at the two of them, and the dog, and the pickup, and the open unit door, with the flat, unhurried patience of someone who had seen everything in the storage business at least twice.

"You're moving the inventory," he said.

"We're moving it to the barn," Tessa said. "On Quarry Road. Before the first of August."

"Len told me you'd say that," Ferris said. "He said, 'Ferris, when my niece comes in with a clipboard and a face like a thunderstorm, you tell her the unit's paid through December, and she can take whatever she wants, and she'll bring it all back by Labor Day because she can't stand to see a good table sitting empty.'" He took a sip from his mug. "He was right about most things, that man. You want me to help you carry?"

"Ask Owen," Tessa said. "He's the one who carries the couch."

Ferris laughed, a dry, pleasant sound, and set his mug down on the hood of his truck.

"Then I'll carry the lamps," he said. "Couch is yours."

It took them the whole day.

They moved in a rhythm that Tessa could not have planned and did not have to explain. Owen lifted, and she checked the tag. She called out the room and the initials, and he set each piece down in the right place in the bed of the pickup, wrapped in gray moving blankets and roped down with yellow nylon straps. The walnut desk went in first, with its brass pulls wrapped in tissue. The rattan chairs followed, then the pine table, then the framed prints, each one in its tissue, each one labeled in Len's pencil. Then the lamps, one by one, the urn and the banker's lamp and the tall bronze floor lamp with the pleated shade, until the pickup bed was crowded with brass and ceramic and linen, and the cab smelled of cedar and old wool and the sweet, dusty scent of a place that had been kept for a very long time.

At noon they stopped in the shade of the storage unit wall and ate Bev's cinnamon rolls with their fingers, sitting on the tailgate with their feet dangling in the gravel.

"Is it always like this?" Tessa asked.

"Meaning what?"

"Exactly this. Every day. Carrying things out of rooms that other people are going to live in, and putting them back in a barn so they don't get sold."

Owen wiped his fingers on his jeans. "Yeah," he said. "Mostly. Except when they do get sold, and you stand in the drive and watch the buyer drive away with the best lamp you ever had, and you tell yourself it's going to a better room."

"Did that ever happen to Len?"

"Every week," Owen said. "He cried over the Stickley desk twice. Once when he sold it to the Ostranders for four times what he paid, and once when he realized he'd been right about it all along and nobody was ever going to know."

Tessa looked at him sideways. "You're making that up."

"I'm not making anything up," Owen said, and his mouth curved. "I've been carrying his couches for two years, Tessa. I know exactly how much he cried."

She laughed, and he laughed with her, and for a moment they sat together on the tailgate in the sun, not talking, with the sunflowers rising gold beyond the fence and the dog asleep on the back seat with his nose buried in a moving blanket.

The barn was waiting for them when they got back to Quarry Road. Its red doors stood open to the morning air, and the hayloft door was propped wide, and the workbench was clear for the first time in months. Tessa stood at the edge of the drive and looked at it, the leaning walls and the rusted tin roof and the row of sugar maples screening the back field, and she felt, for the first time, something close to ownership, the kind that did not have to be argued about or written on the back of a menu.

"We'll unload," she said.

They unloaded until four. By the time the last table was carried in and set against the north wall, and the last lamp had been tagged and placed on the shelf, Tessa's back ached, her hands were scratched and raw, and Owen had a smear of dust across one cheek that made him look like a boy who had been playing in a coal bin.

"You've got—" she said, and gestured at her own face.

"Where?"

She reached up and wiped the dust from his cheek with her thumb. He went very still under her touch. Then he turned his head, very slightly, into her palm.

"Thanks," he said quietly.

"For what?"

"For asking me to come in the first place," he said. "I know I didn't deserve it."

"Don't make it a speech," Tessa said. "I'm too tired to cry."

"I'm not making a speech. I'm telling you the truth."

"Then tell it to the Board," Tessa said, and stepped back, and felt her cheeks go warm.

The car came up the drive at four thirty.

It was a gray county sedan with a faded seal on the door, and it stopped at the edge of the gravel with a crunch that made Moose sit up in the back seat of the pickup and bark once, sharply. A woman got out. She was in her late fifties, with iron-gray hair pulled back into a tight knot, a navy blazer over a white blouse, and a leather portfolio under one arm. She wore sensible shoes and a pair of reading glasses on a cord, and she looked at the barn, and the open doors, and the pickup, and the two of them standing side by side in the dust with the sunlight slanting across the field behind them, with the flat, assessing calm of someone who had spent thirty years telling people no.

"Ms. Marsh," she said. "Mr. Reyes. I'm Joyce Kessler, from the township zoning office. I'm not here for the hearing. I'm here for the barn."

"You're early," Owen said.

"I'm always early," Joyce said. "It's the only way to see what people are hiding."

"We're not hiding anything," Tessa said.

Joyce's eyes went past her, to the barn, to the north wall, where the tables and lamps and rugs had been arranged with their tags turned outward, every brass label catching the late light. She walked slowly toward the open doors, her portfolio held against her hip, and stopped in the threshold.

For a long moment, she said nothing at all.

"Ms. Marsh," she said at last, without turning around. "Who tagged these?"

"My uncle," Tessa said. "Len Marsh."

"And the others?"

"Owen Reyes," Tessa said. "And me."

Joyce turned. Her face was impassive, but her eyes moved from the tags to the lamps to the rolled-up rugs, and something in them shifted, very slightly, like a door opening an inch.

"Len's tags," she said. "I've seen them before. He sent me a set in 2016, with a note that said, 'Joyce, if you want to inspect my barn, you'll need to read my handwriting first.'" She paused. "I didn't read it. I sent him a letter instead. He framed the letter and hung it over his workbench, I'm told."

"He did," Owen said.

"Of course he did." Joyce pushed her glasses up her nose. "All right. I'm going to tell you what I'm going to do, and then I'm going to tell you what the Board is going to do, and then you can decide whether you want to argue with either of us."

"Go ahead," Tessa said.

"I'm going to inspect the barn," Joyce said. "All of it. The hayloft, the back room, the storage shelves, the Route 9 inventory, which I'm assuming is now in this building, since it's not in the unit on the township's list. I'm going to measure the parking. I'm going to check the traffic log that Hank Dressler has been sending me for six years, which says there have been forty-two customer vehicles at this address since January, most of them on Sunday afternoons." She paused. "And I'm going to tell you that I don't care about the lamps."

Tessa blinked. "You don't?"

"The Board doesn't care about the lamps," Joyce said. "The Board cares about the road. About Quarry Road, which is one lane wide at the bend, and about the parking, which is two cars on a good day and a school bus on a bad one. And the Board cares about the fact that this building is zoned for agricultural storage, which means no retail, no sales from the door, no customers after six in the evening, and no signs. You have a sign, Ms. Marsh."

"It's on the gate," Tessa said. "It's small."

"It says MARSH HOME STAGING in letters three inches high," Joyce said dryly. "And it has a painted lamp on it. The Board is going to find that very, very difficult to explain."

Owen let out a short, startled laugh. Joyce ignored him.

"So here is what I need from you," she said. "I need you to tell me, in writing, before the fourth of August, what you are actually doing in this building. Not what Len did. Not what your staging business used to be. What you intend to do, with these tables and lamps, and how many people you expect to come through that door."

"No one," Tessa said.

Joyce raised one eyebrow.

"No one," Tessa repeated. "We don't sell from the barn. We never have. The tags are for the inventory, so we know where each piece goes when we stage a house. Nobody walks in off the road. Nobody buys a lamp from the back door. Every sale happens in the house where the piece is going, with the owner or the seller present, and by appointment, in writing." She took a breath. "Len's inventory is a warehouse, Ms. Kessler. It's a set of tags, and a hayloft full of quilts, and a dog who won't sleep anywhere else. It's not a store."

Joyce stared at her for a long moment over the rims of her glasses.

"And the sign?" she asked.

"The sign comes down tonight," Owen said quietly. "And the lamp on it comes down with it."

"Good," Joyce said. "That's the first sensible thing I've heard about this building since 2015." She turned back toward the open doors and looked again at the north wall, the tags, the lamps. "Who wrote the tag on the brass lamp with the green shade?"

"Owen did," Tessa said. "He wrote do not sell without asking."

Joyce was quiet for a moment. Then she took a small notebook from the inside pocket of her blazer and wrote something in it, briefly.

"I'll put that in the file," she said, "under *Owner's Instructions*. It's the only thing in this barn that's ever going to make the Board smile."

She turned to go, then stopped, and looked back over her shoulder at Tessa.

"Ms. Marsh," she said.

"Yes?"

"Len wrote me a letter every year for nine years," Joyce said. "Two lines, sometimes three. He'd ask me how the township was doing, and whether I'd finally gotten the drainage on Quarry Road fixed, and whether I'd like to come to the barn for a cup of coffee. I never once went." She paused. "I'm going to the hearing on the fourth, Ms. Marsh, because the law requires me to, and because Hank Dressler is going to stand up and call your uncle's barn a public nuisance. And I'm going to have to say, on the record, what I saw in this building today. I want you to know that before you walk into that room. I'm not your enemy. I'm the person who has to explain to a board of four retired farmers and one nervous dentist why a man's life's work is being shut down over a lamp."

"Thanks," Tessa said.

"Don't thank me yet," Joyce said. "Bring me a plan. A real one, in writing, with the parking spaces drawn in pencil and the hours of operation printed in capitals. And bring me the dog. I've heard about him from every single person in this county, and I want to see if he's as much trouble as they say."

She walked back to the sedan with her portfolio under her arm and her shoes ringing on the gravel. At the car door she paused and looked back, once, at the barn, and at the two of them standing in the open doorway with the dust still on their faces and the sun going down behind the maples.

"It's a good building," she said. "I'll give him that."

Then she got in the car and drove away down Quarry Road, very slowly, the way a person drives when she is thinking about something she does not want to say out loud.

Owen let out a long breath.

"Oh," he said. "That went better than the lamp."

"Don't say that," Tessa said.

"I'm going to say it every time," Owen said, and reached for her hand, and she let him take it. "It's the only thing I know for sure about this town."

Moose came out of the pickup, shook himself vigorously, and trotted up to the barn door, where he sat down on the threshold and looked out at the field with the air of a dog who had been waiting for this moment his entire life.

"He's been waiting for her," Tessa said.

"He's been waiting for all of us," Owen said. "He just didn't know what it was for."

## Chapter 21: Dressler Marine

Hank Dressler's marina sat at the far end of Quarry Road, where the county blacktop gave out and the lake began in earnest. It had four long docks, a gas pump with a faded red sign, a cinder-block office with a screen door that had been patched with duct tape more times than Tessa could count, and a back field that ran down to the water in a rough, sloping meadow of goldenrod and wild grass. A chain-link fence surrounded the field. Behind it, a dozen boats sat on steel trailers under blue tarps, and a brand-new metal storage building stood at the top of the rise like a challenge.

"He built that in June," Owen said, parking the pickup beside a dented tow truck. "Without a permit. The township issued a stop-work order on the fourth, and Hank ignored it for two days, and then he finally got tired of looking at it and went to Joyce Kessler's office with a box of doughnuts and a list of grievances as long as my arm."

"Does he have a case?"

"He has a case against Len," Owen said. "He doesn't have a case against the barn. Not really." He turned off the engine and sat for a moment with his hands on the wheel. "I don't think he wants the barn shut down, Tessa. I think he wants the back field. He's wanted it since his father died, and Len wouldn't sell it to him, and every time Hank came up with a new offer Len just laughed and said he'd sooner give the field to the dog."

"And now?"

"Now he's got a steel building and a stop-work order and a boat he can't store anywhere else," Owen said, "and he's going to have to be humble to get any of it back. And Hank Dressler has never once been humble in his life."

Tessa looked out the windshield at the sloping goldenrod field, the blue tarps glinting in the sun, the lake beyond with its scatter of white sails.

"Then let's go be humble for him," she said, and opened the door.

Hank was in the boat yard, on his knees beside the hull of a twenty-foot runabout with a socket wrench in one hand and a cooler at his elbow. He was sixty years old, lean and sunburned, with a gray crew cut and a faded Dressler Marine T-shirt stretched across shoulders that had spent a lifetime lifting motors. He looked up as they came across the gravel, and his face closed like a fist.

"Reyes," he said. "I didn't know you were the kind of man who brought company to a fight."

"I'm not here to fight," Owen said.

"Then you're the first one who's ever come out here without a lawyer or a grudge." Hank's eyes moved to Tessa. "And you're Len's girl. The one who wrote the grocery list on the back of the lamp."

"I'm Tessa Marsh," Tessa said. "And I'm not going to pretend I'm sorry you filed the complaint, Mr. Dressler, because you know exactly what the barn looks like on a Sunday afternoon, and it's been a mess for twenty years."

Hank stared at her for a long moment. Then he set the wrench down on the cooler lid, very carefully, and stood up. He was a head taller than Owen, and he wiped his hands on a rag that was black with grease.

"Twenty years," he said. "You want to know what it's like to have a neighbor who takes out a permit to store chairs and then parks his whole life across your driveway? Your uncle had a sign on that gate that said Marsh Home Staging, and every Sunday for twenty years, I've watched a line of cars come up Quarry Road to look at his lamps. Doctors' wives. Schoolteachers. The men in khakis who wanted to know whether they could take a Stickley desk home for the weekend. And not one of them ever asked me whether I'd like to buy a boat."

"Did you ever ask my uncle to sell you the field?" Tessa asked.

"Every year," Hank said. "Every spring. Every time I saw him at the hardware store. And every time he'd laugh at me and say, 'Hank, you'd put a boat on the moon if the township would let you park it.'" His jaw worked. "He said that to my face, in front of the whole counter. He said it in front of my son."

"Your son?"

Hank's expression changed, very slightly, like the surface of the lake when a cloud passes over it.

"Eli," he said quietly. "He used to work here. Summers, when he was in high school. He wanted to learn the engines, and he wanted to be a marine mechanic, and my father wouldn't let him touch a wrench until he was sixteen. Len used to come down to the marina every Saturday morning for a cup of coffee and a bag of bait, and he'd sit on that bench by the pump, and he'd watch Eli work on the engines. He used to tell Eli he had good hands." Hank looked away, toward the lake. "Eli moved to Minneapolis in 2019. He's an engineer for a company that builds boats for the navy. He calls me every Sunday, and he never once asks whether I've sold the back field."

Tessa did not say anything. She looked at Hank Dressler's hands, which were still black with grease and very still at his sides, and she thought about her uncle sitting on a bench by a gas pump, watching a boy fix an engine and telling him he had good hands.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"Don't be sorry," Hank said gruffly. "Be a neighbor. Len was never a neighbor to me. He was a stubborn old fool who sold chairs out of a barn and wouldn't let anybody else in on the joke."

"He was a stubborn old fool," Owen said, quietly. "And he loved this town more than anybody I ever met. He wrote everything down. Every sale, every complaint, every tag. He wrote you down, Hank. There's a line about you in the Ledger."

Hank looked up sharply. "What does it say?"

"It says, 'Hank's going to be the death of me, and he's going to be right about the traffic, and he's going to cry when he sees the field.'" Owen's mouth twitched. "I think Len was trying to be funny."

Hank made a sound that was half a laugh and half a cough, and turned away, pressing the back of his wrist against his mouth.

"That son of a gun," he said thickly.

"He was," Tessa said.

They stood in silence for a long moment, the three of them, in the bright glare of the boat yard. A gull cried somewhere over the water. Out on the lake, a sail tacked slowly toward the breakwater, its white triangle catching the sun.

"Here's what I'm going to offer you," Tessa said.

Hank turned back. His eyes were wet, but his jaw was set.

"I'm not going to sell you the back field," she said. "I'm not going to sell it to anybody, because it's not mine to sell, not yet, and when it is, I'm going to make that decision myself. But I'll lease it to you. For the winter. For boat storage, the way Len always said he would if you asked him properly. Five years, at a fair rate, with a written agreement, and a clause that says you can't build anything else on it without going to the township first."

Hank stared at her.

"And in return?" he asked, carefully.

"In return, you withdraw the complaint," Tessa said. "Before the fourth. In writing. And you stand up at the hearing, if you have to, and say that the barn was never a store, and the Route 9 inventory is in the building where the Board can see it, and the traffic is going to be a great deal better once there are no customers on Sundays."

"Why would I do that?"

"Because you're tired," Tessa said. "I can see it in your face. You've been fighting this for twenty years, and you don't want the field anymore. You want the boat to float, and your son to call on Sundays, and a neighbor to stop treating you like a problem. And because my uncle would have wanted you to have the field, even if he never once said so out loud."

Hank was silent. He looked at the steel building at the top of the rise, the stop-work order taped to its door, the blue tarps on the boats. He looked at Owen, who said nothing. He looked at Tessa, for a long time, with a strange, complicated expression that was not quite anger and not quite surrender.

"Five years," he said at last.

"Five years," Tessa said.

"And the clause?"

"The clause is in writing. Two copies. One for you, one for the township."

Hank nodded slowly. "And the sign?"

"The sign comes down tonight," Owen said.

"And the lamp on it?"

"The lamp stays," Tessa said. "It's the only thing about that sign anybody likes."

Hank Dressler actually smiled then, a brief, startled, almost boyish smile, the kind of expression a man might wear when he has been caught doing something he had forgotten he enjoyed.

"Len would have said no," he said.

"Len would have said no for twenty years," Tessa said, "and then he'd have called you on a Sunday and told you the lease was ready."

Hank laughed out loud, a deep, rough bark that made the gull lift off the dock and wheel away over the water.

"Yeah," he said. "He would have."

He picked up the socket wrench from the cooler, turned it over in his greasy hand, and looked at it for a moment, as if it were a thing he had not held in years.

"You'll want to come back and see the field in September," he said gruffly. "The goldenrod's going to be out. Len always said it was the best thing about the lake, and he was right about that, too."

"We'll come," Tessa said.

"Bring the dog," Hank said. "Eli's going to want to meet him. He's been asking me for years whether we'd ever get a golden retriever for the marina."

"He's a hayloft dog," Owen said. "He doesn't like boats."

"Everybody likes boats," Hank said. "They just don't know it yet."

They walked back up the gravel toward the pickup in silence. Halfway there, Owen stopped and looked back, over his shoulder, at the boat yard and the steel building and the sloping goldenrod field, bright and gold in the midsummer sun.

"You did that," he said.

"We did that."

"You did that," Owen said again, quietly. "I was going to sell him the lot for eighty thousand dollars and a handshake. I'd been working on the offer for three weeks."

Tessa stopped walking. She turned to look at him.

"You were going to sell the field?"

"Not the field," Owen said. "The lot next to it. The one that's been on the township's list since 2019. Hank's been trying to get it for his boat storage, and I've got a buyer from Oak Park who wants it for a summer rental." He looked away. "It was a bad idea. I know that now. I'd have made a commission and lost the only friend Len ever had in this town."

"Hank wasn't Len's friend."

"Hank was the closest thing he had to one," Owen said, "that didn't come to the barn with a clipboard and a bag of Bev's pie."

Tessa looked at him for a long moment in the bright July light. He looked tired, and a little ashamed, and very, very honest, and she felt something inside her soften that she had been trying to keep hard for a month.

"Don't sell the lot," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"I know you weren't," Tessa said. "But I want you to promise me something anyway."

"Whatever."

"Ask me first," she said. "Every time, Owen. Every lot, every lamp, every buyer from Oak Park. Ask me first, even if you think you already know the answer, because I'm not going to be the woman who finds out about a deal on the back of a menu."

Owen was quiet for a moment. Then he smiled, slowly, the way he had on the first morning in the drive, when he had held out his hand and asked for a truce.

"Can I ask you now?" he said.

"Ask me."

"Would you come to Lake Days this weekend, and sit in a booth with me, and let me buy you a lemonade, and watch the fireworks from the end of the pier, and not tell me once that I'm being an idiot?"

"That's four questions," Tessa said.

"I know," Owen said. "I'm trying to be efficient."

She laughed, and took his hand, and they walked the rest of the way to the pickup together through the warm, gold afternoon, with the lake glittering beyond the fence and the dog barking hopefully from the cab, as though he had just heard the word *boat* and was not yet sure whether he approved.

"Yes," Tessa said.

"To which one?"

"All of them," she said. "Except the lemonade. I want the good stuff, from Gus, with the cherries in it."

"Deal," Owen said, and opened the passenger door for her, and bowed, very slightly, with a straight face that broke into a grin the moment she looked at him.

## Chapter 22: Lake Days

Lucy Ambrose stepped off the Friday train in Larkspur Lake wearing white sneakers, a striped sundress, and a pair of enormous sunglasses, and she stood on the platform of the tiny brick depot with her hands on her hips and her suitcase at her feet, looking around the town with the expression of a woman who had been promised a fairy tale and was prepared to be disappointed.

"Oh," she said, after a long moment. "Oh, Tess. It's *perfect*. It's a postcard. Somebody has to be lying about this."

"Nobody's lying," Tessa said, hugging her hard across the platform. "The lake is exactly this blue, and the diner is exactly this loud, and the pie is exactly this dangerous."

"Where's the man?"

"Which one?"

"The one with the dog," Lucy said, taking off her sunglasses and looking at Tessa with narrowed eyes. "The one who keeps your uncle's lamps in a barn. The one who sent you a text on the train last month that said, and I quote, *She's going to hate me for a week, and then she's going to forgive me, and then she's going to make me carry something heavy for the rest of my life.*"

"He sent you a text?"

"He sent me a text," Lucy said with great satisfaction, "because you wouldn't give him my number and he didn't have the sense to ask you first. So he found me through my cousin Connie. I'm told that's very Wisconsin."

"That's very Owen," Tessa said, and felt her face go hot.

Owen was waiting by the pickup in the parking lot behind the depot, leaning against the tailgate with the dog sitting beside him and a paper bag of cherry lemonade in one hand. He straightened up when he saw them, and his face did something complicated and shy, and Moose stood up, wagged his entire back half, and launched himself at Lucy's sundress with an enthusiastic whine.

"Oh, no," Lucy said, delighted. "Oh, no, no, no, he's *yours*. Tess, he's *yours*."

"He's Len's," Tessa said.

"He's yours now," Lucy said, kneeling in the gravel to rub the dog's ears. She looked up at Owen over the golden head with a bright, appraising stare. "And you must be the realtor."

"I'm the realtor," Owen said.

"Lucy Ambrose," Lucy said, standing up and holding out her hand. "Tessa's roommate for six years, and the only person in Chicago who has ever told her no and stuck to it. You're going to have to carry my suitcase."

"I'm very good at carrying things," Owen said, taking the suitcase in one hand and her hand in the other, and shaking both with a grave, polite smile.

"So I've been told," Lucy said. "By Brent Malloy. Who called me on Tuesday to ask whether you'd been seen coming out of the Dock Room with a brass lamp under each arm. I told him it was a lamp, not a child, and he should mind his own business."

"That's very kind of you," Owen said.

"It's not kind," Lucy said. "It's self-defense. I'm going to need someone to drive me home at midnight, and I'd rather it was a man who can carry a couch than a man who can sell one."

They drove into town with the windows down and the radio playing, past the sunflower field and the farm-supply store and the cedar-lined road to the lake. The town had gone festive for Lake Days. Red and white bunting hung from every lamppost along Front Street, and the bait shop had a banner across its sign that read WELCOME LAKE DAYS, WE LOVE OUR LIMIT, and someone had strung paper lanterns from the pier to the bandstand in the town square. The square itself was crowded already, with food trucks lined up along the curb, a kettle corn stand sending a sweet cloud across the grass, and a row of white tents where the Garden Club sold preserves and quilts and hand-painted birdhouses to anybody with a dollar and a sunny disposition.

"It's a county fair," Lucy said, staring at the bunting. "It's a county fair with a lake."

"It's Lake Days," Tessa said. "We've had it for a hundred and twelve years. It's the only week of the year when everybody in Larkspur Lake stops arguing about zoning."

"How long does it last?"

"Until the fireworks," Owen said.

"And when are the fireworks?"

"Tomorrow night," Tessa said. "Out at the end of the pier. And on Sunday afternoon, the Board holds its open session in the town hall, where anyone can come and complain about the parking, and the hearing for the barn is the following Tuesday."

"So this weekend," Lucy said slowly, "is the calm before the storm."

"This weekend," Owen said, "is pie."

Lucy laughed, and it was a real laugh, and Tessa felt something in her chest unclench a little more.

Brent Malloy called at eleven o'clock on Saturday morning, while Tessa was standing in the middle of the town square with a paper cup of lemonade in one hand and a lemon bar in the other, watching Lucy try to win a stuffed bass at the ring-toss booth. Owen was on the other side of the square, arguing good-naturedly with Gus about whether the new kettle corn was too sweet. Bev was sitting on a folding chair under the Garden Club tent, judging a pie contest with an iron hand and a mouth full of blueberry filling.

Tessa stepped out of the crowd, behind the kettle corn stand, where the noise was less.

"Brent," she said.

"Tessa," Brent said. "It's August first."

"I know what day it is."

"I've been holding the Milwaukee office for you since Monday," Brent said. He sounded tired, and a little impatient, and something under the impatience that she could not quite name. "Regina has the paperwork on her desk. The buyers' lawyers have called three times. I've told them you're a very serious woman, and that you'll give them an answer by the end of the day, and I've told them the same thing every day for three weeks, and I'm starting to look like an idiot."

"Then I'll make you look smart," Tessa said.

There was a long pause on the line.

"Go on," Brent said.

Tessa took a breath. She looked across the square at the bunting and the kettle corn and the sunlight glittering on the lake beyond the bandstand. She looked at Owen, laughing with Gus, a paper cup of coffee in one hand. She looked at Lucy, still trying to win the bass, her sunglasses pushed up in her hair. She looked at Bev, under the Garden Club tent, with a pie knife in her hand and a glint in her eye that could have stripped paint.

"I'm not selling," she said.

Brent said nothing.

"I'm not selling the business," Tessa said. "Not to you, not to the buyers, not to anybody. Marsh Home Staging is going to stay in Larkspur Lake, in the barn on Quarry Road, with the tags and the inventory and the dog. And I'm going to run it, with an agreement that has the forty cents on the dollar in writing, and a partner who carries the couch, and an inspector from the township who thinks I'm a nuisance. And I'm going to call that the most sensible thing I've ever decided."

"You're making a mistake," Brent said quietly.

"Maybe," Tessa said. "But it's my mistake to make, Brent. And I'll tell you what I'd like instead, if you're willing to hear it."

"Go on."

"The Blackwood Group is building four houses on the north shore," Tessa said. "You want a stager who knows the water. I'm the only one who does, and I'll tell you right now that the best views in that county are going to be wasted on a Chicago crew that's never once seen a lake freeze. So I'll give you the Blackwood jobs. Every one. For a referral fee, of course, the standard rate, paid in writing, and I'll handle the install myself, with my own team, from Larkspur Lake. You get the contract and the commission. I get the work and the view. And nobody has to buy anybody's uncle's lamps."

Brent was silent for so long that she thought the line had dropped.

"You want me to subcontract to a barn," he said at last, in a voice that sounded almost amused.

"I want you to subcontract to the best stager you ever trained," Tessa said. "She just happens to live in a barn."

Brent laughed. It was a short, surprised, not entirely unhappy sound.

"Len used to say that," he said.

"He did?"

"Every Tuesday for six years," Brent said. "He'd say, 'Brent, your best closer is going to end up in a barn someday, and when she does, you'll wish you'd bought her a nicer chair.' I thought he was being sentimental." He paused. "I think I'm going to have to call Regina and tell her to tear up the paperwork."

"Tell her to keep the paperwork," Tessa said. "Place it in a drawer. Name it *Ask First.*"

"Ask first?"

"It's a rule," Tessa said. "It's a good one. You should try it sometime."

"I'll try it when I'm dead," Brent said, but his voice was warm now, and tired, and almost fond. "All right, Tessa. Referral agreement. Standard rate. Send it to my office in writing by Monday. And if the Blackwood people ask why their north-shore house is being staged by a woman with a dog, I'm going to tell them the truth."

"Tell them the light always tells the truth," Tessa said.

"What does that mean?"

"It means my uncle was right," Tessa said, and felt her throat tighten, "and you should have listened to him."

"Yeah," Brent said quietly. "I should have. Good luck, Tess. Eat something."

The line went dead.

Tessa stood behind the kettle corn stand for a long moment, with the lemonade going warm in her hand and the sugary smell of the stand drifting around her, and she felt, for the first time since February, something like peace.

"Did you do it?"

She turned around. Lucy was standing at the edge of the stand, with the stuffed bass tucked under one arm and her sunglasses back on her nose, watching her with bright, anxious eyes.

"I did it," Tessa said.

"You're not selling?"

"I'm not selling."

"You're going to stay?"

"I'm going to stay," Tessa said, and her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "For the summer. For the hearing, and the house, and the dog, and the lamps. And after that, I'm going to make a list, Lucy, and it's going to have a line on it that says *Owen* in capital letters, and I'm going to decide what to do about the rest of it when I can see the lake from the top step."

Lucy stared at her. Then she let out a sound that was half a squeal and half a sob, dropped the stuffed bass on the grass, and threw both arms around Tessa's neck so hard that the lemonade went flying.

"I knew it," Lucy said fiercely, into her shoulder. "I knew it. I told Brent you'd never sell that barn. I told him you'd rather die in a storm with a dog and a realtor than sign a piece of paper in a Chicago office on a Monday."

"It was a Friday," Tessa said, laughing and crying at once.

"It was always going to be a Friday," Lucy said. "You're impossible. I love you. Now give me the bass before the ring-toss man tries to sell it back to me."

They laughed together in the shade of the kettle corn stand, with the sun on the grass and the band warming up under the bandstand and the bunting snapping in the breeze off the lake. And when Tessa turned back toward the square, she saw Owen coming toward them through the crowd, his hands full of two paper cups of lemonade and a plate of Gus's cherry pie, his face lit up with a question he had clearly been trying very hard not to ask.

He stopped a few feet away and held out the lemonade.

"Ask me," Tessa said.

"I don't have to ask," Owen said quietly. "I can see it on your face."

"Ask me anyway."

He looked at her for a long moment, the crowd swirling around them, the bunting snapping overhead, the lake glittering blue and endless past the bandstand. Then he smiled, very slowly, and held out the plate of pie.

"Did you sell it?" he asked.

"No," Tessa said. "I'm keeping the house."

"Good," said Owen, and handed her the pie, and did not let go of the plate until she had taken it, and then did not let go of her hand. "Then I'm going to need you to hold on to something for me, too."

"What?"

"The lemonade," he said, and grinned. "It's getting warm."

## Chapter 23: The Plan in Pencil

The kitchen table at Birch Street was covered by Sunday night with three sheets of graph paper, two cups of cold coffee, a plate of Bev's lemon bars with one bite missing, and the hand-drawn map of Quarry Road that Tessa had been trying to get right since Saturday.

"Two spaces," Owen said, leaning over her shoulder with a pencil in his hand. "Here, by the gate. Not three. Joyce said two on a good day."

"Two is for customers," Tessa said. "We don't have customers."

"We have Bev," Owen said. "On Sundays. With a pie."

"Bev doesn't park. Bev walks from Birch Street with a casserole dish on her head and makes a speech about the township on the way."

"Mark her down as a customer, then. Two spaces, marked with paint, and a note that says *Pedestrian Access Only, Mrs. Halvorsen Exempt*."

Tessa snorted and wrote it down in capitals, because Joyce Kessler had said the hours of operation had to be printed in capitals, and the Board liked to see things in writing that could not be argued with later.

The plan was taking shape. It was not elegant. It was twelve pages long, typed on Owen's laptop at the kitchen table in a font Bev had insisted was *more dignified than that nonsense the children use*, and it was covered with pencil notes in two different hands, Tessa's neat and slanted, Owen's cramped and impatient. It listed the hours: BY APPOINTMENT ONLY, MONDAY THROUGH FRIDAY, 9:00 A.M. TO 4:00 P.M., NO ACCESS ON SUNDAYS, NO SIGNAGE OTHER THAN ONE SIX-INCH PLAQUE AT THE GATE. It listed the parking: TWO SPACES, PAINTED, LOCATED WITHIN THE GATE. It listed the inventory: ALL ITEMS FROM LAKESIDE STORAGE UNIT 9, RT. 9, TO BE RELOCATED TO THE QUARRY ROAD BARN BY AUGUST 1, AND THE UNIT LEASE TERMINATED IN WRITING. It listed the tags. It listed the sale of goods: NONE FROM THE BARN. ALL SALES CONDUCTED ON-SITE AT THE PROPERTY WHERE THE ITEM IS PLACED, BY AGREEMENT, IN WRITING. And at the bottom, in Tessa's careful hand, under a line that said *Owner's Instructions*, was a single sentence Owen had insisted on adding, and which she had decided, after a long argument, to leave in.

*Brass lamp, green shade, Dock Room, Birch Street: DO NOT SELL WITHOUT ASKING.*

"Joyce is going to laugh," Owen said.

"Joyce is going to put it in the file," Tessa said. "She said so."

"She's going to laugh and put it in the file."

"Then she'll have something to read on the drive home."

He grinned and pulled out the chair beside her and sat down, heavily, with his own cup of coffee, and for a moment they both looked at the twelve pages in silence. The kitchen was warm and quiet. Through the window, the sun was going down over the cedars, turning the lake to a sheet of hammered copper, and somewhere down the street Bev was singing an old hymn in her backyard, slightly flat, to the tune of a hose.

"It's a good plan," Owen said at last.

"It's a boring plan."

"It's a boring plan that's going to keep the barn open," Owen said. "That's the only kind of plan that ever worked in this town. Your uncle used to say the best ideas were the ones nobody remembered, because nobody had to argue with them."

"My uncle," Tessa said, "would have hated this plan."

"He'd have called it a funeral for fun," Owen said, "and then he'd have signed it and framed it over the workbench."

She laughed. The sound was easy now, and she did not have to think about whether it was allowed.

Hank Dressler's letter came on Monday morning, in a plain white envelope with a Dressler Marine return address and a single line written across the front in heavy black marker: *TO: THE TOWNSHIP. FROM: THE COMPLAINANT. READ THIS FIRST.*

Tessa opened it on the porch steps at Birch Street while Owen was measuring the railing with a tape. Inside was a single sheet of paper, typed on a manual typewriter, with a crooked signature at the bottom and a stain in one corner that might have been coffee or motor oil.

*To the Larkspur Lake township zoning office, Attn: Joyce Kessler. I withdraw my complaint regarding the Quarry Road barn, filed June 2. I was wrong about the Sunday traffic, and I was wrong about the sign. The barn has never been a store. The traffic is worse at the marina than it ever was at the barn. I have leased the back field to Marsh Home Staging for winter storage at a fair rate, under a written agreement, and I will stand up at the hearing if the Board asks me to say so. — Henry Dressler.*

Under the signature, in smaller letters, he had added a single line.

*Tell Len I got the field. He'll know what I mean.*

Tessa read it twice, then handed it to Owen without a word. He read it, and his face changed, and he sat down on the step beside her and rested his forearms on his knees.

"He's going to stand up at the hearing," Owen said quietly.

"He says so."

"Hank Dressler has never once in his life stood up in a room and said he was wrong."

"Then he's going to be very uncomfortable on Tuesday," Tessa said, "and he's going to be brave about it, and Joyce is going to put it in the file under *Miscellaneous Acts of Decency*."

"That's not a real file."

"It is now."

Owen laughed, and then he stopped laughing, and he put his hand on the back of her neck and leaned his forehead against her temple, very lightly, the way a man might lean against a door he was not sure he was allowed to open.

"I'm proud of you," he said.

"You're not supposed to say that before the hearing."

"I'm not saying it before the hearing. I'm saying it now, on a Monday, on the porch, because I can see the lake from here and I've got a dog on my foot and I don't want to waste it."

"Owen."

"Yes?"

"Ask me something."

He lifted his head and looked at her. His eyes were very serious, and very warm, and she could see in them the same tired, careful hope she had seen on the hayloft floor and the pier and the stairs at Ray Pruitt's office.

"Would you have dinner with me on Friday," he said, "after the Ostrander house closes on Friday, at the table in my mother's kitchen, and let her threaten you with another plate of tamales, and then let me drive you home along Route 9 with the windows down, and not say anything about Joyce Kessler the whole way?"

"That's four questions again."

"I'm trying to be efficient."

"Yes," Tessa said. "To all of it, except the part about not saying anything about Joyce."

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to need to practice what I'm going to say to her on Tuesday," Tessa said, "and you're going to have to listen to every word and tell me if I sound like an idiot."

"You'll never sound like an idiot."

"Then tell me that, too."

He smiled, slowly, and she felt the warmth of it all the way down to her toes.

"You'll never sound like an idiot," he said.

"Liar."

"Yes," Owen said. "I'm a terrible liar. Ask anybody in town."

They spent the rest of Monday in the barn. Joyce Kessler's office had sent a second letter, in a brown envelope this time, requesting a written inventory of every item in the building, with its owner's initials and its room of origin, and a list of every piece that had been moved in or out of the township since June. It was a fool's errand, and Tessa knew it, and she also knew that Joyce had asked for it precisely because she wanted to see whether anyone would finish it. So they sat at the workbench under the high windows, with the afternoon light falling across the oak floorboards and the dog snoring at their feet, and they counted.

It took four hours. There were two hundred and eleven items on the list, and forty-three of them had tags with two sets of initials, and nine had been moved between Route 9 and the barn three times in as many years. There was a Stickley desk with a fake brass plate that had been sold to the Ostranders, and then returned, and then sold again to a retired dentist in Oak Park who had never once used it. There was a painting of a sailboat that Owen said was the ugliest thing he had ever seen, and Tessa said was the only thing in the building that Len had ever signed in full, and they argued about it for twenty minutes before Owen finally gave in and wrote *Keep* on the line beside it, in pencil, with a small, reluctant flourish.

"There," he said. "Now you're happy."

"I'm not happy," Tessa said. "I'm finished."

"Those are the same thing when it comes to Len's paintings," Owen said.

At six o'clock, Ethel Sorensen came up the drive.

Tessa did not recognize her at first. She was wearing a plain blue dress and a pair of sensible flats, and she had her hair pinned up in a tight gray knot, and she was carrying a large covered dish in both hands, with the expression of a woman who had been asked to deliver an apology and had decided that the easiest way to do it was to bring food.

"Ms. Marsh," she said stiffly, from the edge of the gravel. "I'm Ethel Sorensen. I chair the Garden Club."

"I know who you are," Tessa said.

"I was rude to you back in June," Ethel said. "In the diner. I said some things about you and Mr. Reyes that I should not have said in front of Gus Pelletier and the pie case, and I want you to know that I'm sorry. I've been sorry ever since. Bev Halvorsen has been telling me so every morning since I said it, and she has a very loud voice, and I had to sit down in the middle of the diner and listen." She held out the dish. "It's rhubarb. My mother's recipe. I make it every summer for the Lake Days pie contest, and I've never once won, because Bev always gets the blue ribbon. I'd like you to have some, if you'll accept it."

Tessa took the dish. It was still warm.

"Thanks," she said.

"And I'd like to say one more thing," Ethel said, very quietly, "about your uncle. He came to every Garden Club meeting for twenty years, and he sat in the back and never once said a word, and then he'd stand up at the end and ask us whether we'd like to see his barn. Nobody ever went. I thought he was a crank." She looked past Tessa, toward the barn with its doors open and its hayloft window glowing in the late light. "I was wrong. And I'd like to come to the hearing on Tuesday and say so, on the record, in front of Joyce Kessler and God and Hank Dressler, if you'll let me."

Tessa looked at the older woman for a long moment, at the stiff back and the trembling hands and the rhubarb dish warm against her palms.

"Yes," she said. "Go and say it."

Ethel's shoulders loosened, very slightly.

"Thanks," she said. Then she turned and walked back down the drive without another word, and Tessa watched her go, and felt the rhubarb dish warm and heavy in her arms.

"Who was that?" Owen asked, from the barn door.

"The Garden Club," Tessa said. "She's coming to the hearing."

"To say what?"

"That she was wrong," Tessa said. "About everything."

Owen came down the step and took the dish out of her hands, very gently, and looked at it, and then at her.

"Len would have framed that," he said.

"He would have framed the dish."

"He'd have made Ethel pay for the frame," Owen said, and his mouth twitched, and then he put one arm around her shoulders and pulled her close against his side, and they stood together in the warm evening light at the edge of the drive, watching the sun drop behind the maples, with the rhubarb dish between them and the dog at their heels and the whole of Larkspur Lake going quiet and gold beyond the field.

"Tomorrow," Tessa said.

"Tomorrow," Owen agreed.

"Are you scared?"

"Of Joyce?"

"Of the Board."

"Of the Board," Owen said, after a moment, "I'm terrified. Of losing you," he added, very quietly, "I was terrified for two years. I'm getting used to it."

She turned her head and looked up at him. His face in the evening light was tired and steady, and it was the face of a man who had carried a great many couches and had finally, after all of them, found somebody willing to take the other end.

"Then don't get used to it," Tessa said. "Just hold on to the other end, and I'll hold on to mine, and we'll see what happens on Tuesday."

"That's the plan," Owen said.

"That's the plan," Tessa agreed, and rested her head against his shoulder, and for a long while neither of them said anything at all.

## Chapter 24: The Hearing

The Larkspur Lake town hall had been built in 1911 as a schoolhouse, and it still smelled like one: chalk dust, floor wax, and the faint, sour ghost of a hundred years of wet wool coats hung on iron hooks along the back wall. The room had tall windows that let in the last of the evening light, a raised oak dais with five chairs behind a long table, a flag in one corner, and a portrait of the township's first board chair, who had a white beard and the disapproving eyes of a man who had never once been surprised by anything.

By six forty-five, every folding chair had been taken.

Tessa sat in the second row, on the aisle, with the Ledger in her lap and the twelve-page plan in a clear plastic sleeve on top of it. Owen sat beside her, close enough that their knees touched, with his hands folded tightly on his own copy of the plan and his jaw set in the particular way that meant he was rehearsing every word in his head. Moose lay under the row of chairs at their feet, his chin on Tessa's sneaker, pretending to be asleep and sighing every time someone walked past in squeaky shoes.

Bev Halvorsen sat on Tessa's other side, in a pale blue dress with a white collar and a straw hat with a silk band, and she held a black leather handbag in her lap with both hands, like a woman about to pass judgment on a casserole.

"Relax, dear," Bev said. "You look like you're going to be sick on the dog."

"I'm going to be sick on the dog," Tessa said.

"You're not going to be sick on the dog. You're going to stand up and tell the Board the truth, and Joyce Kessler is going to love you for it, because she's been waiting nine years for someone to tell that Board the truth without a lawyer in the room."

"Did she tell you that?"

"She told Gus," Bev said. "And Gus told me, while he was slicing my blueberry pie, and he told me in such a way that I'm sure he was thinking about the lamp."

Gus was seated at the end of the third row, in a pressed white shirt and a bolo tie that Tessa had never seen before, with his arms folded across his chest and a look of immense seriousness on his face. He caught her eye and gave her a single, solemn nod, as if he were a pallbearer at a funeral and she was the family.

Ethel Sorensen was on the far side of the room, next to the Garden Club women, with a sheaf of typed notes folded in her lap and her spine as straight as a pool cue. Rosa Reyes sat beside her, in a black cardigan and reading glasses, with her handbag on her knees and an expression that suggested she had come to watch her son make a fool of himself and had not yet decided whether to enjoy it.

Hank Dressler came in at six fifty, late, in a clean button-down shirt and pressed khakis, with a boy of about twenty-five at his side. The young man was tall and lean and had his father's jaw and his mother's careful way of looking at a room before he entered it. He stopped at the back of the hall and looked around at the crowded chairs with a mixture of nerves and curiosity, and then his eyes found Tessa, and he lifted one hand in an awkward, half-embarrassed wave.

"That's Eli," Owen said quietly. "He flew in from Minneapolis last night. Hank called me at ten to say so, and then he hung up without saying anything else."

"Why did he come?"

"Because his father asked him to," Owen said. "And because he wanted to see the lake before he went home."

Joyce Kessler came in through the side door at six fifty-eight, with her portfolio under her arm and her reading glasses on their cord, and she walked to the table at the front of the room without looking at anyone. She sat down in the chair at the end, opened the portfolio, and laid out three typed pages in neat, precise order, with a fountain pen beside them and a glass of water she did not touch.

At seven o'clock precisely, the Board chair, a broad-shouldered farmer named Gerald Lund with a sunburned face and a pair of heavy, black-framed glasses, rapped his gavel once on the oak table and called the meeting to order.

The Board sat in a row behind him. Tessa counted them without meaning to. There were four men and one woman, all in their sixties or seventies, and at the far end of the row, a thin, nervous-looking man in a navy suit who kept touching his jaw with two fingers and looked as though he would rather be somewhere, anywhere, with a drill in his hand.

"Dr. Penwell," Bev whispered. "The dentist. He's been on the board for twelve years, and he's never once voted against anything he didn't have to. He's the reason the township has a sidewalk."

The first item on the agenda was the Quarry Road barn. Joyce Kessler stood up.

She read her report in a flat, clear voice, without notes, and she did not leave anything out. She described the complaint, filed June 2 by the owner of the adjoining marina, alleging retail activity in violation of the agricultural storage permit. She described the inspection, conducted on June 29 by herself, in the presence of the owner's representatives, which had found that the barn contained no retail display, no sales counter, and no signage beyond a small painted sign on the front gate, which the owner had voluntarily removed on July 31. She described the inventory, two hundred and eleven items, each with a tag, each recorded in writing, all relocated from a storage unit on Route 9 to the barn by August 1, in accordance with a lease termination filed the same day. She described the traffic log, which showed forty-two customer vehicles at the address since January, all of them on Sunday afternoons, and which the owner of the adjoining marina had since withdrawn in writing.

"Mr. Dressler has withdrawn his complaint," Joyce said, looking up from the page. "In a letter dated August 3, which I have in my possession and which I will read into the record, if the Board wishes. He has also agreed to lease the back field of the Quarry Road parcel to the applicant for winter storage, under a written agreement with a five-year term, which I have reviewed and found in order."

A murmur went around the room. Gerald Lund raised one heavy hand, and it stopped.

"Is Mr. Dressler here?" he asked.

Hank stood up at the back of the hall. He did not walk forward. He stood where he was, with his big hands at his sides and his son's arm steady against his shoulder, and he cleared his throat twice before he could speak.

"I'm here," he said.

"Do you stand by the letter?" Gerald asked.

"I do," Hank said, and his voice cracked slightly on the last word. He cleared his throat again and looked directly at the Board. "I was wrong about the traffic. I was wrong about the sign. I was wrong about Len Marsh, and I told him so at the marina last week, which he would've loved, because he'd have said I was finally making sense." He stopped. "I was wrong for twenty years. And I'd like to say, on the record, that I'm glad the barn is still standing. Because the man who built it is the only reason anybody in this county knows how to tell a fake Stickley from a real one."

There was a long, quiet pause. Then, from the Garden Club side of the room, a single voice said, very clearly, "Hear, hear."

It was Ethel Sorensen.

Gerald Lund looked at her over the top of his glasses, and then at Hank, and then he nodded, slowly, and wrote something on a pad in front of him.

"Thanks, Mr. Dressler," he said. "Please sit down."

Hank sat. His son put a hand on his back, very lightly, and left it there.

Then the chair opened the floor for public comment.

Gus Pelletier was the first to stand. He walked to the front of the room, in his pressed shirt and bolo tie, and stood before the Board with his hands clasped behind his back like a judge at a county fair.

"I'm the owner of the Dock Street Diner," he said. "I've been serving breakfast in this town for thirty-one years. I know the Marsh family. I know the barn. And I know the pie crust in Bev Halvorsen's kitchen is better than mine, which is the only thing I've ever lost an argument about in my life." He paused, and a ripple of laughter went around the room. "And I'm here to tell you that the placemat in my diner, the one with the blue ink on it, is the only binding contract I've ever witnessed in this county. Len wrote it on a menu in 2023 and I signed it as a witness, and I'm not ashamed to say I told him he was an idiot. He was. But he was the most honest idiot in Larkspur Lake, and this town is better for having had him in it."

He turned and walked back to his seat. He did not look at Tessa as he passed her, but as he sat down, he reached over and squeezed her shoulder, hard, once.

Bev rose next, without being called. She set her black handbag on the chair beside her and walked to the front of the room, moving very slowly in her pale blue dress, and she stood beside Gus with her chin lifted and her straw hat tilted at a dangerous angle.

"Gerald Lund," she said, "I taught your daughter to read in 1989."

"Yes, Mrs. Halvorsen," Gerald said, in the tone of a man who had been reminded of this many times.

"And I'm going to tell you something I've never once told anybody on this Board," Bev said. "Len Marsh was the kindest man I ever knew, and the most annoying, and he ate baked beans for thirty years because he didn't want to be a burden to anybody. He drove his truck up and down Quarry Road every single morning for twenty years, past my house and past the marina, and he never once complained about the traffic. He just waved." She drew a breath. "The barn is not a store. It's a home. For a dog, and a cot, and a hayloft full of quilts, and a girl who was too stubborn to come back and too smart not to. And if this Board shuts it down, you'll be shutting down the only place in this county where somebody still thought to write down what everybody owed."

She turned and walked back to her seat. Her hands were shaking as she picked up her handbag. Tessa reached over and took one of them, and Bev squeezed hard, and did not let go until the Board had called for the next speaker.

Then Ethel Sorensen rose, and walked to the front of the room, and she read her statement from her typed notes in a voice that did not shake once.

"I was wrong about Len Marsh," she said. "I was wrong about his barn, and his lamps, and his Ledger, which I have never seen, and which I have been told is the most honest book in the county. I was wrong about his niece, whom I first saw in the Dock Street Diner on her first morning in town, and whom I accused of stealing her uncle's business in front of my own Garden Club, six weeks ago. I apologize to her, publicly, and I apologize to Mr. Reyes, who has been carrying Len's couches for two years without a word of thanks from me. And I ask the Board to approve this permit, not because the barn is a business, but because it is a light, and this township has had too few of those."

She folded her notes, very precisely, and walked back to her seat.

Owen stood up beside Tessa. He did not look at her. He stepped into the aisle, and walked to the front, and stood before the Board with his hands in his pockets and his face pale under its tan.

"I'm Owen Reyes," he said. "I'm the listing agent on the Birch Street sale, and I've carried most of the inventory in that barn out of this township in two years. I used to think that made me the guy who did the heavy lifting. Now I think it makes me the guy who didn't ask first." He took a breath. "I lent Len Marsh money that he couldn't pay back, against a business he never told his niece about, without ever telling her the terms. I kept the inventory in a unit on Route 9 under a tag that said do not sell without asking, and I never asked. I'm sorry. And I'm not asking this Board for anything except the chance to keep carrying the couch, under whatever rules you decide, with the inspector looking over my shoulder and the doors of that barn wide open."

He stopped. For a moment he looked as though he might say something more. Then he turned and walked back to his seat, and sat down beside Tessa, and put his hand over hers on the arm of the chair, and did not look at her at all.

Dr. Penwell, at the end of the Board's row, cleared his throat and asked a question, quietly, about the fire exits. Joyce answered it in two sentences. The room laughed, uneasily.

Then Gerald Lund looked over his glasses at the second row.

"Ms. Marsh," he said.

Tessa stood up. Her knees felt like water. She picked up the twelve-page plan in its clear plastic sleeve and walked to the front of the room, past the rows of folding chairs and the faces she had come to know over the summer, past Gus's bolo tie and Bev's straw hat and Ethel Sorensen's folded notes, until she stood at the lectern before the oak table and the five men and one woman who would decide the fate of her uncle's barn.

She set the plan on the lectern. She did not look at it.

"My uncle was a difficult man," she said.

A ripple of laughter went through the room. Gerald Lund's mouth twitched.

"He was a stubborn one," Tessa said. "He wouldn't sell the back field to Hank Dressler, and he wouldn't take the Milwaukee offer, and he wouldn't let anyone else touch the tags. He wrote everything down, in pencil, in capital letters, and he left most of it in a green notebook under a cot in a barn hayloft, where only the dog could reach it." She paused. "He was wrong about a lot of things. He was right about the light."

She took a breath.

"I've read his Ledger," she said. "Every page. And I'm not going to read it to you, because it's not mine to read in public. But I'm going to tell you what the first entry says, because it's the only sentence in there he wrote without changing it. *Marsh Home Staging. Opened in barn, Quarry Road. Tess is seventeen and wants to be a designer. Tell her no.*"

Someone in the back laughed, a warm, startled sound.

"He didn't tell me no," Tessa said. "He didn't tell me anything. He just kept the light on, and waited, and wrote it all down, and hoped I'd come back to see it." She looked up at the Board, and her voice steadied. "I'm here now. I've read the inventory, and I've counted the lamps twice, and I've signed the plan. I'm asking you to approve the permit under the conditions in that plan. No retail. No signs but one six-inch plaque. Two parking spaces. By appointment only, Monday through Friday, nine to four. A review in two years, and an inspector at any time, with the barn doors open. I'm asking you to let us keep the light on, under your rules, because I think my uncle would have wanted it that way, even if he would never have admitted it."

She stopped. The room was utterly silent.

Gerald Lund leaned forward over the oak table and took off his heavy glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose.

"The sign," he said.

"Yes," said Tessa.

"Joyce says there's a lamp on it."

"There is," Tessa said. "It's a small painted lamp, six inches across, with a green shade and the words *Marsh Home Staging* underneath. It's been on the gate since 2009, and it's in the barn hayloft tonight, waiting to go back up as a plaque. It's the only thing my uncle ever asked the township for, and it's the only thing he ever got in writing."

Gerald looked at her for a long moment, then at Joyce, then at Dr. Penwell, who had both hands folded neatly on the table and his eyes fixed on the plan in its clear plastic sleeve.

"Do you want to keep the lamp?" Gerald asked.

"Yes," said Tessa.

"Ask first?"

She smiled, very slightly.

"I've been asking first all summer, Mr. Lund," she said. "The answer is yes."

Gerald Lund put his glasses back on, and picked up his gavel, and looked down the row at his fellow board members, one by one.

"All in favor of approving the Quarry Road permit, under the conditions in the applicant's plan, with a review in twenty-four months?" he said.

Four hands went up. Then, after a long, uncomfortable pause, Dr. Penwell lifted his, very slowly, with the air of a man who had just discovered that he had been waiting twelve years for an excuse to vote for something he liked.

"Five," Gerald said. "The permit is approved. Miss Marsh, you can sit down."

Tessa did not sit down. She stood at the lectern, with her hands flat on the wood, and she felt the room move around her, the scrape of chairs, the rustle of coats, the sudden, rising murmur of a hundred voices, and then, from the back, one clear, rough, unmistakable shout.

"Hear, hear!"

It was Hank Dressler, on his feet, with his son's hand on his shoulder and his face wet and wide open, and his grin stretched so far across his sunburned face that Tessa thought he might never close his mouth again.

Bev Halvorsen started to clap.

Gus did not clap. He only stood up, very slowly, and put two fingers to his lips and let out a whistle so loud that Moose, under the row of chairs, leapt straight up with his tail going like a propeller and barked three times at the ceiling.

And Owen Reyes, in the second row, got to his feet, and walked the length of the aisle without a word, and stopped in front of the lectern, and took her face in both his hands.

"Ask me," he said, very quietly, under the noise of the room.

"Ask me what?"

"Whatever," Owen said. "I don't care. Ask me anything you want, and I'll say yes."

Tessa laughed, and cried, and put her hands over his, and did not let go.

"Bring me to see the fireworks," she said.

"Yes," said Owen.

"Are you going to carry the blanket?"

"I'm going to carry everything," he said, and kissed her, in front of the Larkspur Lake town board, the Garden Club, the Dock Street Diner, and one very small, very outraged dog, who had been shouted at by a whistle and was not going to be ignored a moment longer.

## Chapter 25: Fireworks Over the Pier

The fireworks at Lake Days had been launched off the end of the public pier for ninety-one years, and the township's fire chief, a stout, gray-haired man named Walt Ruddock who had been setting them off since the Eisenhower administration, took the job as seriously as a surgeon.

By nine o'clock the pier was packed. Families had spread blankets along the planks and the grassy slope beside the bait shop. Teenagers sat on the railings with their legs dangling over the water. Children ran up and down the boards with glow sticks, shrieking, and dogs of every size and temperament sniffed one another with polite, mutual suspicion. The lampposts had been turned off for the show, and the only light came from the paper lanterns strung above the crowd and the soft, golden glow from the windows of the Dock Street Diner, where Gus had propped the door open and was selling pie by the slice from a folding table on the sidewalk.

Tessa stood at the end of the pier with a blanket over one arm and her hand in Owen's, and she felt, for the first time since the last Tuesday in May, that she was exactly where she was supposed to be.

"You're quiet," Owen said.

"I'm thinking."

"About what?"

"About how Joyce Kessler looked when Hank Dressler shouted *hear, hear*," Tessa said. "As if she'd swallowed a lemon and found it was a lemon drop."

Owen laughed, low and easy, and his thumb moved slowly over her knuckles. "She's going to put that in the file."

"I think she's already written it down."

"She's going to make it the first line of the township's annual report," Owen said. "Under *Acts of Unexpected Civic Decency.*"

"That's not a real file either."

"It is now," Owen said, and grinned at her in the lantern light, and she realized, with a sudden warm jolt, that she was going to miss this, this ridiculous, easy, impossible man, when the summer ended and the leaves turned and the lake went cold.

She was not going to let that happen.

Moose was sitting on the blanket at their feet, with his nose lifted to the wind and his ears straight up, looking out over the water with the grave concentration of a dog who had been promised fireworks and intended to hold everybody to it. Lucy had gone home to Chicago that afternoon, on the late train, after a long, teary farewell at the depot in which she had made Owen promise to call her every Sunday and tell her exactly how many lamps were in the barn. Bev had sent a pie for the train. Gus had sent a second pie for Lucy's mother, who had never once in her life been to Wisconsin and who was, Lucy had said, going to be furious about it for the rest of her days.

"Tessa," Owen said.

"Yes?"

"Can I ask you something before the show starts?"

"You always ask me something before the show starts."

"I know," he said, and his voice was quiet, and very serious. "I'm not going to ask you the big thing tonight. I'm not going to ask you about the house, or the barn, or the forty cents, or the note, or the three hundred dollars a month that Nora says we're supposed to be splitting for the roof. I'm not going to ask you about Milwaukee or Chicago or the Blackwood people, or whether you're going to keep the dog."

"I'm keeping the dog."

"I know you're keeping the dog," Owen said. "Moose made that decision in May. I'm just telling you I know."

She laughed, softly, and leaned her shoulder against his. "Then what are you going to ask?"

"Whether you'd like to sit down," he said. "On the bench. At the end of the pier. Where the kids carved their names, and the township made them sand the boards, and my father used to drink beer with Len when nobody in this town was looking."

Tessa looked at him for a long moment. Then she smiled, and nodded, and let him lead her down the pier, with the blanket over her arm and the dog trotting at their heels, past the families and the glow sticks and the paper lanterns swaying in the warm, humid air.

The bench at the end of the pier was old and weathered, its planks gray and silver from a hundred summers of sun and rain. The carved names were still there, faint and uneven, a whole history of teenage longing and bad judgment in a dozen different hands. Tessa ran her thumb along one of them, a heart with two sets of initials, and felt the grooves catch on her skin.

"Somebody's going to get sanded for that," she said.

"Somebody's always going to get sanded for that," Owen said. He sat down beside her, close, and spread the blanket over both their laps. Moose climbed onto the bench between them, turned around twice, and flopped down with a contented groan, his head on Tessa's thigh.

For a long moment, they sat without speaking. The lake lay black and silver beyond the breakwater, flat and calm under the stars. A single sailboat had anchored out by the buoy, its running lights blinking slowly, and beyond it the far shore of the lake glowed with the lights of Sunset Point and the scattered windows of the cottages along Cedar Point Road. The air smelled of cut grass and pie and wood smoke from the bonfire at the far end of the town square.

"My father would have hated tonight," Owen said at last, very quietly.

"Why?"

"Because it was too crowded, and too cheerful, and there were too many people he used to owe money." He smiled faintly. "He'd have stood at the back of the diner with a cup of coffee, pretending he wasn't here, and then he'd have walked down to the bench and sat with Len, and they'd have argued about the ballgame for three hours."

"Did they argue?"

"Every night," Owen said. "Len said my father was the only man in the county who could lose an argument and still think he'd won. He said it was the most annoying thing about him, and the only reason he kept coming back."

"Your mother said Len was lonely."

"He was." Owen looked down at the planks. "I didn't see it until the funeral. I thought he was just grumpy and stubborn and wouldn't let anybody help him carry anything. And then I went out to the barn after everyone had gone home, and I found the cot, and I found the dog asleep on it, and the Ledger on the workbench, open to the page where he'd written *Owen's a good kid, and I haven't told him so.*" He swallowed. "And I sat on the floor for an hour and didn't move."

Tessa did not say anything. She reached out and found his hand on the blanket and held it.

"You never told me that," she said.

"You never asked."

"I'm asking now."

He turned his head and looked at her in the dim light. His face was tired, and open, and very young, the way it had been on the hayloft floor, and in the storm, and on the stairs at Ray Pruitt's office, when he had told her the number, and then waited for her to decide what it was worth.

"What do you want to know?" he said.

"Everything," Tessa said. "Every single thing you didn't tell me in June. I want to know what you thought when you saw the Ledger, and what you were going to say to me if I'd never come back. I want to know why you kept the lamp in the Dock Room for three years and called it yours. And I want to know what you want, Owen, not for the barn or the house or the note, but for you."

He was quiet for a long time.

Then he said, very softly, "I want you to stay."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got," Owen said. "I want you to stay here, in this town, in this barn, with this dog, and I want you to keep counting the lamps, and tell me when I'm wrong about the rug. I want you to stand at the back of the diner on a Sunday morning and argue with Gus about his pie crust. I want you to sit on the bench and watch the fireworks with me every August for the next fifty years, and I want you to be the one who says no when I try to carry the couch by myself." He swallowed hard. "And if you don't want that, if you want Milwaukee, or Chicago, or some other town I've never heard of, I'm going to be sad, and I'm going to carry the couch anyway, because that's the only way I know how to love anybody."

Tessa stared at him.

"You said love," she said.

"I did."

"You didn't say it on the pier."

"I didn't say it on the pier," Owen agreed, a little helplessly. "I said *not yet* was the right answer, and I was going to wait for you to say it first, because I didn't want to make you feel like you owed me anything. And then I sat on the floor of the Dock Room in the storm and held your hand, and I realized I'd been waiting for you to say it for six weeks, and that I was never going to stop."

Tessa's throat closed. She could not speak.

Over the lake, a single thin white line of light rose from the pier's far end, climbing slowly into the black sky, and then burst, with a soft, distant crack, into a cascade of gold and silver stars that spilled out across the water. The crowd on the pier cheered. The children shrieked. Moose lifted his head from her thigh and barked once, at the sky, in deep and grave disapproval.

"You're supposed to watch the fireworks," Owen said, smiling.

"I'm watching you," Tessa said.

"They're much better than me."

"They're not," Tessa said, and her voice broke on the words. "Ask me again."

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you were going to ask me," Tessa said, "the first night, on the porch, when you wanted to know whether I'd sign the contract, and I wouldn't tell you. Ask me now."

Owen was quiet. Then he said, very carefully, as if he were setting down a piece of glass that might break in his hands, "Tessa Marsh. Would you stay?"

The next firework burst over the water, red and gold and green, and the light spilled down over his face, and she could see every line of it: the scar above his eyebrow, the tired mouth, the eyes that had been watching her since the first morning in the drive with a look she had mistaken, for weeks, for amusement.

"Yes," she said.

He let out a breath that shook.

"Yes?" he said.

"Yes," Tessa said, and laughed through the tears, and leaned across the blanket and the sleeping dog and kissed him, hard, under a sky full of exploding gold. "Yes to the barn, and the house, and the lamps. Yes to the forty cents, if you insist on keeping it, and yes to the counting, and the Board, and Joyce Kessler, and every Sunday morning at the diner for the rest of my life. Yes to the bench. Yes to the dog. Yes to you, you impossible, carrying, couch-moving, lamp-stealing man."

"Is that a yes to the rug?" Owen asked, against her mouth.

"That's a yes to the rug," Tessa said. "But the blue one goes in the Dock Room. I'm not going to argue about it again."

"You said that about the lamp."

"I'm saying it about the rug," Tessa said, and kissed him again, and somewhere out on the water the fireworks went on, bursting and falling and bursting again, and the whole pier roared and cheered, and Bev Halvorsen's voice rang out from the grass, very loud and very clear, "Gus, she said yes!"

"I heard!" Gus shouted, from the diner, over the crowd. "I'm bringing out the good pie!"

Owen started to laugh, helplessly, with his forehead pressed to hers and his hands tangled in her hair, and Tessa could feel the sound of it all the way through her chest.

"We're going to be the talk of the township," he said.

"We were the talk of the township on the first morning," Tessa said. "The sofa was on the lawn, and the dog was sitting on it, and your face was doing that thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look like you know exactly what's going to happen," Tessa said, "and you're going to pretend you don't, just so I'll be the one to say it first."

He pulled back just far enough to look at her. The light from the fireworks was on his face, gold and red and green, and there were tears on his cheeks too, and he did not wipe them away.

"You said it first," he said.

"I know," Tessa said, and laughed, and put her hand against his cheek. "That's because you're a terrible salesman, and I'm the best closer Brent Malloy ever trained. Now shut up and watch the sky, Owen Reyes, before Moose decides we're not worth the trouble and goes home to the hayloft without us."

Moose lifted his head from her thigh, looked up at the two of them with an expression of profound and weary patience, and then, very deliberately, put his head back down.

He had already decided.

They sat on the bench at the end of the pier, under the bursting sky, with the blanket over their laps and the carved names beneath their feet and the whole of Larkspur Lake glittering silver and gold around them. And for the first time in the whole long summer, Tessa did not count anything at all.

## Chapter 26: Closing Day

The closing for the Birch Street cottage was set for ten o'clock on Friday, August 22, at the offices of a title company in Racine, and Nora Pruitt arrived at the conference room at nine forty-five with a leather briefcase, a rolling suitcase, a legal pad, and a small paper bag from the Dock Street Diner that smelled, faintly and irresistibly, of blueberries.

"Gus sent it," she said, setting the bag on the table. "He says it's a peace offering for the hour I'm going to make you wait while the title agent finds her glasses. He says she's been losing them since 1994."

"He's right," said Tessa, who had been sitting at the end of the table since nine o'clock with a cup of coffee going cold between her hands.

"He's always right about the people in this county," Nora said, and sat down across from her, and looked at her for a long moment with the frank, assessing expression that she had worn on the Birch Street lawn in May. "You look different."

"I'm sunburned."

"That's not what I mean."

"I know," Tessa said, and smiled. "I've been sleeping."

Nora's mouth curved, very slightly. "Good. Ada never slept in that house, you know. Not once in forty years. She said the lake was too loud at night, and she'd lie awake listening to the water hit the pier and thinking about who she'd lost to it." She looked down at her legal pad. "I'm glad somebody finally slept there."

Owen came through the door at nine fifty-five. He had a manila envelope under one arm and his good shirt on, the blue one Bev had ironed for him on Thursday night while Tessa stood in the kitchen doorway pretending she was not watching. His hair was combed. His boots had been polished. He looked, Tessa thought, exactly like a man who had been up since five with his heart in his throat, and had decided, somewhere around the second cup of coffee, that the only thing he could do about it was to walk into the room and sit down.

"Morning," he said.

"Morning," Nora said, without looking up. "You're late."

"I'm four minutes early."

"You're four minutes late. The title agent is already in the hall, and she's wearing both pairs of glasses at once, which means she's going to tell us the deed is missing." Nora pushed a yellow pad across the table. "Sit down, Mr. Reyes. You're making Ms. Marsh nervous."

"I'm not nervous," Tessa said.

"You're holding your coffee like a grenade," Nora said. "Sit down and drink it."

Owen sat down beside her, and his knee bumped hers under the table, and she did not move it away.

The title agent came in at ten fifteen, with both pairs of glasses on her nose, a sheaf of documents in one hand, and an expression of profound and personal grievance at the state of the Racine county records office. She laid out the closing papers in front of them with the practiced speed of a woman who had done this four thousand times, and she read every line aloud, slowly, in a flat, clear voice, while Nora signed and initialed and wrote the date in a neat, slanting hand that did not waver once.

The buyers signed at ten forty. Priscilla Fenner came through the door first, in a cream linen dress and white sandals, with a pen in one hand and a box of Gus's cherry turnovers under her arm, and she set the box on the table and hugged Tessa before Tessa could stand up.

"Oh, you did it," Priscilla said, into her shoulder. "You did it, you wonderful woman. We've been talking about the Dock Room since June. Warren has a list of seven different ways to fix the shade."

"Nobody's fixing the shade," Tessa said, laughing. "It's fine. It's in the barn, wrapped in tissue, with a note on it."

"What kind of note?"

"It says *Owner's Instructions*," Owen said, from the end of the table. "And it's in capitals."

"Oh, I love that," Priscilla said, and sat down beside Nora, and reached for the pen.

Warren came in behind her, in a crisp blue shirt, carrying a manila folder of his own. He shook Owen's hand, and then Tessa's, and then, after a moment's hesitation, leaned down and kissed Nora's cheek, which made the retired ophthalmologist from Phoenix turn very pink and say, "Warren Fenner, you'll be sorry you did that in front of the title agent."

"I'm already sorry," Warren said cheerfully. "Pris made me do it."

The papers went around the table, one by one. The purchase price was $589,000, with the furniture included and the brass lamp excluded. The closet lamp had been purchased separately, by Priscilla, for one dollar, paid in cash, with a candy bar that she had delivered to Tessa personally, along with a handwritten receipt that said *One (1) closet lamp, crooked cord, sold as is, to the most charming woman in Wisconsin.* The agent read the receipt twice, and then looked at Tessa over her glasses and said, "I'm not sure this is a legally binding document," and Priscilla said, "It's on a placemat, Margaret, it's binding in the state of Wisconsin," and the entire conference room broke into laughter.

The check came from the title company at eleven. It was made out to the Pruitt estate, and the agent placed it in front of Nora, who looked at it for a long moment without picking it up.

"Ada would have been furious," Nora said quietly.

"Why?" Tessa asked.

"Because it's too much money for a house that was never going to be anything but a summer place," Nora said, "and she'd have wanted it to go to a charity for lighthouse repairs. She hated the thought that anyone would make a profit off the view."

"The view isn't for sale," Owen said.

Nora looked at him. "Then what is it for?"

"It's for keeping the light on," Owen said, "for the people who come up the drive. My uncle..." He stopped, looked at Tessa, and corrected himself. "Len used to say that."

"He said it to me, too," Nora said. She picked up the check, folded it in half without looking at it, and tucked it into the inside pocket of her navy linen jacket. "I'll give half to the lighthouse fund in Door County, and I'll give the other half to the Larkspur Lake Public Library, which Bev Halvorsen has been threatening to shut down for three years, because she says the reading room smells like a wet dog. And then I'm going to put the rest in a shoebox and not touch it until I'm ninety."

"That's the most sensible thing I've ever heard you say," Tessa said.

"I'm a sensible woman," Nora said. "Ada was the crazy one. She used to sleep with the windows open in the middle of a snowstorm." She looked at Tessa across the table, and for the first time, her face softened into something that was almost affection. "And I want you to know, Ms. Marsh, that I'm glad it was you. Not because you're the best stager in the county. Because you counted the lamps, and you told me the truth about the roof, and you didn't once let your partner carry the whole thing by himself."

Owen went very still beside her.

"Partner," he said quietly.

Tessa looked down at the table.

"I'll get to that," she said.

The loan was closed at eleven twenty.

It had taken two weeks to draft, three phone calls to a lawyer in Milwaukee, and a long argument at the kitchen table on Birch Street that Bev had refereed from the stove. The placemat had come up in the argument more than once. It had lived in the breast pocket of Owen's button-down since the first Monday in June, through the storm and the stairs and the hearing and the pier, and on the Tuesday before the closing he took it out at the kitchen table, unfolded it, and smoothed it flat with both palms.

The paper was the same as it had always been: a little soft at the corners, with a faint ring of coffee on one side and a smear of Bev's blueberry filling on the other. The terms were still there in blue ink, in Tessa's neat, slanted hand. At the bottom was Owen's square print, *Who pays for the paint?*, and under that, in pencil, a line Tessa did not remember writing.

*BLUE RUG: TESSA. LAMP: OWEN, TO BE DECIDED FRIDAY.*

"Gus framed the copy," Owen had said quietly. "The original's been in my pocket all summer. I think it belongs on the table where it started."

"Len would have liked that," Tessa had said.

"Len would have said the ink was the only thing in Larkspur Lake that never lied," Owen said, and smiled at the paper, and did not look up.

Now the placemat lay in the middle of the conference table in Racine, between the closing papers and the check, its blue ink bright under the fluorescent light.

Owen took the manila envelope from under his arm and opened it.

Inside was a single sheet of typed paper, three pages long, with two signatures at the bottom and a notary's seal in the corner. It was headed *Marsh Home Staging, Quarry Road, Larkspur Lake, Wisconsin*. And under that, in plain black type, the terms.

*Forty cents of every net dollar of Marsh Home Staging, as set forth in the Ledger of Leonard Marsh, March 15, 2023, shall be paid to Owen Reyes for so long as he keeps the books and carries the couch. The loan of $30,000 is hereby satisfied in full. Neither party shall sell the Quarry Road barn or the Route 9 inventory without first asking the other. Any disagreement shall be settled at the kitchen table on Birch Street, or at Gus's, whichever is closer, and the decision shall be final.*

Tessa read it twice. Then she looked up at Owen.

"You wrote this," she said.

"I wrote the first draft," Owen said, very quietly. "On the back of a menu. Gus said I wasn't allowed to write on the menu again, so I used the printer at the library, and Bev read it over my shoulder, and said the last line was too sentimental, and I said that was the point."

"Did she change it?"

"No," said Owen. "She said it was perfect. And then she cried into her muffin."

Tessa laughed, and it came out wet, and she wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist and picked up the pen.

"Ask me first," she said.

"Tessa."

"Ask me," she said, and held the pen out across the table, and her hand was steady.

Owen looked at her for a long time. Then he reached out and took the pen, slowly, and bent his head, and signed his name beside the line marked *Partner*, in square, careful print that did not waver once.

"Your turn," he said.

Tessa signed. She signed beside the same word, in her neat, slanted hand, and the word looked strange and bright and very real on the page, like a lamp set in the right window at the right hour of the afternoon.

Nora Pruitt, at the far end of the table, took off her reading glasses and polished them very slowly with a folded tissue.

"Oh," she said, to nobody in particular. "I suppose that's binding in the state of Wisconsin."

"It is," said Owen.

"Good," Nora said. "Now somebody tell me what happened to the lamp."

Tessa looked across the table at Priscilla Fenner, who had been sitting very quietly with her hands folded in her lap and an expression of enormous, unbearable restraint.

"It stays in the Dock Room," Tessa said. "It goes with the house. Pay the asking price, Priscilla, and the lamp stays in the window, and the light comes off the water at four, and you can have Owen and me come over every Thursday to make sure the shade isn't crooked."

Priscilla's face went through several emotions in quick succession.

"You're giving us the lamp?" she whispered.

"We're giving you the lamp," Tessa said. "On one condition."

"Whatever."

"You tell every grandchild who visits that it belonged to a man named Len who bought his lamps for a quarter," Tessa said, "and that the light always tells the truth."

Priscilla burst into tears. Warren handed her a handkerchief without a word, and she blew her nose, very loudly, and said, "Oh, damn it, Warren, I'm going to need a bigger check."

"You can have the lamp, Pris," Warren said gently. "We'll get the bigger check."

"And the closet lamp?" Nora asked dryly.

"Still a dollar," Priscilla said through her tears. "And a candy bar. I'm not made of money."

The conference room filled with laughter. The title agent, who had been standing by the door with her two pairs of glasses in one hand, looked at the placemat on the table, then at the signatures, then at the check folded in Nora Pruitt's jacket pocket, and finally at Tessa, and said, in a voice of profound and resigned professional dignity, "I'm going to need a copy of this for the file."

"Of course," Owen said. "It's binding in the state of Wisconsin."

"That isn't how it works," said the title agent.

"It is in Larkspur Lake," said Tessa, and reached across the table, and took Owen's hand.

## Chapter 27: The Goldenrod Field

On Labor Day morning, the back field at Quarry Road was the color of the sun.

The goldenrod had come up in waves all summer, and by the first week of September it stood waist-high along the slope from the barn to the water, bright and shaggy and humming with bees. The late light caught it and turned it to molten gold. Beyond it the lake lay flat and blue, with a scatter of white sails riding at anchor off the marina and a single gull wheeling over the breakwater, crying out to nobody in particular.

Hank Dressler had cut a path through the middle of the field with a push mower that morning, a narrow gravel-edged lane that ran from the gate at the top of the rise down to the dock at the water's edge, and he had stood at the top of it for twenty minutes, with his hands in his pockets, waiting for Tessa's truck to come up Quarry Road.

"You're late," he said, when she climbed out, with Moose tumbling after her and Owen's pickup pulling in behind.

"We're on time," Owen said.

"You're on Hank's time," Hank said, "which is a different thing entirely." He looked past them, toward the barn, and his face softened. "Eli's in the boat already. He wanted to see the dog."

Eli Dressler was standing on the end of the dock, in a faded green T-shirt and a pair of cutoff jeans, with a wrench in one hand and a boat cushion under one arm. He had his father's jaw and his mother's careful way of looking at a thing before he moved toward it, and when he saw Moose come bounding down the lane through the goldenrod, barking with delight and sending a cloud of bees into the air, he dropped the cushion and went down on one knee on the warm planks and held out both his hands.

"Hey, buddy," he said softly. "Hey. Who's a good dog?"

Moose reached the dock at full speed, skidded on the boards, and threw himself into Eli's arms with a happy, grunting howl. Eli laughed and went over backward onto the planks, and the dog climbed onto his chest, licking his face with enormous enthusiasm, and Eli lay there with his arms around the golden retriever and his eyes shut tight, laughing and blinking and not quite able to stop.

"He's a hayloft dog," Owen said, from the bank. "He doesn't like boats."

"He's a boat dog," Eli said, from the planks, wiping his eyes. "He just doesn't know it yet."

"Everybody says that," Tessa said.

"Because it's true," Eli said, and sat up, with the dog in his lap and his hair full of goldenrod seeds. "Dad says Len used to bring him down here every Saturday for coffee and a bag of bait. He says the dog sat on this dock like it was his, and Len sat on that bench by the pump and told everybody who came down the lane that the dog was the best judge of character in the county."

"Was he?" Tessa asked.

Eli looked up at her, and his face was open and serious.

"He never once barked at my father," he said. "Not in twenty years. And he barked at every single person who came down this lane to tell Dad what he was doing wrong." He scratched the dog's ears. "So I guess he was right."

Hank came down the lane behind them, carrying a cooler in one hand and a stack of paper cups in the other, and he set the cooler on the dock and popped the lid. Inside was a jug of lemonade, a plate of Bev's sugar cookies, and a bottle of something amber with a cork in the top that Hank said was from a distillery in Door County, and that Gus had sworn was not for drinking on the job.

"It's for toasting," Hank said gruffly. "Len would have wanted it."

"Len would have wanted it on the house," Owen said.

"Len would have wanted it on the lamp," Hank said, and poured four small cups, and handed them out with a stiff, ceremonial air. "To the back field," he said. "And to the barn. And to the old fool who wouldn't sell me either one."

They lifted the cups. The sun was warm on their faces. The goldenrod hummed. Eli raised his cup with one hand and kept the other buried in the dog's fur.

"To Len," Tessa said.

"To Len," Owen said.

"To the light," said Eli, quietly, and drank.

Hank did not say anything. He only nodded, once, and looked down at the cup in his hand, and drank it in one long swallow, and when he lifted his head again his eyes were wet and his jaw was set, and he looked, for the first time since Tessa had met him, exactly like a man who had come home.

They spent the afternoon on the dock. Eli showed Owen how to tune a two-stroke outboard, which Owen pretended to understand and failed, very cheerfully, to do. Hank sat on the bench by the gas pump, the same bench Len had sat on every Saturday morning for twenty years, and watched his son work, with a face that kept changing as if he were trying to hold it still and could not. Moose slept in a patch of sun at the end of the dock, with his head on Eli's boat cushion and one paw twitching in dreams.

Tessa sat on the warm planks with her feet in the water and her back against a piling, and she did not count anything at all.

At four o'clock, her phone rang. It was her mother.

"Tessa," Diane Marsh said, in the voice she used when she had been crying and had decided, very firmly, to stop. "I'm in Milwaukee. I'm at the airport, in the parking lot. I've been trying to decide whether to drive up to the lake or get on the next plane home, and I don't know how to get to Larkspur Lake from here, and your cousin Connie says I should just ask the realtor."

Tessa laughed, and felt her eyes sting.

"Ask the realtor, Mom," she said. "He'll meet you at the depot. He'll carry your suitcase."

"Is he going to carry it?"

"He's going to carry everything," Tessa said. "Bring a sweater. The lake gets cold after dark, even in September, and Bev Halvorsen is going to feed you until you can't stand up."

"I'm not sure I want to meet the woman who wrote the Birch Street lamp into the township file," her mother said, but her voice was warm, and wet, and a little bit amused.

"You're going to love her," Tessa said. "Everybody does, eventually. Even Joyce Kessler. Even Hank Dressler, who came down his own lane this afternoon to tell me the goldenrod's going to be the best it's been in twenty years, and that Len would've been insufferable about it."

There was a long, shaky silence on the line.

"He would have," her mother said, very quietly. "He was insufferable about everything. He was insufferable at Christmas, and insufferable at your father's funeral, and insufferable on the phone every single Sunday for twenty-six years. And I miss him so much, Tess, I can't breathe some days."

"I know, Mom."

"You never came home for the holidays after you started at Northline."

"I know."

"Are you coming home for the holidays now?"

Tessa looked out across the goldenrod, toward the barn on the rise with its red doors open and the hayloft window glowing in the late light. She looked at the dock, where her uncle's dog was sleeping in the sun, and at Eli Dressler, laughing as Owen dropped a socket wrench into the lake and swore at it in a language Tessa did not recognize, and at Hank, on the bench, with the empty paper cup in his hand and his face turned toward the water.

"I'm not coming home for the holidays, Mom," she said.

Her mother drew a sharp breath.

"I'm coming for the November holiday," Tessa said, "and I'm bringing you a lamp. A small one, brass, with a green shade, from a storage unit on Route 9. And I'm going to sit you down in the Dock Room, Mom, at four in the afternoon, when the light comes off the water. And then you're going to tell me whether I was right about the blue quilt."

Her mother was silent for a moment. Then she laughed, a sudden, startled, delighted sound, the same laugh Tessa remembered from the car on the way to Larkspur Lake when she was nine years old and her uncle had leaned out of his truck window to say something that made her mother forget to be angry.

"Oh, Tessa," she said. "He'd have loved that."

"I know," Tessa said. "He wrote it down."

She hung up. She sat very still on the dock for a long moment, with the phone in her hand and the lake lapping softly at her bare feet and the goldenrod humming on the slope above her.

Then Owen came down the planks, barefoot and sunburned, with a cut across one thumb and a grin on his face that stretched from one ear to the other, and he sat down beside her and put his arm around her shoulders and kissed the top of her head.

"Your mother," he said.

"She's coming for the November holiday."

"And?"

"And I'm going to make her sit in the Dock Room with a brass lamp," Tessa said, "and tell me the truth about the rug."

"She's going to say it's gray."

"She's going to say it's blue," Tessa said, "because she was raised in a house where my uncle made her agree to everything, and she never once in her life admitted he was right."

Owen laughed, and pulled her closer, and the dog lifted his head from the cushion, looked at the two of them with the weary patience of a creature who had been waiting all his life for this exact moment, and then, very deliberately, climbed down off the boat, walked the length of the dock, and lay down across both their legs.

"He's decided," Owen said.

"He decided in May," Tessa said.

"He decided in the hayloft," said Eli Dressler, from the boat, wiping his hands on a rag. "Dad says he's been waiting nine years for someone to take him down to the water and tell him he was a good dog. Tell him, Owen. Tell him he's a good dog."

"He's a good dog," Owen said, and scratched the golden head with one hand, and Moose sighed with immense satisfaction and closed his eyes.

On the bench by the gas pump, Hank Dressler lifted his empty cup, very slowly, toward the lake, the barn, and the goldenrod field beyond it, as though he were toasting somebody who had just walked away down the lane and would be back before the frost.

Nobody said anything. There was nothing to say.

The light came off the water and lay across the dock in long, warm bars of gold, and the goldenrod hummed, and the sails rode at anchor in the bay, and at the top of the rise the red barn stood with its doors wide open to the afternoon, the way it had always stood, waiting for somebody honest enough to come up the lane and ask.

## Chapter 28: Sumac Court

The first frost came to Larkspur Lake on the second Tuesday in October, and it came the way Len Marsh had always said it would: all at once, in the night, with a white hush that settled over the cedars and the pier and the tin roof of the barn before anyone was awake to see it.

Tessa woke at six, in the bed in the Dock Room, under the blue quilt that had been dry-cleaned in Racine twice and had come back, at last, smelling of nothing but cedar and the lake. The window was gray with light. Beyond the glass, the cedars were rimed with frost, and the water was flat and steaming in the cold, and a pair of mallards were paddling in the shallows near the pier as if nothing in the world had changed since May.

Owen was still asleep beside her, on his back, with one arm flung across his eyes and his mouth slightly open, looking younger than she had ever seen him. Moose was on the foot of the bed, curled in a heavy golden knot, snoring softly. The brass lamp stood in the corner by the window, dark, with its green shade catching the first gray light off the water.

Tessa got up quietly, pulled on a sweater, and went downstairs to make the coffee.

It was a ritual now. She had learned it in August, on the first morning after the hearing, when she had come down to the kitchen at Birch Street and found Owen already at the stove with two cups out and a sheet of graph paper spread on the table. He had not asked her anything. He had only smiled and slid one of the cups across to her, and she had understood, without either of them saying it, that the coffee was going to be made by whoever got up first, and that the other one was going to be waiting with the sugar.

She filled the pot. She set out the cups. She stood at the sink, in the cold kitchen with the window full of frost and the radio murmuring low on the counter, and she looked out at the lake and the pier and the bait shop with its sign still bolted above the door, and she felt the old, familiar tightness in her chest, the sense of a plan slipping sideways, and then she felt it let go.

The mail came at eight. It came, as it always did, in a worn gray truck that stopped at the end of the Birch Street drive, and the carrier, a cheerful woman named Shirley who had delivered to the lake since the nineties, waved at Tessa from the window and held up a stack of envelopes with the air of someone delivering a thousand bills.

"Something for you," Shirley called across the frost-white lawn. "Marsh Home Staging. It's on the top. It's got a Sumac Court return address, and the handwriting looks like a woman who means business."

Tessa took the stack from her, with thanks, and sorted through it on the porch steps while the sun came up over the lake. Most of it was the usual: a bill for the roof from Pete Ostrowski in Racine, a flyer for the Garden Club's fall preserve sale, a card from Lucy with a picture of a Chicago lake freezing over, and a letter from Brent Malloy's office in Milwaukee, on heavy cream stationery, confirming the Blackwood referral agreement and enclosing a check for the first north-shore house, which was, Brent had written in a handwritten note at the bottom, *a stupid amount of money for a view, Tess, and I hope you know what you're doing.*

The last envelope was heavy and cream-colored, addressed in a round, careful hand that was not her uncle's and not Bev's and not anyone she had ever seen before.

*MARSH HOME STAGING, QUARRY ROAD BARN, LARKSPUR LAKE, WI. ATTN: MS. TESSA MARSH, OR WHOEVER WANTS THE JOB.*

She opened it on the steps, with the frost melting off the railing in the sun and a cup of coffee going cold in her other hand.

*Dear Ms. Marsh,*

*My name is June Abernathy. I am seventy-three years old, and I have lived at 9 Sumac Court, on the north shore of Larkspur Lake, since 1979, when my husband, Gordon, and I bought the house from a retired schoolteacher named Harriet Vogel, who had kept it perfectly for thirty years and would not let us paint a single room.*

*Gordon died in March. I am writing because he left me a house full of things I do not understand, and a note that says I am to call a man named Leonard Marsh, who is dead, and ask his niece to come up the lane and tell me what to do with it.*

*I was not going to write. I am too old and too stubborn, and I have been told by three different people in this town that my house looks like a crime scene. But on Sunday I went to the Dock Street Diner for the first time since Gordon's funeral, and I sat in the booth by the window, and a woman named Bev Halvorsen came over to my table with a blueberry pie and said, "You're June. Len Marsh wrote your name in his book in 2019 and said you'd be the next one to call him. He never got the chance. You'll have to call the niece instead." And then she set down the pie and walked away before I could say a single word.*

*So I am calling the niece. Or writing to her, which I am told is the proper thing to do in Larkspur Lake, where nobody answers the telephone before nine in the morning and everybody reads the newspaper with their coffee.*

*There is a cot in the attic of 9 Sumac Court. It is iron, painted white, with a gray wool blanket folded at the foot. Gordon said Len Marsh had sold it to him in 1984, at a yard sale in Racine, for a quarter, and told him it was the best quarter he would ever spend. There is also a tin lunch box, a pair of reading glasses, and a box of brass tags in a closet under the eaves, each one labeled in pencil, and every one of them says DO NOT SELL WITHOUT ASKING, and then a set of initials I do not recognize, and a room. The room is always the Dock Room.*

*I would like you to come up the lane, Ms. Marsh, and tell me what it all means. I would like you to bring the dog, if he is still alive. And I would like you to tell me whether it is true, as Bev Halvorsen says, that the light always tells the truth, because I have spent the last six months in a house full of Gordon's things, and I have not once seen it.*

*With respect, and some hope,*

*June Abernathy*

*P.S. There is a second green notebook in the box under the cot. I did not open it. Gordon said it was yours to read first.*

Tessa put the letter down on her knee.

She sat for a long time on the porch steps, with the sun full on her face now and the frost gone off the lawn, and the lake shining silver and blue beyond the cedars. The mallards had moved out toward the middle of the bay. Somewhere up the street, a screen door banged, and Bev Halvorsen's voice rose over the hedge, calling a cat named Admiral in for breakfast, and then, after a pause, a second voice answered, low and laughing, from the kitchen window across the street, which was Owen's voice, and which was saying, with a theatrical sigh, "Bev, you're not allowed to feed him a whole pie."

Tessa laughed out loud. The sound startled a pair of sparrows off the railing.

She heard the front door open behind her. She heard the soft pad of bare feet on the porch boards, and then the warm weight of a hand on her shoulder, and a kiss against her hair, and Owen's voice, sleepy and amused and entirely unrepentant.

"You're up early," he said.

"I made the coffee."

"I know. I could smell it from the bed." He sat down on the step beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and looked at her face. "What's that?"

"A letter," Tessa said, and held it out to him.

He read it slowly, with his lips moving slightly on the words the way they did when he was concentrating. When he reached the postscript, he stopped, and read it again, and then he set the paper down on his knee very carefully, as if it might break.

"Sumac Court," he said.

"Sumac Court."

"That's the north shore. Past the Ostrander place. There's nothing up there but cottages and a couple of old families who don't talk to anybody." He looked up at her, and his face was alight with something that was not quite surprise, and not quite fear, and was very nearly joy. "June Abernathy. I know her. She used to bring her husband's shirts to the dry cleaner in Racine every Thursday, and she never once let anybody carry them for her. Len used to say she was the most stubborn woman in the county."

"Bev says he wrote her name in his book in 2019."

"Of course he did," Owen said, and laughed, softly. "He wrote everybody's name in that book. He just never got around to reading it to anybody."

Tessa looked down at the letter in his hands. She thought about the cot in the attic, and the box of brass tags, and the second green notebook under it, which was hers to read first, as a dead man's friend had put it who had never once been able to say what he meant.

"Owen," she said.

"Yes?"

"Ask me."

He looked at her for a long moment, in the sunlight, with the frost melting off the rail and the dog snoring softly inside the open door, and then he smiled, slowly, the way he had on the first morning in the drive, when he had held out his hand and asked her for a truce.

"Do you want to go up the lane?" he asked.

"Yes," said Tessa. "But not until you've finished your coffee. And not until Moose has been properly introduced to the cot, because I'm not going to be the one who tells him he has to sleep somewhere else."

"He's going to want to bring the cot home."

"He's going to try," Tessa said. "And we're going to let him."

She stood up, and held out her hand, and Owen took it, and they went into the kitchen together, where the coffee was waiting, and the sugar, and the dog, who had heard the word *cot* from the foot of the stairs and was already coming down them, one heavy, golden step at a time, with the air of an animal who had been waiting his entire life for someone to ask him the right question.

Up the lane, past the Ostrander dock and the cedars and the stone wall at the top of Sumac Court, a house with a cot in its attic and a box of tags under its eaves had been waiting since March for somebody honest enough to come up the drive and count the lamps.

Tessa Marsh had already started the list.
