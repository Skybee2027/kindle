## Chapter 1: Last Words for Minutes

Every word spoken in Harbor Center on the evening of July 14 went onto paper tape, and Greta Lowe intended to keep it that way. In thirty-one years as a certified shorthand reporter, first in the Carrow County courthouse and later as a freelancer who could be trusted with anything from a probate hearing to a school board fight, she had never let a word go missing. She did not intend to start in a room with a broken ceiling fan and a coffee urn that gurgled like a drain.

She set up at the end of the second table, the way she always did. Her machine sat on its folding tripod, and the tape fed out of the front of it into a plastic basket at her feet. She had a fresh spool, a spare pen, two sharpened pencils she would never use, and a bottle of water that she would forget to drink. The basket was the only part of the setup that had ever made her feel foolish, but it kept the tape from snaking across the floor, and Greta had long ago decided that small dignities were worth the trouble.

Judge had not come with her. The gray cat had considered the matter at breakfast and decided that a council meeting was a poor use of a sunny afternoon, and he had spent the evening asleep on the bench of her steno notes in the spare room, which was where he preferred to be whenever she was working. She had named him for his judicial habits, and he had never once disappointed her.

At six fifty-two, the room was filling. Ruth Kalinowski, the council chair, was sorting papers at the head of the long table under the tall windows. Through the glass behind her, the bay had turned the flat silver it took on in the evenings of a hot July, and the masts in the marina below Front Street stood very still against it. Ed Matlin and Carla Sayer sat together on the left side of the dais, talking in low voices and laughing at something on Carla's phone. Lorraine Haskins sat at the far end of the dais with a ball of blue yarn in her lap and her needles going, as she had done at every meeting since the nineties. Ted Vance, who had not yet decided how he would vote on anything since he was elected, was rearranging his pens for the third time.

Dale Whitcomb took the chair nearest the aisle without speaking to anyone. He was seventy-one years old, lean in the way of men who had spent their lives hauling coils of rope and outboard motors, and he wore the same navy windbreaker he had worn to every meeting for as long as Greta had been covering them. He owned Whitcomb Marine Supply on Front Street. He had been a harbor commissioner for nine years. He had buried a wife, raised a son who now lived in Chicago and rarely called, and he had a small brown pill bottle that rode in the breast pocket of that windbreaker. Greta had seen him take one of the pills once, quickly, with his back to the room, and had decided not to notice.

Priya Rao came in at seven minutes past seven and took a seat in the gallery, midway back on the left. She owned Page and Thistle Bookshop across the street and had a paperback folded lengthwise in one hand, the way she always carried a book to a meeting, as if it were a shield. She caught Greta's eye and lifted one eyebrow about half an inch, which was the whole of their conversation until the meeting was over.

Walt Brandt took the last seat in the back row, against the wall, under the portrait of the harbor's first commissioner, who looked as though he had regretted the whole project. Walt was seventy-seven and had fished the bay out of Lantern Bay for fifty years. He had a paper cup of coffee from the Gull Café balanced on his knee and the stillness of a man who expected to wait a long time for a fish.

Two rows in front of Walt sat Helen Marsh, the town clerk, with her laptop open and a hand pressed flat against the top of it, as though it might try to leave. Helen had been clerk for eleven years and had never once looked this tired. Beside her, with a black laptop bag at her feet and a pair of earbuds looped over one shoulder, sat Bernice Oakes.

Greta had known Bernice for nearly twenty years, which was long enough to know that the two of them had never been friends. Bernice had come up through the same courthouse as Greta a decade before Greta retired, and she had been the one who got the county's appellate transcript contract the year Greta lost it, which Greta had decided, after some reflection, was nobody's fault but the county's. Bernice had since built Oakes Transcription into a respectable shop in Fennick Falls, forty minutes south, with a staff of five and a list of clients that included two law firms and the Lantern Bay town government. She wore three silver bangles on her left wrist, and they chimed faintly whenever she moved. Tonight they chimed as she took off her cardigan and folded it over the chair back.

"You're early," Helen said to her, not quite a question.

"I always am," Bernice said. "The record keeper gets the best seat in the house."

Greta filed the remark away, though she could not have said why. Then she looked toward the front row, where Gordon Strand was taking off a tan linen jacket and laying it across his knees.

Gordon was fifty-two and wore his success with a casualness that had cost him real money to acquire. He was the managing partner of Strand Lakeside Partners, and he had come to Lantern Bay two years earlier with a plan to build sixty condominiums and a public boardwalk on the north basin of the harbor. The plan had been called Harbor Point in every press release, and the press releases had been very good. Beside him, notebook open on her knee and a manila folder balanced on top of it, sat his assistant, Tessa Quill. She was twenty-nine, pale, and had the wary posture of someone who had been told to be polite to people she did not trust.

At seven-oh-four, Ruth Kalinowski tapped her gavel once, and the room settled.

"We'll call the regular meeting of the Lantern Bay Town Council to order," Ruth said. "Clerk, roll call."

Helen read the names. Greta typed them onto the tape in the shorthand that had become second nature years ago, the strokes coming in clusters like tiny keys on a piano, and in the margin of her mind a quieter voice counted the minutes. She had pressed the time-mark key at seven o'clock, and she would press it every five minutes after, so that when she sat down to read the tape later, she would know where the room had been at any hour of the evening. It was the first habit a court reporter learned and the last one she would ever abandon.

The first hour was ordinary. The council approved the minutes from June. It approved a contract for new sidewalk benches along the boardwalk, and Carla Sayer made a speech about the benches that ran long enough for Ed Matlin to drop a pen and then pick it up again. It discussed a grant for the sewer line on Cutter Street, and Ted Vance asked three questions in a row, each one more confused than the last, and Ruth patiently answered each one as though it had been the first. Greta's fingers moved steadily through it. She had no trouble with a room that wandered. A room that wandered gave the reporter time to breathe.

At seven-fifty-eight, Ruth called item seven. The harbor lease.

"The council has before it a request from Strand Lakeside Partners for a forty-year lease of the north basin," Ruth read, her reading glasses pushed up into her silver hair. "The lease would allow the construction of the Harbor Point development, with sixty residential units and a public boardwalk. The lease has been reviewed by the town attorney and the harbor commission. The commission has issued a split recommendation. We will hear from the public, and then the council will set a date for a final vote."

Gordon Strand rose and walked to the lectern before the clerk had finished. He spoke for six minutes in a warm, unhurried voice, the voice of a man who had sold a great many things across a great many tables. He talked about the marina's aging seawall, the falling number of slips, the young families who could not afford to live in Lantern Bay, and the boardwalk that would be open to every resident and every visitor. He smiled at Walt Brandt, who did not smile back. He mentioned the survey, which had been completed by a licensed firm in the first days of July, and he said that the survey confirmed everything his company had told the town from the beginning.

Greta typed it all. At the end of his speech, Gordon folded his hands on the lectern and looked at the council with an expression of deep and friendly confidence.

Then Dale Whitcomb stood up.

He did not walk to the lectern. He stood in the aisle beside his chair, a hand resting on the back of it, and he spoke loudly enough that the people in the back row did not have to lean forward.

"I'm going to say my piece," Dale said, "and then Ruth can tell me I've had my turn."

"You've had your turn, Dale," Ruth said kindly. "Go ahead."

"This town gave its harbor to the families who built it," Dale said. "My father hauled bait out of that basin. Walt there hauled perch out of it. Greta's husband, God rest him, rebuilt the fuel dock with his own hands." He did not look at Greta, but she felt the words go through her like a draft from an open door. "Now a man who has never tied a bowline in his life wants forty years of that water for sixty condominiums and a boardwalk with a coffee shop on it. And the survey he's waving around was signed on the second of July. The public comment period on this lease closed on the thirtieth of June. I want that on the record. I want somebody to explain to me how a survey signed after the comment period closed gets to be the reason we vote on Tuesday night."

The room went quiet in the particular way it did when a man said a thing everyone had been thinking and nobody had wanted to say first.

Gordon Strand laughed, not unkindly. "Dale, the survey is a formality."

"Then it won't mind being read into the record," Dale said.

"The town attorney has reviewed the survey," Ruth said. "The date issue is noted, Dale. We'll take it up at the final vote."

"You'll take it up when it suits you," Dale said. He sat down. His face had gone a dull red across the cheekbones, and he took the brown pill bottle from his breast pocket, glanced at it, and put it away again without opening it.

Greta typed, and typed, and kept her face very still. Across the room, Tessa Quill had stopped taking notes. She was looking at the manila folder on her knees as though it had grown heavier in the last minute.

At eight-thirty-eight, Ted Vance asked whether the council could delay the vote until the survey question was settled, and Ruth said she was inclined to agree with him, and Ed Matlin objected that the lease had been in front of the council since April, and they went around for twelve minutes on whether to hold the vote on the twenty-seventh or the thirtieth. In the end they chose the twenty-seventh, a Monday, at a special session in the same room. Helen wrote it down. Greta wrote it down.

At eight-fifty, Ruth tapped her gavel. "We'll take a ten-minute recess," she said. "Coffee's in the lobby, and the facilities are at the top of the stairs."

Greta pressed the key. The tape advanced. She stood, stretched her back, and checked the basket. Nearly five feet of paper, neatly folded, the first twenty minutes of a dispute that would decide the shape of Lantern Bay's north shore for forty years. She looked up.

People were moving. Carla and Ed went out toward the stairs, still talking. Helen closed her laptop and went after them. Walt Brandt stayed in his seat, cup in hand. Priya stood at the gallery rail and mouthed something Greta could not read, which probably meant that she was going to buy coffee and was asking whether Greta wanted any. Greta shook her head.

What Greta saw next she would think about for the rest of her life, in the way one notices a single bird at the edge of a field after a long drive, and only later realizes it was the only thing moving.

Dale went out into the lobby, and Gordon Strand followed him, slowly, as if he had decided to go and talk to the coffee urn and happened to be walking in the same direction. Bernice Oakes stood up from the back row, slung her laptop bag over her shoulder, and went after them. The bangles on her wrist caught the light from the hallway and flashed once, like something thrown.

The door swung shut.

Greta sat back down. It was none of her business. She was there to take down what was said in the room, and what was said outside the room belonged to the people who had said it. She poured herself a glass of the water she would not drink and looked at her own handwriting on the tape, which she could read perfectly well from where she sat: *7:58, item 7, Strand, Lakeside, sixty units, forty years.* And below that, in a shorthand that was almost private, a line she had written without thinking about it at all: *Whitcomb, survey date, comment closed 6/30.*

At nine-oh-five, Ruth gaveled the room back to order, and Greta pressed the time-mark key again. The tape showed the moment in neat printed numerals: *9:05, resumed.* Bernice Oakes came back through the door at the back of the room and sat down in the back row beside Walt Brandt. She reached into her bag for a notebook, and the bangles on her wrist chimed softly against the metal of the chair arm. Walt glanced at her, then at the front of the room, and said nothing.

Dale did not come back for another four minutes. When he did, he walked in with his jaw set and did not look at Gordon Strand as he passed him. He sat down, pulled his windbreaker closed, and folded his hands on the table in front of him like a man determined not to fidget. Gordon came in a minute later and sat down in the front row with a smile that did not reach his eyes.

The rest of the meeting went quickly. The council voted to adjourn at nine-fourteen. Ruth said goodnight to the room, and the room began to empty.

Greta packed her machine. She unclipped the tape from the basket, folded it into a flat stack, slipped a rubber band around it, and tucked it into the zippered pocket of her canvas bag. She was halfway to the aisle when a shadow fell across the table, and she looked up to find Dale Whitcomb standing there, his windbreaker zipped to the collarbone, his hands jammed into its pockets.

"Greta," he said.

"Dale."

He looked down at her canvas bag. He did not touch it. "Your machine doesn't lie, does it?"

"It only writes what's said," Greta said carefully. "That's the whole job."

"Good," Dale said. He looked at her for a moment longer, and something in his eyes was almost apologetic, though she could not have said what for. "That's what I'm counting on."

He turned and went down the stairs without waiting for an answer. Greta listened to his footsteps fade, heard the door to Front Street open and close, and then there was only the sound of the coffee urn in the empty lobby, gurgling away to itself as if nothing had happened.

She put her hand on the canvas bag. Under the cloth she could feel the stiff edge of the folded tape, the record of everything anyone had said in that room that night, including the things they had not meant the room to hear.

It was the last full sentence Dale Whitcomb ever spoke in her hearing.

## Chapter 2: Judge on the Notes

The drive from Front Street to the lake house took eleven minutes if the road was clear, and on the night of July 14 it was clear, though Greta drove slowly anyway. She took Cutter Street out past the sewer work and the shuttered ice cream stand, then climbed the long curve of County Route 9 up onto the bluff, where the trees opened and you could see the whole bay spread below you like a dark plate with a few lights set on its rim. Juniper Lane was a gravel road that dropped off to the right just before the bend, and at the bottom of it, half hidden by a stand of white pines, was the house her late husband had built for them in the year they were married. Greta had lived in it for thirty-one years, and she still turned onto the gravel every evening with a small private pleasure, the way a person might touch a familiar doorframe in passing.

She parked beside the porch, carried her canvas bag and her machine case up the steps, and let herself in. The kitchen light was still on, the way she had left it. The screen door swung shut behind her with its usual soft complaint.

Judge was waiting at the top of the hall. He was a large gray tabby with a face like a mildly disappointed banker and a tail that he held straight up when he was pleased and straight out behind him when he was not. Tonight it was straight up. He walked the length of the hall in front of her with a sort of dignified hurry, stopped at the door of the spare room, and sat down in front of it to wait for her to catch up.

"I know," Greta said. "I've been out all evening. Nobody asked me to be a reporter for the entire county."

Judge looked at her. Judge had opinions about her work schedule and did not hide them.

She went into the spare room and set her machine case on the desk by the window. The room smelled of paper and the cedar lining of the old chest where she kept her transcripts from the courthouse years. Steno notes filled three cardboard boxes along the far wall, each labeled in her own square handwriting with a case name and a year. Most of them had gone to the county archives long ago, but Greta had never been able to throw away the notes themselves, the raw pages of pencil shorthand that she had written in courtrooms before there were machines to do it for her. They were the first thing she had ever written with her own hand, and she thought of them the way other people thought of old photographs.

The bench under the window, which she had cushioned with an old quilt, held her current notes from the Lantern Bay Gazette's verbatim meeting record, the job she had taken on three years earlier to keep her hand in. It held a stack of the June minutes, a box of pencils, a cold mug of tea. And it held Judge's favorite place to sleep, which was directly on top of anything Greta needed to read.

He hopped up onto the bench, turned around twice, and lay down on the tape she had just pulled from her bag, his tail curled over the top of the stack.

"That's the record of the meeting," Greta said.

Judge closed his eyes.

She sighed and sat down on the stool beside the desk. Gently, one finger at a time, she slid the tape out from under the cat's body. Judge permitted this with the air of a monarch allowing his subjects to approach the throne. She spread the first fifteen feet of paper across the desk under the lamp, weighted the edges with a pencil and a glass, and began to read.

It was one thing to take down a meeting in real time and another to read it back. In the room, you were a machine, and then at the end you were a person again, slowly remembering what it had felt like to sit in the middle of all that talk. On paper, the meeting came back to her in strange, stripped-down pieces. *Matlin: I move we accept the June minutes as presented.* *Vance: Second, if that's the right word for it.* *Sayer: It's the word.* The little jokes lost their timing on paper and became plain. The sidewalk benches read like a sales pitch. Ted Vance's questions read like a man trying to find a door in a wall.

Then she reached the item seven section, and the room came back all at once.

*Strand Lakeside. Forty years. Sixty units. Boardwalk.* Her own shorthand had put Gordon's speech into neat, quick lines, and she could hear his warm voice saying *a formality* as clearly as if he were standing at the other end of the desk. She read on. *Whitcomb: This town gave its harbor to the families who built it.* She read the line twice, the second time more slowly, because it was the sort of sentence that a court reporter did not get to hear often, a sentence that was said in exactly the way it was meant. Then she came to the comment-period date, the line she had written in the margin without thinking, and she sat back on the stool and let out a long breath.

*Comment closed 6/30. Survey 7/2.*

It was a small matter. It was also, she knew, the sort of small matter that could decide a vote. The town had set a deadline for public comment, and the survey was supposed to have been completed before the deadline so that the commission could weigh it. If it had not, the survey could be ignored, and the lease could go forward on the strength of everything else. Dale had understood that. Greta suspected Ruth Kalinowski had understood it too, and that Ruth had decided to let the matter wait for the twenty-seventh because she did not want to be the one to say it out loud.

She turned the paper over and kept reading. Ted Vance. Carla Sayer. The vote delay. *Monday, the twenty-seventh.* Her time marks came up at intervals, neat and regular. *7:00. 7:05. 7:10.*

*8:50, recess.*

She stopped there. Her eyes went to the words beneath it, which she had typed, as she always did, in the order the room had spoken, with nothing added and nothing taken away.

*8:50, recess.* Then, a few lines below, after the room had emptied and the tape had been still for ten minutes: *9:05, resumed.*

Between those two marks was nothing. The tape had simply stopped, because the room had stopped talking and Greta had stopped typing. She had been sitting at the table with her hands in her lap, watching the lobby door and wondering whether she should go and find a glass of water for herself. The recess had been dull. She had seen nothing she thought worth recording.

But she had not been able to see the lobby, and she had not been able to hear it. She had seen Dale go out, and Gordon after him, and Bernice Oakes after both of them. She had heard the bangles chime at the door.

Greta frowned at the paper and thought about the little brown pill bottle.

She got up, went to the window, and looked out at the bay. From the bluff, she could just see the lights of Front Street through the trees, and the dark shape of the harbor beyond them, a few pale points on the water where the mooring buoys caught the light. She could see the back of the Harbor Office, its low roof and its one bright window, and the strip of gravel where the commission's trucks were parked overnight. She could not see the back stairs. They were on the far side of the building, behind a line of cedar hedging that the commission had planted to hide the fuel tanks. She had never once thought about them until that moment.

Her phone buzzed on the desk.

She had turned it to silent before the meeting, and she had forgotten it entirely. Now the screen lit up with a notification she did not recognize at first, a small gray symbol with a number beside it. She picked the phone up, frowning.

*Missed call. Dale Whitcomb. 9:41 PM.*

Under it, in a second line she had not expected, a voicemail icon.

She sat down again and looked at the screen for a long moment. It was ten thirty-two in the evening. Dale had called her at nine forty-one, while she was still in the hall with her bag over her shoulder, watching the last of the council members leave. He had not called her in the eight years she had known him. The town had a number for transcripts, and a number for the Gazette, and a number for her email. Dale had always preferred to shout across a room.

She pressed play.

There was a rustling sound, like a hand moving over fabric, and then Dale's voice came through the speaker, lower than she remembered, with a hoarse edge to it that made her think of a man who had been talking all night to someone who would not listen.

*"Greta, it's Dale Whitcomb. I'm going to make this quick. I was in the lobby tonight and I heard somebody at the back tell Gordon Strand they'd have the record cleaned up by morning. I didn't catch the name. Your machine got me right, didn't it? Don't let anybody tell you different. Come by the Harbor Office at seven tomorrow morning. Pier Street, the back door. Bring your tape."*

A pause. The rustle again. Then, very quietly, as if he had turned his head away from the phone to speak to someone else in the room:

*"So. Let them."*

The message ended.

Greta played it again. Then a third time, with her eyes closed, the way she had once listened to a witness's testimony on a bad recording to find the word that had been swallowed. *Somebody at the back.* Her mind filled the room with faces. Gordon in the front row. Tessa beside him with the folder. Walt under the portrait. Helen with her hand on the laptop. Priya in the gallery. Ed and Carla on the dais. And in the back row, beside Walt, the bangles glinting in the fluorescent light as Bernice Oakes reached for her notebook.

Half of the room had been at the back of the room at one point or another that night. Dale had not said who. He had only said that someone had said it, and that he had heard.

She typed a reply with her thumb: *Seven. I'll be there. I'll bring the tape.* She sent it, and watched the small word *Delivered* appear beneath it, and then, after a moment, the phone lit up again with a single line in reply.

*Good.*

She set the phone face down on the desk.

Judge woke, stretched his front legs out until his claws pricked the quilt, yawned enormously, and jumped down from the bench onto the desk, landing squarely on the tape. He stalked across the paper, turned around, and sat down in the middle of item seven, his tail wrapped neatly around his feet.

"You're no help at all," Greta told him.

He did not dispute it.

She gathered the tape from under him as gently as she could, rolled it back up, and slid it into the canvas bag. Then she went to the kitchen and made herself a cup of chamomile, which she did not especially like, because her doctor had once told her to try to sleep more and she had found that it helped her to pretend she was following instructions. She drank it standing at the sink, looking out at the black water of the bay beyond the trees.

She went to bed at a quarter past eleven. She set her alarm clock for six-thirty, which gave her time for coffee, a shower, and the drive to Pier Street before seven, with enough margin to take the long way around the marina if the morning fog was heavy. She put her phone on the nightstand, face down, and turned out the lamp.

Judge came in a few minutes later, as he always did, and walked the length of the bed with the careful deliberation of a cat who has decided that the bed is his and only his. He stopped beside the nightstand, looked at the alarm clock, and then, with one neat sweep of a paw, sent it skittering off the edge of the table.

It hit the floorboards with a bright little clatter and went silent.

Greta, half asleep already, heard the noise and thought, *I'll deal with that in the morning.* She turned onto her side and did not wake again until the sun had climbed well above the pines.

## Chapter 3: The Phone Before Seven

The phone rang at twenty minutes to eight, and Greta came awake all at once, the way she had learned to wake in courtrooms, with a jolt that ran from her heart to her fingertips. The light in the room was bright and warm and entirely wrong. She reached for the phone, found it, and saw the name on the screen.

"Priya?"

"Greta, are you up?" Priya's voice was tight and careful, the voice of a woman trying hard to say a thing she did not want to be true. "Lyle Kemp came in to unlock the marina at six fifty-five and he found Dale. At the bottom of the back stairs at the Harbor Office. Sam Ortiz is over there now. I'm at the shop. I just got the call from Ruth."

Greta sat up so quickly that the room tilted. For a moment she could not make the words fit together in her head. Then they did, and the room tilted back.

"Dale," she said.

"I'm sorry. I know you two were—"

"We weren't anything," Greta said. "He asked me to come by at seven."

There was a short silence on the line. Priya, who knew her well, did not ask what she meant.

"I'm going over there," Greta said. "I'll call you."

She hung up, got out of bed, and found that her feet were cold on the boards. The alarm clock lay on the floor beside the nightstand, its face turned up to the ceiling, its hands still pointing at six-thirty as though nothing had happened to any of them. She picked it up, looked at it, and set it on the nightstand with its back to the room.

She dressed in the first clothes that came to hand: khaki slacks, a blue blouse, the cardigan she had worn to every court she had ever sat in. She did not stop to make coffee. She did not stop to feed the cat, who came trotting down the hall as she passed the kitchen and looked up at her with an expression of mild outrage. She put the canvas bag over her shoulder, went out onto the porch, and was halfway down the gravel of Juniper Lane before she realized she had left her machine case on the desk in the spare room.

She stopped. She stood in the gravel with the sun on the back of her neck and her heart going like a hammer. Then she turned around, went back up the steps, and got the case. The canvas bag was heavy and zipped against her hip, with the tape rolled up tight inside. Dale had said *bring your tape*. She had brought her tape. It was the only thing she had been asked to do.

The drive down the bluff took twenty-two minutes. The fog had lifted off the bay, but it lay in a low white band along the shoreline, so that Front Street appeared and disappeared as she came down into town. The Gull Café had its shutters up and a single light on in the kitchen. A delivery truck was parked outside the bakery with its back doors open. A little boy in a red sweatshirt stood on the sidewalk outside the library, watching a dog that was watching a pigeon, and did not look up as she passed.

Pier Street ran along the north side of the harbor, past the boat yards and the fuel dock and the old bait shop that Gordon Strand had leased for his sales office. At the far end, where the road turned to gravel and gave out in a loose apron of crushed shell, sat the Harbor Office, a low brick building with a flat roof, a white door in front, and a set of concrete steps running down the back from a landing to the boat ramp below.

Greta could see the flashing lights before she reached the turn. Two county patrol cars were parked nose to tail at the edge of the gravel, and a third stood empty on the far side with its door open. A strip of yellow tape had been strung between two posts at the edge of the lot. Beyond the tape, a little group of people stood in the cool shade of the cedar hedge, speaking in low voices.

She parked behind the last patrol car and got out. The air smelled of diesel and water and the sweet rot of seaweed on the rocks at the foot of the ramp.

Lyle Kemp sat on a bench near the fuel tanks with his cap in his hands. He was sixty years old, a thick, slow man who had run the marina for as long as Greta had lived in Lantern Bay, and he looked as though he had aged ten years since breakfast. He lifted his head when he saw her. His eyes were wet.

"Greta," he said. "I'm sorry. I didn't know who else to call."

"You called Priya," Greta said gently. "That was right."

Lyle nodded and looked back down at his cap.

Deputy Sam Ortiz came around the side of the building at that moment, pulling off a pair of blue gloves. He was thirty-six, the only county deputy who lived year-round in Lantern Bay, and he had grown up two streets over from the bakery. He had been a good student, Greta remembered, the kind of boy who wrote careful, unhurried essays and turned them in on time. He saw her and stopped.

"Greta," he said. "You shouldn't be here."

"I was supposed to be here at seven," Greta said. "Dale asked me to come."

Sam's face changed. He looked at her for a long moment, and she saw him decide something.

"Come over here," he said, and walked her a few steps along the side of the building, away from the bench and the others. He stopped at the corner of the hedge, where she could see past him to the top of the back stairs.

At the foot of the stairs, a blue tarp had been laid over something on the concrete. It was neatly arranged, as if whoever had placed it had wanted to be careful. Beside it stood a pair of rubber boots, the kind a man wore to go down a boat ramp. Greta did not look at the tarp for very long. She looked at the boots.

"Dale fell," Sam said quietly. "From the landing to the bottom of the stairs. It looks like his heart went first. We found his pills in his shirt pocket, the ones he has for his angina, and the bottle was half empty. Lyle thinks he was coming down to check the ramp lines before the morning tide. The dock's wet from the rain last night and the bottom step is slick. It's a bad fall, Greta. I'm sorry."

Greta's throat felt tight. She could not look away from the boots.

"What time did he die?" she asked.

Sam hesitated. "We won't know for sure until the county medical examiner has him. The preliminary is between six-thirty and seven. Maybe a little earlier. Lyle says the back door was unlocked when he came around the building, and that's the first thing I'm going to ask about."

"Six-thirty," Greta said. She was thinking, without quite meaning to, that she had been asleep at six-thirty. That she had heard the clock hit the floor and gone back to sleep with the thought that she would deal with it in the morning. She had been a few minutes' drive away, with her alarm on the floor and her tape in the hall, while a man she had known for eight years lay at the foot of a set of concrete steps, listening for the engine of a car that would never come.

"Greta," Sam said. "Are you all right?"

"No," she said. "I don't think I am."

He put a hand on her arm, lightly, the way a person might steady a glass on a table. Then he looked back toward the tarp, and the lines of his face settled into something harder and more careful.

"Dale called you last night," he said. It was not a question.

"He left a voicemail," Greta said. "At nine forty-one. He wanted me here at seven. He said somebody had said something in the lobby at the recess that he didn't like, and he wanted me to bring my tape."

Sam was quiet for a moment. Then he said, very softly, "Show me the voicemail, Greta. Before anybody else gets to it."

Across the lot, the crowd by the hedge had begun to turn toward them. Gordon Strand had arrived in a dark sedan and was standing beside it with his phone in his hand and a look of grave concern on his face. Beside him, Tessa Quill stood very still, her arms wrapped around her ribs, staring not at the tarp but at the open back door of the Harbor Office, where the light from inside fell out across the concrete landing in a long pale stripe.

And beyond both of them, at the edge of the gravel, Bernice Oakes was getting out of a white hatchback with a cracked red taillight on the left side, and she was looking straight at Greta with an expression that Greta could not read at all.


## Chapter 4: Natural Causes

Sam Ortiz listened to the voicemail twice, once with the phone held out at arm's length and once with it pressed to his ear, standing in the shade of the cedar hedge with his back to the crowd. Greta watched his face while he did it. He had a habit she remembered from high school, of tilting his head slightly to the left when he was concentrating, as if he were trying to hear a sound coming from the next room.

"He says *somebody at the back*," Sam said finally.

"He said he didn't catch the name," Greta said.

"And he says to bring your tape." Sam handed the phone back to her. "Did you bring it?"

"It's in my bag."

"Hold it there for now." He glanced toward the back of the Harbor Office, where the open door let out a stripe of pale light. "Greta, I'm going to ask you to write down everything he said to you last night. Everything. The meeting, the recess, the lobby, what he said to you at the table. And I want you to write down what you saw during the recess, even if you think it doesn't matter."

"I didn't see much," Greta said. "I was sitting at my table. Dale went out to the lobby with Gordon Strand. Bernice Oakes went after them. That's all I know."

"Bernice Oakes went out after Dale."

"After both of them. She came back at nine-oh-five."

Sam wrote the name on a small notebook in careful capitals. He did not say anything about it, but Greta saw him underline it once.

The county medical examiner's investigator arrived at eight-twenty in a silver minivan with a rack of bright orange cones on the roof, and Greta stepped back from the hedge to give her room. The investigator was a small, brisk woman named Dana Reyes whom Greta had met once at a county inquest years before. She nodded to Greta without stopping and went straight to the blue tarp. Sam followed her. Greta could see only their backs through the gap in the hedge, bent over the shape at the foot of the steps, speaking in low voices.

While she waited, Greta looked at the lot.

It was a plain stretch of gravel and crushed shell that ran from the front of the Harbor Office down along the side of the building to the back, where the commission's trucks were parked and where a single tall light pole stood over a cluster of old oil drums. Beyond the drums, a row of cedar hedging hid the fuel tanks from the marina road. The back door stood open. The concrete landing outside it was about five feet deep and had a rusted iron railing along one side. From the landing, a flight of fourteen concrete steps ran down to the boat ramp, which sloped into the water and had a wooden walkway along its north edge where the dock lines were tied.

Greta counted the steps from the top, the way she counted everything. Thirteen. She could see the bottom one, slick with a dull dark sheen that might have been water or might have been something else, and she could see that the railing on the lower half of the flight had a bright new scrape along its middle bar, as though someone had caught it with a shoe or a hip and slid.

Her stomach turned. She looked away.

Gordon Strand had come around the side of the hedge with his phone still in his hand. He saw her and his face arranged itself into an expression of grief. He walked over to her, and his shoes crunched on the shell.

"Greta," he said softly. "I'm so sorry. I was on the phone with my office when Lyle called. I came straight over. This is awful."

"It is," Greta said.

"Dale and I didn't always see eye to eye." Gordon lowered his voice, as if the subject needed care. "He was passionate. I respected that. He was a good man who cared about this town, and I think we'd have reached a better deal if we'd had more time together." He looked toward the tarp, then back at her. "Has anyone told Ryan yet?"

"I don't know," Greta said.

"I'll call him." Gordon sighed. "It's the least I can do."

He touched her arm briefly, the way Sam had, and walked away toward the deputies. Greta watched him go. She thought about the recess, and the lobby, and a man in a tan linen jacket standing near a coffee urn with his head bent low to listen to someone who was trying not to be overheard. She had not seen Gordon's face at that moment. She had only seen the back of Bernice's head and the flash of silver on her wrist.

At the edge of the lot, Tessa Quill stood apart from the others, one arm still wrapped across her ribs. She was looking at the back door again. When Greta glanced at her, Tessa's eyes skittered away, and she took a small step back toward the bait shop, as if she had been caught doing something she should not.

Then Bernice Oakes walked over, slowly, through the shell, and stopped a few feet from Greta with her hands folded in front of her.

She had put on a black cardigan over her blouse and had pinned her hair back neatly, but there was a faint redness around her eyes that Greta did not think came entirely from the morning wind off the bay. Her bangles were quiet. She had taken them off, Greta realized, and was holding them in her left hand, two thin silver circles pressed together like a set of keys, and a bare place on her wrist where a third should have been.

"Greta," Bernice said. "I'm so sorry. I heard from Helen. I came as soon as I could."

"Thank you," Greta said.

"He was rude to me in June." Bernice's voice was steady, but her gaze went to the tarp and stayed there. "When I came by the Harbor Office to pick up a transcript, he told me in front of the whole staff that the town didn't need an outsider's typing. He wasn't wrong about everything, but he wasn't kind about it." She drew a breath. "He didn't deserve to die on those steps."

"No," Greta said.

"I left the meeting early last night," Bernice went on. "I want you to know that. I had a long drive ahead of me and I had to be in Fennick Falls for a client at eight this morning. I told Helen I'd be out of there by a quarter past eight, and I was. I was home by nine-thirty." She tilted her head slightly. "I didn't see Dale after the lobby. I didn't speak to him. I'm sure the deputy will ask me the same thing."

Greta looked at her for a long moment.

She could see the car over Bernice's shoulder, the white hatchback parked on the gravel near the bait shop. It had a dent in the back door on the passenger side and a long, careful scratch along the bumper, the sort of mark a person gets backing out of a tight driveway. And the left taillight was cracked straight across, a thin red line through the lens, like a hairline fracture in a bone.

Greta had sat behind Bernice Oakes in the back row at the Carrow County courthouse, and she had sat beside her at a dozen bar association lunches. She knew what Bernice sounded like when she was being exact, and she knew how she sounded when she was not. Bernice had just been exact.

But Greta also knew exactly what she had seen on the tape, and it was not a quarter past eight. It was a line typed in the margin at nine-oh-five, after the recess, when Bernice Oakes had come back through the door and sat down in the back row beside Walt Brandt, her bangles chiming against the arm of the chair.

"That's very clear," Greta said.

Bernice's eyes narrowed a fraction, then smoothed over again. "I've always been clear, Greta. You know that."

"I do," Greta said.

Bernice nodded, turned, and walked back toward the hatchback. She did not put her bangles on again. She got in the driver's seat, shut the door, and sat there with her hands on the wheel, looking straight ahead at the back of the Harbor Office, where Sam Ortiz had come out onto the landing and was standing at the top of the steps with a clipboard tucked under one arm.

Greta went over to the bench where Lyle Kemp still sat with his cap in his lap.

"Lyle," she said, sitting down beside him. "Did you see anybody else this morning? Before the deputies came?"

He shook his head slowly. "Just the fog. And Walt's truck, down by the boat yard. Walt was going out the way he always does, six o'clock, to check on his boat. He doesn't talk much."

"Did you see Dale's car?"

"He walked," Lyle said. "He always walked from his house on Pier Street when he came in to the office. Said it cleared his head." He looked at his cap. "The back door was unlocked. I came around the side and saw the light was on inside, and then I saw him. I should have called the county right away. I didn't. I went down the steps to see if he was all right, and I knew he wasn't."

"You did the right thing," Greta said.

"I don't feel like I did."

She put a hand on his shoulder and left it there for a moment. Lyle did not look up.

At nine-fifteen, the county van came for the body. Greta stood at the edge of the cedar hedge with her arms folded and watched the two attendants lift the stretcher up the back steps, one slow step at a time, with Sam Ortiz at the top holding the door. The blue tarp was gone. The stretcher was covered with a plain gray sheet. Greta looked at the sheet until the van had pulled away, and then she looked at the empty landing, and at the stripe of pale light falling out through the open door, and at the rusted railing with its bright new scrape.

Sam came down the steps to meet her. He looked tired and older than his age.

"Preliminary," he said quietly. "Dana Reyes thinks it was the fall, probably the head, maybe the heart first. She'll know more after the autopsy. But the pills were in his pocket, and the bottle was half-empty, and Lyle found him at the bottom of the stairs with the railing bent. It's a bad accident, Greta. I know you don't want to hear that."

"I don't," Greta said. "What about his phone?"

Sam blinked. "What?"

"Dale always had his phone in the windbreaker. The inside pocket, zipped. He used to hold it up at the meetings to show people the weather radar, and he'd lose his temper when the screen went dark. He'd have had it with him last night. He called me from it."

Sam looked at her for a long moment. Then he turned and looked up at the landing, as if he could see through the door into the room beyond.

"We didn't find a phone on him," he said. "Keys, wallet, the pill bottle, a handkerchief, a pen. No phone."

"Then somebody has it," Greta said. "Or he left it somewhere."

"Or he dropped it on the stairs," Sam said. "In the dark. People do that." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. "I'll look. I'll look in the lot and along the ramp. But Greta, I need you to hear me. Sheriff Corliss is going to want this closed by the end of the week. He's been on the phone to Ruth Kalinowski twice already this morning. A seventy-one-year-old man with a heart condition falls down a flight of concrete steps in the rain. That's what it looks like. That's what the report is going to say unless somebody gives me a reason to write something else."

"Then I'll give you one," Greta said. "After I've read my tape."

He let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "I was afraid you'd say that."

"You were the one who listened to his voicemail," Greta said. "You know he wasn't calling to say goodbye."

Sam did not answer that. He looked at her with an expression she could not quite read. Then he nodded once, shortly, and walked back toward the patrol cars with his clipboard under his arm.

Greta stood alone by the hedge for a while longer. The bay had burned off most of its fog by then, and the water beyond the boat yard was flat and bright and perfectly calm, the sort of morning that made people want to go out in a boat and forget about anything at all. Somewhere out on the north basin, a single gull was circling above the mooring buoys, crying out in a thin, sharp voice that carried across the water.

She took out her phone and typed a note to herself, the way she had been taught to do after any hearing that might later be disputed: the time, the place, the names of the people present, and exactly what each one had said. Her hands were not quite steady. The screen blurred once, and she blinked until it cleared.

*8:20 a.m. Harbor Office lot. Sam Ortiz. Dana Reyes, medical examiner's investigator. Gordon Strand: "I was on the phone with my office." Bernice Oakes: "I was home by nine-thirty." Dale's phone: not on the body.*

She stopped typing and looked at the last line for a long time.

Then, very carefully, she added one more line beneath it: *Bangles off. Held in left hand.*

## Chapter 5: The Gull Café

By noon on Wednesday, the whole town knew, and by one o'clock every table in the Gull Café had a version of the story at it. Greta had known the place for thirty years. It had been a diner once, with a row of red vinyl stools and a jukebox that played nothing newer than 1979, and it had become a café only in the sense that the owner, a warm and unflappable woman named Frances Dunn, had put an espresso machine behind the counter and a chalkboard out front that listed the soup of the day in a looping, optimistic hand. The pie was still the best in the county. The coffee was still better than it had any right to be.

Greta took the stool at the far end of the counter, nearest the window, where she could see the marina and the boat yard and the long gray line of the breakwater. Walt Brandt was already there, on the stool beside hers, with a plate of eggs he had not touched and a mug of black coffee going cold at his elbow. He did not look up when she sat down. He was looking out the window at the water, where a single fishing boat was coming in slowly past the breakwater, its outboard muttering.

"That's Gene Osterhout's boat," Walt said. "He's been out since five. He'll have nothing to show for it."

"Probably not," Greta agreed.

Frances came down the counter with the coffee pot and filled Greta's cup without asking. Her eyes were red-rimmed, and she set the pot down a little harder than she needed to.

"Lyle's been in," she said. "He's in the back with a bottle of beer and he won't come out. Owen Ferris wanted to go over and sit with him and Lyle told him to get lost." She shook her head. "Dale Whitcomb. I can't believe it. I sold him a sandwich every Friday for twenty years. Ham and rye, no mustard, extra pickle. He never once said thank you and he never once forgot the pickle."

"He was a hard man to like," Walt said, not unkindly. He still did not look away from the water.

"He was a hard man to like," Frances agreed, "and he was a good man to have in the room. There's a difference."

She went back down the counter, and Greta picked up her coffee. The mug was hot in her hands, and she held it for a moment without drinking, feeling the warmth seep into her fingers.

"You walk out to your boat at six," she said to Walt quietly.

"Every morning for forty-two years," he said. "Six o'clock, rain or shine. The *Mabel Rose* is at the end of the north dock, third slip from the fuel pump. I check her lines and I check the bilge and I drink a cup of coffee in the cabin with the door open so I can hear the water." He turned his head at last and looked at her, his gray eyes steady beneath heavy white brows. "I saw Dale yesterday afternoon. Not in the morning. Here, at the counter, about five o'clock. He had his phone out and he was pecking at it like a bird. Typing and deleting and typing again. I asked him what was wrong and he said his thumbs were slower than his temper."

"Did he say what he was typing?"

"He said he was sending you a message." Walt picked up his fork at last and poked at his eggs. "He said, *Greta's going to get me the record, Walt. The real one. They're going to try to make me sound like a fool and Greta's machine is going to say otherwise.* Then he laughed in that way he had, like somebody had just taken the last bolt out of a motor. He said, *I've been robbed of my own voice.*" Walt put the fork down. "I didn't know what he meant. I thought maybe he'd been talking to his son."

"Ryan," Greta said.

"Ryan's in Chicago, I think. He hasn't been home in a year, not since the thing at the holiday dinner." Walt glanced at her. "You knew that, I expect."

"I knew they weren't close," Greta said.

"Nobody was close to Dale Whitcomb," Walt said. "He didn't let anybody get that close. He only let them get near enough to shout at."

The bell over the door jingled, and both of them looked up.

Gordon Strand came in with Tessa Quill two steps behind him. He had changed his shirt since the morning, and his hair was combed flat and neat, but his face looked like a man who had not slept well and had been working on that problem with a great deal of effort. Tessa looked worse. She wore a loose green cardigan that was too big for her and carried a canvas tote on one shoulder, with a folder poking out of the top. She did not look at the counter. She went straight to a booth at the back and sat down with her back to the wall, and when Frances came to take her order, she shook her head and said she was not hungry.

Gordon came to the counter. He nodded to Walt, who did not nod back, and then to Greta, who did not look away from him.

"Greta," he said. "I wanted to say something to you privately, if I could. I know we haven't always agreed. But you've been a fixture in this town for a long time, and I know how much Dale's death must have hit you."

"Thank you," Greta said evenly.

"I've been in touch with Helen," Gordon went on, leaning one elbow on the counter. "The council minutes are being finalized this afternoon. The town will post them on the website when they're ready. I understand you'll want to see them. I think the record is going to reflect the discussion accurately, and I think that's going to matter to people." He looked down at her with an expression of gentle, almost paternal concern. "The vote is still set for the twenty-seventh. That part has not changed. Dale's passing doesn't alter the council's obligation to decide. I hope you'll see that."

"I see that you're in a hurry," Greta said.

Gordon's smile did not waver, but something moved behind it. He straightened. "We all want the same thing, Greta. A harbor that works for the people who live here. I'm sure we'll find our way to it."

He turned to the booth at the back and lifted a hand toward Tessa. "Let's go. We have a call with the state at two."

Tessa stood up so fast she nearly knocked over the salt shaker. She clutched the tote to her ribs and hurried past the counter without looking at anyone. As she passed Greta's stool, she slowed just long enough to say, in a voice so low that Greta barely heard it, "The survey folder is in my car. I'm supposed to be taking it to the state office in Lansing on Friday."

Greta turned her head. Tessa was already walking toward the door.

"Tessa," Greta said.

The younger woman stopped with her hand on the door handle, half turned, her face pale and anxious.

"Are you all right?" Greta asked.

For a moment Tessa looked as though she might say something real. Her mouth opened. Then Gordon said her name from the sidewalk, sharply, and Tessa flinched and pushed the door open, and the bell jingled again as she went out into the sunshine.

Walt watched the door swing shut. "That girl's scared to death," he said.

"Of Gordon?"

"Of something," Walt said. "She's been looking at that harbor office like it might bite her since before Dale died. Lyle says she's been in there twice this week, once at night."

Greta sipped her coffee slowly. She did not know whether to believe him, though she thought Walt Brandt was not a man who tended to invent things. He had been quiet at the meeting, and he had stayed in his seat through the recess, and he had seen the tide come in and go out over fifty years without ever once needing to make a speech about it.

"Walt," she said. "Did you see anyone at the harbor last night? After the meeting?"

He picked up his cup and drank the cold coffee in one swallow, then set it down with great care, as though he were setting down a fragile thing.

"I went home at nine-thirty," he said. "Drove the truck along the shore road and went to bed at ten. Just as always. I don't go out at night anymore." He looked at her sidelong. "But I'll be out at six tomorrow, same as always. If somebody's down by that office with a car at six o'clock, I'll see them. You can count on that."

"Thank you," Greta said.

"Don't thank me yet," Walt said. "I'm an old man and I don't see much past the end of the dock. But I see the cars."

She paid for her coffee and left a dollar on the counter for Frances, who pretended not to notice it. Outside, the sun was hot on the sidewalk and the breeze off the bay smelled of salt and cut grass. Owen Ferris from the hardware store was standing on the corner with a clipboard, talking to a man in a cowboy hat whom Greta did not know. Two boys on bicycles went past her in a blur of red and blue, shouting something about a dock.

Her phone buzzed in her pocket. She took it out and saw a text from Priya.

*Ruth called. Funeral is Saturday at Our Lady of the Harbor, 10 a.m. Ryan is flying in tonight from Midway. Are you going to be okay?*

Greta typed back: *I don't know yet. I'll be at the funeral. I need to read my tape first.*

*Read it slowly,* Priya wrote. *And come see me after. I have a stack of Agatha Christie in the back room that I'm not going to sell to anybody but you.*

Greta almost smiled. She put the phone away and walked toward the library, where the public computers were, because she wanted to see the town's website before Helen posted anything and before anyone else had a chance to read it. She had a feeling about the minutes. She had been taking down the record of Lantern Bay's council meetings for three years, and she knew how the draft usually looked on the screen the afternoon after: the header in bold, the attendance list, the motions in neat paragraphs, the votes recorded in parentheses.

She knew it would not look the way the room had sounded.

## Chapter 6: Draft Minutes

The draft minutes went up on the town's website at ten minutes to one, and Greta read them on the library's oldest computer, the one with the sticky space bar and the chair that tilted backward if you leaned on it. Joan Cho, the reference librarian, had given her the desk by the window without asking why, which was one of the reasons Greta loved the Lantern Bay library. Nobody asked why. Nobody needed to.

The PDF opened slowly, pixel by pixel, and then the text came up in a clean serif font with the town seal at the top and a banner across the first page that read *DRAFT — SUBJECT TO COUNCIL APPROVAL*. Helen Marsh had typed the header herself, Greta supposed. The date was correct. The meeting was called to order at seven-oh-four. The roll was read. Helen had listed Dale Whitcomb among the present, with a small dark note beside his name that the town had added since the meeting: *(deceased, July 15)*.

Greta's stomach tightened, and she made herself keep reading.

The first hour of the minutes was accurate. It was the sort of thing a clerk could do in her sleep. The June minutes were approved, and the benches were approved, and the sewer grant was approved, with the motions and seconds and votes all where they belonged. Greta scrolled through the first six pages, comparing each line against the tape she had folded into the bag at her feet, and found nothing wrong with any of it. The clerk had even kept Ted Vance's confused questions, though she had trimmed them into a neat paragraph, as if to spare him.

Then she reached item seven, and the paragraphs began to change.

*7. Harbor Lease — Strand Lakeside Partners.* The text described the request in clean, official language. It described the survey, and the date of the survey, and the comment period. It did not mention that the survey had been completed after the period closed. Greta read that section twice, then went back to the top of the page and read the whole item again, slowly, with her finger tracing the lines on the screen.

It was not that the minutes were false. It was that they were smoothed, the way a wooden floor is smoothed after the sander has passed over it. The hard edges were gone. The ragged pauses were gone. Ruth's careful remark about the date issue had been turned into a single neat sentence, which said that *the council noted a question regarding the survey date and agreed to resolve the question at the final vote*. Dale's three sharp sentences in the aisle had become a single paragraph in the passive voice: *Commissioner Whitcomb expressed concerns regarding the timing of the survey.*

Greta sat back. That was the sort of thing a clerk did, the sort of thing Greta herself had done a thousand times in courtrooms, when a witness had said something that made a lawyer's face go white and the judge needed to move on. It was not dishonest. It was simply a kind of mercy, and a kind of loss.

Then she reached the recess.

*8:50 p.m. The council recessed for ten minutes.*

And then, below it, in the same neat type:

*8:52 p.m. Commissioner Whitcomb stated for the record: "The survey satisfies my concerns. I withdraw my objection to the lease and ask that the council proceed to a vote."*

Greta read the line once. Then she read it again. Then she put her hand flat on the desk beside the keyboard, because the desk seemed to have moved a fraction of an inch beneath her.

The council had been in recess at eight fifty-two. Everyone had been in the lobby. Ruth had said *ten minutes* and walked out to the hall. Ed and Carla had gone to the stairs. Helen had gone after them with her laptop under her arm. Greta had sat at her table with her hands in her lap and watched the door swing shut, and she had seen Dale go into the lobby with Gordon Strand behind him, and she had heard no word from either of them.

No words had been recorded. The tape had been silent for fourteen minutes. She had marked it herself, with her own time-key: *8:50, recess. 9:05, resumed.* Between those two lines there was nothing but the faint hum of the fluorescent lights and the distant gurgle of the coffee urn. There was no statement. There was no motion. There was no sentence about the survey, and no withdrawal.

And Dale had not come back into the room until nine-oh-nine. Greta knew that because she had looked up from her tape at the sound of the door. He had walked in with his jaw set and his hands in his pockets, and he had sat down in his chair without saying a word, and he had folded his hands on the table in front of him like a man trying not to fidget. He had said nothing for the rest of the meeting. Not one word.

The clerk's minutes said he had spoken during the recess, in a sentence that was almost exactly the opposite of the sentence he had spoken at the very beginning of the evening, and that the sentence had been recorded at eight fifty-two in the middle of a recess when no one in the room was writing anything down.

Greta scrolled down.

*9:05 p.m. The council reconvened. Commissioner Whitcomb did not speak further on item seven.*

That was true, at least. Then:

*Item 7. Motion by Commissioner Sayer, seconded by Commissioner Matlin, to approve the lease with Strand Lakeside Partners for the north basin, as presented, subject to the conditions in Exhibit B. Vote: Matlin, aye. Sayer, aye. Vance, aye. Kalinowski, no. Haskins, no. The motion carried, 3–2.*

Greta read the vote three times. It was the sort of thing she would have caught instantly in the room, if it had happened. It had not happened. She had not heard a motion. She had not heard a second. She had not heard Ruth call a vote on the lease, or Ed Matlin lift his hand, or Ted Vance hesitate and then say *aye* in his small, uncertain voice. The council had voted, in fact, to delay the matter to the twenty-seventh. It was there in her own tape, in her own hand, under the time mark for 8:38. *Motion to postpone to 7/27. Carried 5–0.*

And the vote recorded on the screen said the opposite.

*The motion carried, 3–2.*

It had the look of a thing that had been placed carefully, after the fact, the way a painter lays a thin glaze over an old canvas and hopes no one notices where the brush strokes overlap. Greta pulled the tape from her bag, unrolled the first ten feet across the library table, and held it down with the edge of her hand. She found the line she needed without looking at the time marks. She had written it without thinking, the way she wrote everything that mattered: *Postpone to 7/27. Carried.*

Then she found the other one, the one she had typed in the margin before the recess, while the room was still arguing. *Whitcomb: Survey date, comment closed 6/30.* And under it, after the recess, in the dull gray stretch of tape where nothing had happened at all, a single time mark: *9:05, resumed.*

She put her finger on the time mark.

*Adjournment,* the draft minutes said at the bottom of the page, in the same clean type. *Motion to adjourn at 8:58 p.m. Carried. The meeting adjourned at 8:58 p.m.*

Greta stared at the number.

Her tape said nine-fourteen. She had typed the time herself, at the bottom of the page, when Ruth said *we're adjourned* and Dale Whitcomb had walked out the door without looking back at Gordon Strand. She had written *9:14, adjourned*, and she had underlined it twice, the way she underlined anything that might later have to be read aloud in a room full of people who did not trust her.

It was worse than the number. The same page had the council reconvening at 9:05, and then, a few lines lower, adjourning at 8:58, eight minutes before it had come back into session. Nobody could adjourn a meeting before it resumed, and the clerk who had typed that line had not noticed that it contradicted the line above it. 16 minutes. 16 minutes in which the record of the evening had been cut short, and a vote had been added, and a sentence had been put into the mouth of a man who had been sitting silently in his chair.

She sat back. The tilting chair creaked beneath her, and the library's ceiling fan turned slowly overhead, pushing the warm air in lazy circles. Somewhere behind her a child was reading aloud in a careful, halting voice. A printer clattered. Joan Cho laughed at something on the phone at the front desk.

At the bottom of the draft, below the signature line where Helen Marsh's name was typed in italics, there was a small note in gray type, the kind a clerk added to say where the minutes had come from.

*Prepared from the audio recording of the July 14 meeting by Oakes Transcription, Fennick Falls, under contract to the Town of Lantern Bay. Reviewed and approved by the Town Clerk.*

Greta read the words slowly, and then she read them once more, and then she closed her eyes.

Bernice. Of course it was Bernice. Bernice had the contract, and Bernice had the audio, and Bernice had been sitting in the back row with her bangles chiming and her notebook open, the record keeper with the best seat in the house.

But Helen had approved it. Helen had put her name at the bottom of the page. Helen had seen the vote, and the time, and the sentence that Dale Whitcomb had never said, and she had signed off on all of it anyway.

Greta opened her phone and called the clerk's office. It rang four times before Helen picked up, her voice thin and breathless, as though she had been crying in the back room or running up a flight of stairs.

"Helen," Greta said. "It's Greta Lowe. I've just read the minutes."

There was a long silence on the line. Greta could hear the faint sound of a radio playing somewhere behind Helen, and then the sound of a door closing, carefully, as if someone did not want to be overheard.

"Greta," Helen said at last, very softly. "I need you to not say anything to anybody yet. Please. Gordon's lawyer called me at ten past midnight last night and again at seven this morning. I signed the draft on my phone from my kitchen. I didn't read it all the way through. I know that's not an excuse. I know it's the worst thing I've ever done." Her voice broke on the last word. "But Greta, Bernice told me the audio was all there, and the vote was already on the page when I opened it, and I didn't think anybody would ever look at the tape."

"I looked at my tape," Greta said.

Helen made a small, wounded sound.

"Where's the audio now?" Greta asked. "The recording from the council room. Is it still on the town's server?"

"It should be," Helen said. "It's on the TownShare drive in the Harbor Office. It's always been there. Only the clerk and the transcription service have access to it." She hesitated. "Greta, you shouldn't go over there alone. Not after what happened this morning."

"I'm not going alone," Greta said. "I'm going with you."

There was another silence, longer this time.

"Tomorrow," Helen said finally. "10 o'clock. Lyle will open the back room for us. I'll bring the key to the drive. But Greta, please. Whatever you find on that server, I need you to promise me you'll come to me first."

"I'll come to you first," Greta said. "If you tell me the truth."

She ended the call and sat looking at the screen for a long time, the cursor blinking beside the line that said *The motion carried, 3–2.*

Then she picked up the tape, rolled it carefully, and slipped it back into the canvas bag. She did not look up when the door to the reading room opened behind her, and she did not turn around when a pair of footsteps came slowly down the aisle between the shelves and paused, just for a moment, behind her chair.

When she finally looked over her shoulder, the aisle was empty, and the only sound was the slow turning of the ceiling fan, and the faint, steady ticking of the clock above the reference desk, which had always been a minute fast.


## Chapter 7: Keys and Badges

Helen Marsh was waiting on the steps of the Harbor Office at ten minutes to ten on Thursday morning, holding a canvas tote in front of her like a shield. She wore a navy cardigan and a pair of sensible flats, and her short gray hair had been brushed flat with a great deal of attention. She had not slept. Greta could see it in the bruised shadows under her eyes and the careful way she held her mouth, as if it might give way if she relaxed it.

"Sam's coming," Helen said, before Greta had reached the bottom of the walk. "I called him. I didn't want to be the only one in there with the server."

"That was wise," Greta said.

"I don't feel wise." Helen turned the tote over in her hands. "I feel like I'm walking into the room where I'm going to be fired."

Lyle Kemp came around the corner of the building with a ring of keys on a leather tab. He had not shaved, and his cap was pulled low over his eyes, but he nodded to both women and unlocked the white front door without a word. The lock turned with a heavy click. Inside, the Harbor Office smelled of floor wax, old coffee, and the sharp chemical tang of the marine charts pinned along the wall, each one marked in red and blue pencil with the depths and the slip numbers and the names of the boats that had been moored there since before anyone in town could remember.

"The back room's through there," Lyle said, pointing. "I'll stay out front. I don't want to be in the way."

"You won't be," Greta said. "Thank you, Lyle."

He went to the front desk and sat down behind it, put his cap on the blotter, and stared at the wall of charts without seeing any of them.

The back room was small and windowless and cold, cooled by a window unit in the far wall that rattled on its brackets. A plain metal desk stood against one side with a computer on it, a tower in a beige case with a yellow sticky note stuck to the front that said, in a careful hand, *TOWNSHARE — DO NOT UNPLUG — ASK HELEN*. Beside the desk stood a gray cabinet about the size of a refrigerator, with a row of small green lights along its top and a brass plate that read *Tidewater Services, Inc.* in worn letters. A cable ran from the cabinet to a wall jack. The room smelled faintly of hot dust.

By the back door, which opened onto the landing and the concrete steps, a keypad was mounted on the wall at shoulder height, with a steel plate beneath it and a narrow slot for a key card. A clipboard hung on a nail beside the keypad, its top sheet covered in handwritten entries.

Greta stopped in front of the clipboard before she did anything else.

"What's that?" Helen asked, from the desk.

"A sign-in sheet for after hours," Greta said. "Somebody has to write their name down if they come in after the office closes. It's on the wall, right where you'd see it going out."

She read the sheet from the top, moving her finger slowly down the columns. The entries were in a dozen different hands, most of them harbor staff and dock workers who came in to check the tide tables. *D. Whitcomb, 6/11, 6:40 a.m., slip keys.* *L. Kemp, 6/18, 5:55 p.m., fuel log.* *B. Lindqvist, 6/20, 8:00 p.m., pump 2 — sorry.* Greta paused on that one, then moved on.

The last entries were from the previous week. *E. Strand, 7/9, 9:50 p.m., survey file.* *T. Quill, 7/10, 4:30 p.m., survey file.* And then, on Tuesday, in neat, rounded letters that sloped slightly to the right:

*T. Quill, 7/14, 9:35 p.m. Survey folder for G. Strand. Back door.*

There was no signature beside the entry, but there was a time, and a reason, and a name. Beneath it, the next line was blank. Nobody had written anything on Wednesday morning.

"Helen," Greta said quietly. "Who is E. Strand?"

Helen came over and looked. Her face tightened. "Gordon's wife. Eleanor. She's on the board of the Friends of the Harbor. She had a key to the commission's survey file for the boardwalk project in the spring." Helen touched the paper with one fingertip, very lightly. "Tessa's not supposed to be here after dark. That's the rule. The survey file is supposed to stay in the commission's locked cabinet. Dale made a fuss about it at the June meeting, I remember. He said the survey was the only document in town that belonged to the public, and anybody who wanted to take it home was going to have to tell the commission why."

"And Tessa took it home," Greta said.

"Tessa took it to Gordon's office on Tuesday night," Helen said. "I'd assume." She looked away from the clipboard. "I don't know what the survey file is for anymore, Greta. I used to think I did."

Greta went to the cabinet and crouched down beside the computer. Helen came behind her, holding the edge of the desk.

"Show me the recordings," Greta said.

Helen sat in the chair. The computer took a long time to wake. When the screen finally lit up, it showed a plain gray desktop with a single folder labeled *Council Recordings*. Helen clicked it. A list of files appeared, each one named in the same tidy format: a two-digit year, a month and day, the word *Regular* or *Special*, and the extension *.wav*.

*CC_0623_Regular.wav.* *CC_0630_Special.wav.* *CC_0707_Regular.wav.*

Then nothing.

Greta leaned in. "Where's the fourteenth?"

Helen stared at the screen. Her lips moved silently. She scrolled down, then back up, then clicked the column heading to sort the list by date, and then by name, and then by size. The file was not there.

"It should be here," she said. Her voice had gone thin. "I put it on the drive myself on Tuesday night. 10:40, I think, I came back from the Center and I uploaded it from my laptop before I went home. I remember because I was so tired I had to type my password twice." She clicked again. "Greta, it's not here. It's gone. It was here Tuesday night. Bernice's service picks up the audio from this folder for the transcripts. That's how the system works. Nobody else has the login."

"Nobody else?" Greta asked.

"Me. Bernice's staff. The vendor's technician if something breaks. That's all." Helen's hand went to her throat. "Oh, God. Oh, God, Greta."

"Helen," Greta said, as steadily as she could. "Check the trash."

Helen clicked a small icon at the bottom of the window. A message appeared in the middle of the screen, in a flat, polite font.

*Recycle Bin is empty. Items are removed automatically by scheduled task at 11:00 PM daily.*

"The vendor set it up that way," Helen whispered. "To save space. It's in the contract. Bernice said it was the only way to keep the drive from filling up with raw audio." She stared at the screen. "It runs every night at eleven. That means the file could have gone into the trash any time after I uploaded it, and then the job would have cleared it out at eleven on Tuesday night."

"Or on Wednesday night," Greta said.

Helen turned around in the chair and looked up at her. Her eyes were wide and wet.

"I don't know," Helen said. "I don't know when it was taken."

From the front of the building, a door opened and closed. Heavy footsteps crossed the floorboards, and then Sam Ortiz appeared in the doorway of the back room with his hat in one hand and his other hand resting on the belt that carried his radio. He looked at the two women, at the open computer, at the clipboard by the back door. He did not look surprised.

"Helen called me on the way over," he said. "She said somebody's been in the recordings." He came into the room and stood beside Greta, looking at the screen over her shoulder. "What's missing?"

"The council audio from Tuesday," Greta said. "The whole meeting. The file was on the drive on Tuesday night, and now it's not. The trash was emptied by a scheduled job, and the only people with the login are the clerk and the transcription company that Helen uses."

Sam was quiet for a long moment. He looked at the sign-in sheet on the nail, and then back at the screen.

"Did you see anybody in here Wednesday morning?" he asked Lyle, who had come to the doorway and was standing there with his cap in his hands.

"No," Lyle said. "Nobody came in but me, and I didn't come past the front desk. I went straight out to the steps."

"And the back door?"

"It was unlocked when I got there. I told you that." Lyle's voice was rough. "I don't know who unlocked it. I didn't."

"The keypad will know," Helen said. Her voice was calmer now that someone else had taken charge of the room. "Tidewater keeps the access logs. Every code that goes through that door gets written down, with the time. They send the reports to the commission every month. Dale always asked for copies." She swallowed. "He always wanted to see who came in after hours."

"Can we get them today?" Sam asked.

"Not today," Helen said. "Tidewater's office is in Traverse City, and they don't answer the phone on Thursdays. I'd have to send an email, and they'd send it back to the commission, and the commission would send it to the town manager, and the town manager is in Florida until the twenty-first."

"Then we'll do it the slow way," Sam said. He looked at Greta. "What's on your tape?"

"Everything that was said in the room," Greta said. "Including the things that didn't make it into the minutes."

"Good," Sam said. "Then I want you to read me the part about the lobby."

"The lobby isn't on the tape," Greta said. "Nobody said anything there that I could hear."

"Then that's what the tape says." Sam took out his notebook and wrote something in it. "It's the same as the voicemail. Dale heard somebody at the back say something, and then the record went missing, and the vote on the record says the opposite of what happened. I'm not a lawyer, Greta, but that sounds like a lot of coincidence to me. And I don't believe in coincidences that cost a man his life."

Greta looked at him, surprised. He did not look away.

"The clipboard," she said quietly. "Tessa Quill signed in last night at twenty-five to ten."

"I saw," Sam said. "I'll speak to her. Today."

"She's frightened of somebody."

"Everybody in this town is frightened of somebody," Sam said. He tucked the notebook back into his shirt pocket. "That's what happens when the people with money decide what the people without money get to know."

He went back out through the front office. Greta heard him speaking to Lyle in a low voice, then the sound of the front door opening and the crunch of his boots on the gravel. She stood by the desk for a moment longer, looking at the empty folder on the screen, the neat line of files ending abruptly at the seventh of July.

Then she turned back to the clipboard by the door, took out her phone, and photographed the sign-in sheet, the handwriting, and the line that read *T. Quill, 7/14, 9:35 p.m. Survey folder for G. Strand*. She photographed the blank line below it. She photographed the clock above the keypad, which read 10:52 in red numerals, and she photographed the plate beneath the keypad that said *Access logs retained 90 days*.

Ninety days. The logs would be there. Whoever had come through that door on Wednesday morning had done it with a code, and a code was a kind of signature, and a signature could be read.

Helen was still sitting at the desk, staring at the screen, when Greta came back into the room. She had the look of a woman who had just realized she had been living inside a house with a missing wall and had not noticed the wind.

"I'm going to be sick," Helen said.

"Not yet," Greta said gently. "Come outside with me. There's air out there."

Helen stood up slowly. At the door, she paused and looked back at the cabinet with its row of blinking green lights, and then at the empty space on the computer screen where her town's record of the evening had been.

"Greta," she said. "Who would want a recording gone?"

Greta thought about the tape folded into her canvas bag, and the bangles on a wrist that had been held in a fist at the foot of the Harbor Office steps, and a man in a tan linen jacket walking beside a coffee urn with his head bent low.

"Someone who wanted it to say something different," she said.

## Chapter 8: Helen's Signature

They sat in Helen's minivan with the engine running and the air conditioner on high, parked at the top of the gravel lot where the marina's tall light pole threw a long shadow across the hood. The van smelled of crayons and cherry gum, and a small plastic dinosaur lay on the dashboard beside a folded permission slip for a school band trip. Helen had not turned the radio on. She sat with both hands on the wheel, looking at the bay through the windshield, as though she were still waiting for the light to change.

"I need to tell you what happened," she said. "All of it. Before Sam comes back, because he'll ask me, and I want to have said it once to someone who isn't a deputy."

"Tell me," Greta said.

Helen took a long breath. "Tuesday night, I got home from the Center at nine-thirty. Frank was already in bed. I made tea and sat at the kitchen table to finish the agenda for the special session. My phone was on the table. At eleven-thirty, it buzzed." She looked down at her hands. "It was Bernice. She texted me a link to the draft minutes. She said, *Expedited draft for approval tonight. Gordon's asked for them tonight, and I'd like to get them to the state office before Friday.* She said the audio was clean and the vote was in the record. And I opened the link, and it was a PDF, and I scrolled down to the signature block, and I approved it."

"You didn't read it."

"I didn't read all of it," Helen said. "I read the header. I read the attendance. I read the vote, because the vote is always the part people want to see. It said *carried, three to two*, and I thought, *well, that's a relief, Ted finally made up his mind.* And I put my name on it." She laughed without humor. "I thought I was helping. I thought the council had decided. I was so tired, Greta. I've been clerk for eleven years, and I've never once approved minutes that I didn't check line by line. But I didn't, that night. I just pressed the button."

"What time did you approve it?"

"Eleven fifty-two." Helen did not hesitate. "I looked at the clock on the stove when I hit the button. I thought, *there, it's done, go to bed.* And I did."

Greta looked at the bay. A cabin cruiser was motoring slowly along the far breakwater, its white wake spreading behind it in neat arcs. Somewhere a dog was barking at the water.

"Did you talk to Dale on Tuesday?" Greta asked.

Helen's face changed. "Yes. Yes, that afternoon. He came into the office at four, before the meeting. He wanted a copy of the audio. He asked me for the key to the TownShare drive, the one the commission keeps for the public records requests. I told him he could have the recording once the minutes were approved, the way the procedure says. He got very angry. He said, *Helen, you don't approve a record. You just type it.*" She swallowed hard. "I told him I'd send him the audio the next morning, when the transcripts were done. And he said, *Then I'll get it myself*, and he walked out."

"And then he called me."

"I didn't know that," Helen said. "I didn't know he'd called anybody."

"He did," Greta said. "At nine forty-one. He wanted me to come to the Harbor Office at seven. He said he'd heard somebody in the lobby say the record would be cleaned up by morning."

Helen closed her eyes. "Oh, Dale," she whispered.

They sat in silence for a while. Then Helen opened her eyes, reached down beside her seat for a leather folder, and opened it on her knees.

"There's something else," she said. "I didn't want to show it to anyone yet. I was going to take it to the town manager when he gets back, and I was going to let him decide what to do with it, because I didn't know what it meant." She pulled out a sheaf of printed invoices and flipped through them until she found the one she wanted. "This came in yesterday afternoon, dated Wednesday morning. It's from Oakes Transcription, for the June meetings and the July 14 regular session. The usual charges. But there's an extra line at the bottom."

She handed the page to Greta. Greta read it slowly.

*Expedited correction and re-verification, Council Regular Meeting, July 14. 4.5 hours @ $185/hr. Total $832.50.*

And beneath that, in smaller type, a second line that someone had added with a different font:

*Purchase order: Strand Lakeside Partners, per G. Strand. Billed to Town of Lantern Bay, Account 4417. Please remit to Oakes Transcription.*

Greta looked at the words for a long time.

"The town is paying for the correction," she said slowly. "But the purchase order says it's Strand's."

"That's what I thought," Helen said. "I thought it was a clerical error. Somebody in the finance office typed the wrong name. I was going to call them, and then Gordon's lawyer called, and then Dale died, and I haven't called anyone." She pressed her lips together. "The total isn't much. It's not what people would call a bribe. But Greta, why would a developer pay a transcriptionist to correct the minutes of a public meeting? Why would it be billed to the town under his name?"

"Because the correction wasn't for the town," Greta said. "It was for him."

Helen made a small, wounded sound, and turned her face toward the window.

"There's more," Greta said. "The line that Dale supposedly said during the recess. The line that the minutes say he withdrew his objection. It isn't on my tape. Nobody said it in the room. I was there. I was listening. I wrote down every word from the moment the meeting came to order until Ruth said goodnight, and there's no withdrawal from Dale Whitcomb anywhere on that paper."

"I believe you."

"And the vote," Greta said. "Carried, three to two. There was no motion to approve the lease. Ruth moved to postpone to the twenty-seventh. Everyone agreed. Ted Vance asked a question about the survey and went back to his pens. You can see it on the tape in my own handwriting."

Helen was crying quietly now, without making any sound, her shoulders very still. "I know," she whispered. "I know. I know."

"Did Bernice ever say why she needed to re-verify the audio?" Greta asked gently.

Helen wiped her face with the back of her hand. "She said there was a problem with the recording. A hum on the line from the back of the room, and some noise near the lobby door, and she needed to rebuild a few lines of the speaker record so the vote would be clear. She said she'd do it cleanly and nobody would know the difference." Her mouth twisted. "She said it was common practice for transcripts. She said the record was the thing people remembered, not the room."

"There was no hum," Greta said. "I was sitting six feet from the speakers. The room was as quiet as a church for the whole meeting, except for the coffee urn and Ted Vance rustling his papers."

"I know that now," Helen said.

"Did Bernice say where she'd put the original audio, after she'd rebuilt it?"

"No." Helen shook her head. "She said she'd replace it on the drive with the corrected version as soon as she'd finished. I told her to keep the original, too, just in case. I told her that's what the procedure requires." She looked up at Greta with raw, pleading eyes. "She said she would. Greta, I told her to keep it."

Greta said nothing for a moment. She thought about the empty folder on the screen and the trash bin that had been emptied by a scheduled task, and a vendor's login that only the clerk and the transcription service could use.

"I believe you told her," Greta said. "I don't think she kept it."

Helen closed the folder and put it on the seat beside her. Then she reached over and took Greta's hand, quickly, and let it go again.

"I'm going to go to the town manager as soon as he's home," she said. "I'm going to tell him everything. The approval, the time, the invoice, the calls from the lawyer. I'm going to resign, if they want me to. I deserve that much."

"You don't deserve to be fired for being tired," Greta said. "You deserve to be fired for not reading the minutes, and you'll have to live with that. But you don't have to disappear, and you shouldn't. The people in this town need a clerk who will tell them the truth about what was said in the room."

Helen gave a small, unsteady laugh. "You sound like Dale."

"I'd rather not," Greta said.

"No," Helen said. "I don't suppose you would."

Outside, Sam Ortiz came back around the corner of the Harbor Office, and his eyes went at once to the minivan. He walked over, opened the passenger door without asking, and leaned in with one hand on the roof. His face was tight and unreadable.

"Tidewater's office is closed till Monday," he said. "I called anyway. The lady on the answering machine said she'd check the access logs for the county if they sent a request. That's another three days." He looked at Helen. "Mrs. Marsh, I need to know whether the keypad code for the back door is the same one the transcription company uses for after-hours uploads. Do you know?"

Helen hesitated. "I think so," she said. "The vendor has a code for the drive. I don't know the number."

"Find out," Sam said. "Today, if you can."

"I'll try."

Sam turned his head and looked at Greta. "And you. You're not going to like this."

"I've already decided I won't," Greta said.

"Tessa Quill's car is parked outside Strand's office on Pier Street," Sam said. "She's been sitting in it for twenty minutes with the engine off. I walked past her twice. She didn't see me." He paused. "She's holding a folder, Greta. She's holding it like it might bite her."

Greta reached for the door handle. "Then I'd better go and see what's in it."

"I'd better come with you," Sam said.

"No," Greta said. "She won't talk to a deputy. She'll talk to me. We've known each other since she was in high school. Her mother used to bring her to the courthouse library and make her sit quietly with a book while I took depositions."

Sam studied her face for a long moment, then nodded once. "Fifteen minutes," he said. "Then I'm coming in whether she likes it or not."

Greta stepped out onto the gravel. The heat rose off the lot in shimmering waves, and the bay beyond the boat yard was bright as a blade. Behind her, Helen's minivan idled quietly, and the little plastic dinosaur on the dashboard caught the sun and glittered faintly green.

## Chapter 9: Tessa's Photograph

Strand Lakeside Partners occupied what had once been the Bait and Tackle, a long, low building at the foot of Pier Street that had been painted white and fitted with tall glass windows that looked out over the north basin. The old fish sign still hung above the door, its paint faded to a dull brown, and somebody had added a small brass plate beneath it that read *Strand Lakeside — Harbor Point Development — Inquiries Welcome*. The parking lot was nearly empty. The only car parked in the shade of the hedge was a silver sedan with a crumpled bumper and a child's booster seat in the back, and Tessa Quill sat behind its wheel with both hands clenched on the top of the steering column and her forehead resting against them.

Greta walked across the lot slowly so that the crunch of the shell would not startle her. She stopped beside the driver's window and waited. After a moment, Tessa lifted her head and looked at her. Her eyes were red-rimmed and enormous.

"I saw you on the steps yesterday," Tessa said, her voice muffled through the glass. "And at the café. You asked me if I was all right."

"I did," Greta said. "I still want to know."

Tessa's mouth worked silently for a while. Then she reached for the door handle and pushed it open, and the car door groaned outward on its hinges.

"Hurry in," Tessa said. "Please. He can see the front window from his office."

Greta got into the passenger seat. The car smelled of vanilla air freshener and something sweeter underneath, probably the juice box that was rolling back and forth in the footwell. A folder lay on the console between them, a thin green cardboard folder with a clasp, held closed with a rubber band. Tessa put one hand flat on top of it.

"Gordon asked me to do something," she said. "On Monday. I didn't do it. And then on Tuesday he asked me to do something else, and I did that, and I hate myself for it." She drew a shaky breath. "Greta, I don't know what I'm supposed to say."

"Explain what happened," Greta said. "Start at the beginning."

Tessa looked at the folder under her hand. "The survey," she said. "The one Gordon used at the meeting. It was signed by the land surveyor on July second. The comment period on the lease closed on June thirtieth. Gordon knew that. The surveyor told him in writing on July first, when he asked for a rush job, that the survey wouldn't be finished in time to count. He said he'd fix it." She swallowed. "Gordon told me to fix it. He said the surveyor's office would send a corrected copy with a June date on the stamp, and I was supposed to swap it for the one in the commission's file and say nothing. Monday afternoon. I said no. I told him I wouldn't do it."

"What did he say?"

"He said, *Then Bernice will do it.*" Tessa's voice shook. "He said it so calmly, as if he were ordering lunch. And he said, *Bernice knows how to make a record say what it needs to say, Tessa. That's what she's paid for.*"

Greta said nothing. She let the silence sit between them, the way she had in a hundred depositions, and watched Tessa's face.

"On Tuesday morning, Bernice came into the office," Tessa said. "She had an envelope. Gordon took her into his room and shut the door, and I could hear them talking through the wall, but I couldn't make out the words. Then she came out with the envelope gone and Gordon came out after her and said, *Tessa, I need the survey folder from the Harbor Office tonight. The original. Go get it and bring it here. Do not let anyone see you.*" She looked at Greta. "So I went."

"At nine-thirty-five," Greta said.

"At nine-thirty-five. I signed the book. I know I shouldn't have, but I didn't think anybody would look at it." Tessa's hand tightened on the folder. "I got the survey from the commission's cabinet. The original, with the stamp and the signature and the July second date. And I took out my phone and I took pictures of every page. Every single page, Greta, starting at twenty to ten, with the timestamp turned on. I don't know why I did it. I just knew that if I didn't, somebody was going to make it disappear."

"And then?"

"I drove here," Tessa said. "It was after ten. I gave Gordon the folder in his office. He didn't look at it. He just said, *Good girl*, and put it in his desk drawer, and then he went out to the lobby, where Bernice was waiting for him. Her white car was parked behind the building. I could see them through the glass." She pressed her lips together. "I didn't hear what they said. But I saw her laugh."

Greta looked out the windshield at the long white building and the tall windows, and the shadow of the hedge falling across the shell. She thought about a woman with bangles on her wrist, laughing in a lobby.

"Tessa," she said quietly. "Do you know what Gordon was going to tell Bernice to do in the minutes?"

Tessa was silent for a long time. Then she nodded, once, without looking up.

"He told me on Tuesday afternoon, before the meeting, when we were driving back from the Gull," she said. "He said, *If Dale says he's withdrawn his objection, the state won't ask any questions about the survey date. The lease will look settled. Nobody will want to reopen the comment period for a man who already agreed.* He laughed. He said, *Dale Whitcomb has never withdrawn anything in his life, which is exactly why it'll look so convincing.*" Her voice cracked. "I thought he was joking. I didn't think he meant it. I didn't think anybody would actually write it down."

"They did," Greta said.

"I know." Tessa's face crumpled. "I know that now. And Dale's dead, and I was in that building with a folder in my hand, and I didn't even knock on his door."

Greta reached across and laid her hand gently on Tessa's arm.

"Show me the photographs," she said.

Tessa wiped her face with the sleeve of her green cardigan and turned the phone toward her. She unlocked it with a shaking thumb, scrolled through a long list of images that all looked nearly identical, each one a page of cream-colored paper with a neat black heading and a red seal in one corner, and stopped on the fourth from the bottom. The timestamp in the corner read *7/14 9:52 PM*.

It was a survey plat. A drawing of the north basin, with the shoreline in careful blue, the proposed boardwalk marked in dotted lines, and a legend in the bottom corner. Beneath the drawing was a signature block with a flourish of black ink, a surveyor's seal, and a date typed in a plain font: *July 2, 2026*. Beside the date was a stamp, a small rectangular box that read *RECEIVED — HARBOR COMMISSION*. The date in the stamp was smudged, but Greta could read it.

*JUL 2 2026.*

She looked at it for a long time.

"That's the commission's stamp," Tessa said. "It's not the one Gordon wanted. He wanted a June stamp. He had the surveyor's office send a new copy with the stamp already changed, and the surveyor's assistant emailed it to him on Monday night. I have that too." She swiped to the next photo, and the next. A plain email, sent from a shared mailbox, with an attached PDF whose stamp read *JUN 28 2026*. "Gordon forwarded it to me. He said, *Use this one.* I didn't."

Greta stared at the two images, side by side on the cracked screen.

"Tessa," she said. "Are these yours to show me?"

"They're mine," Tessa said. "They're the only thing I have that I did right."

"Then I'm going to need copies," Greta said. "And I'm going to need you to give them to Deputy Ortiz, before Gordon's lawyer gets to the town. Today. Not tomorrow."

Tessa looked up, her face white. "He'll fire me."

"He'll try," Greta said. "I think he'll have a harder time than he expects."

Tessa was silent, her fingers hovering over the screen.

"Malcolm Pryce is coming to town on Friday," she said at last. "Gordon's lawyer, from Grand Rapids. He's going to meet with Ruth Kalinowski and Ted Vance at the Lakeshore Inn on Friday night, before the funeral. Gordon told me to make the reservations." She drew a breath. "I have the confirmation email on my phone. It says the meeting is in the private dining room. Please don't tell him I told you."

Greta looked at the young woman beside her, with her cardigan too big across the shoulders and her hands shaking on the phone, and felt a sudden wave of pity so strong it almost hurt.

"I won't tell him," she said. "But Tessa, you have to tell the truth to Sam. All of it. Including the envelope, and the laugh in the lobby, and the folder in the drawer. He's going to ask you the same questions I asked, and he's going to write down the answers, and then those answers are going to matter a great deal more than your job."

Tessa nodded slowly. Then she looked down at the green folder under her hand, opened the clasp, and lifted out a single sheet of paper. It was the original survey plat, with its real stamp and its real date. She had kept it in the folder all along, folded in quarters. She held it out to Greta with both hands, as carefully as if it were a letter from someone who had died.

"Take it," Tessa said. "Take it to the deputy. Please. I'm so tired of holding it."

Greta took the page. It was smooth and heavy and smelled faintly of toner. She folded it again along the same creases and slipped it into the inside pocket of her cardigan, where it lay against her ribs like a second, quieter heartbeat.

At the top of the lot, the door of the Bait and Tackle opened, and a tall, lean figure in a tan linen jacket stepped out onto the white porch. He stood with his hands in his pockets, looking down at the silver sedan with its crumpled bumper, and his face was perfectly calm.

Tessa made a small, strangled noise in her throat.

"Don't look at him," Greta said quietly. "Look at me. Tell me one more thing about the envelope. What color was it?"

Tessa swallowed, and forced her eyes away from the porch, and said, in a trembling voice, "Brown. Manila. The kind you buy in a box of fifty at the drugstore."

"Thank you," Greta said. "That's the kind of detail a jury remembers."

Gordon Strand came down the porch steps one at a time, unhurried, and walked toward the car. He did not look angry. He looked faintly puzzled, as if he had just noticed a stray cat sitting on his doorstep and could not decide whether it belonged to anyone.

"Tessa," he called pleasantly. "Are you coming back in, or is it going to be one of those mornings?"

Greta opened the car door, slid out onto the shell, and turned to face him.

"Gordon," she said. "I'd like to talk to you about the minutes."

For the first time since she had known him, Gordon Strand's smile slipped entirely off his face. Behind him, through the tall glass windows of the Bait and Tackle, Greta saw a woman in a black cardigan standing at the reception desk, holding a cup of coffee in both hands. She was looking straight out through the glass, and her left wrist, bare of bangles, rested very still against the edge of the counter.


## Chapter 10: Paper Trail Again

Greta gave Sam the photographs that evening, on the porch of the lake house, with the sun going down behind the pines and the bay turning from silver to pewter to a deep, quiet blue. Tessa had sent them to her phone from the parking lot of the Bait and Tackle, one at a time, while Gordon Strand stood on his porch with his arms folded and watched them both with an expression of growing irritation. Gordon Strand had not answered her question on the shell. He had only looked at her for a long moment, then said, "Talk to my lawyer, Greta," and gone back up the porch steps with Tessa's name still hanging in the air between them. Greta had forwarded the photographs to Sam from the car. He had called back in four minutes.

"I'm coming out," he had said. "Don't go anywhere."

He arrived at a quarter to eight in his own pickup, not the county car, and sat on the top step beside her with a can of root beer he did not open. He looked at the photographs on his phone for a long time without speaking. He looked at the one of the survey plat with its July stamp, and then the one of the email with the June stamp, and then the one with the timestamp in the corner, *7/14 9:22 PM*.

"She was in the Harbor Office at nine-thirty-five," he said finally.

"And she was out again before ten," Greta said. "She drove to the Bait and Tackle and handed the folder to Gordon. Tessa says he put it in his desk drawer and said *good girl*."

"Tessa will say that to me herself," Sam said. "On the record. With her lawyer, if she wants one." He set the phone face down on the step beside him. "This is the first thing I've had in two days that isn't a heart attack and a wet staircase, Greta. I don't know whether to be grateful or sick."

"Be both," Greta said. "I was."

He smiled faintly and rubbed his face with one hand.

"Corliss wants the file closed by Monday," he said. "He's been asking me every hour whether the ME has signed off. I told him she's still waiting on the toxicology report and the fall analysis. He said, *That's a week, Ortiz, not a month.*" He shook his head. "I don't think he's going to like what I'm going to bring him."

"What are you going to bring him?"

"A photograph of a survey with a stamp that doesn't match the date, a sign-in sheet that says Tessa Quill was in a locked building the night before a man died, and an audio file that's been deleted from a town server by the one vendor the clerk trusted." He looked out at the bay. "And an honest-to-God voicemail from the victim saying he heard somebody plan to clean up the record. That's not a murder case, Greta. Not yet. But it's the kind of thing that makes a sheriff reach for his reading glasses."

"Then give him the tape, too," Greta said. "I'll make you a copy tonight."

"Do it," Sam said. "But I want the original, Greta. I want it in an evidence bag by morning, with your initials on the seal."

"You'll have it."

He nodded, stood up, and brushed the seat of his pants. At the bottom of the steps he paused and looked back at her.

"You weren't in the lobby," he said. "Were you?"

"No."

"Good. Then you're the only person in that building who can say what was in the room and what wasn't." He turned toward his pickup. "Make sure you keep it that way."

When his taillights had disappeared down the gravel, Greta went inside, made herself a cup of tea she did not want, and sat down at the desk in the spare room with the tape spread out in front of her under the lamp. Judge climbed onto the bench, turned around three times, and lay down on the quilt with his head resting on his front paws, watching her with the air of a cat who has been promised a long night and intends to hold the author to it.

Greta began to read the tape from the beginning. Not the minutes. The tape.

It was a different kind of reading. The minutes had been written by someone who had weighed each sentence and decided what to keep. The tape had been written by no one at all. It was simply the record of what had happened in the room, as it happened, in the order it happened, with every stumble and repetition and false start. Greta had spent her life reading tapes that way, the way a surgeon might read the X-ray of a broken arm, looking for the places where the bone had gone wrong.

Her own shorthand came back to her in fragments as she went. The short forms she had built over thirty years, the strokes that stood for *the council* and *the motion* and *carried*, each one a small private habit that had become as natural as breath. She read through Ruth's call to order. She read Helen's roll. She read the sidewalk benches, and Carla Sayer's long, cheerful speech, and Ted Vance's three confused questions, each one tangled in the one before it, so that the reader could hear him searching for the door in the wall.

At item seven she slowed down.

*Strand, Lakeside, sixty units, forty years.* Gordon's speech. Greta could hear him now as clearly as she had at the meeting, the warm, practiced cadence of a man who had given a great many presentations and enjoyed every one. *The survey confirms everything we have told this town from the beginning.* She read that line and frowned. It was the sort of thing a developer said when he had decided the survey was going to carry the day, and wanted the room to know it.

Then Dale. *This town gave its harbor to the families who built it.* She read his speech all the way through, every sentence, the sharp corners and the old-fashioned turns of phrase, the way a man talks when he has been talking to the same people about the same water for forty years and has stopped caring whether they like it. *I'm going to say my piece, and then Ruth can tell me I've had my turn.* That was Dale. Nobody else in Lantern Bay had ever talked that way, and nobody else would have wanted to.

*I want somebody to explain to me how a survey signed after the comment period closed gets to be the reason we vote on Tuesday night.*

Greta read the words aloud, quietly, in the empty room. They sounded exactly like him.

She turned the page. The recess was in the middle of it, the gap in the stroke-marks where the room had emptied out and the coffee urn had gurgled and the people had gone out to the lobby. Her own time mark sat there, in the margin. *8:50.* And below it, nothing.

She had typed no word between 8:50 and 9:05. She had typed no word because nothing had been said in the room to type. She knew that, and she had known it at the table, and she had known it again when she read the minutes at the library. But the tape gave her the thing the minutes could not. It gave her the blank space itself, a stretch of paper a little more than fourteen minutes long, with nothing on it at all, like a page left open on purpose.

The minutes had filled that space. They had put a sentence into the gap, at eight fifty-two, in Dale's name, in a voice that was not his.

Greta sat back and closed her eyes.

The tape was not a sound recording. It had no voices and no hum and no rustle of papers. But a court reporter learned, after enough years, to hear the rhythm of a room through the way the strokes came down on the keys. A pause before a difficult name. A stumble when two people spoke at once. A slowing when someone leaned close to a microphone to be sure of a word. The tape remembered those things, in a way the minutes could not, because the minutes had been written after the fact by someone who had never sat in the room.

She opened her eyes and looked at the page again, and then at her notes in the margin.

*9:05, resumed.* And below it, a line she had written without any thought at all, the way she wrote everything when the room was still going on: *Mrs. Oakes back, row 4, bangles.*

Greta had typed that line herself. She had typed *bangles* because it was the only thing about Bernice Oakes that had caught her attention in the room that night, the one sound that had broken through the wall of her concentration. She had not thought about it since. But the words were there now, in her own careful shorthand, in the place where the tape said the meeting had come back into session, and they could not be disputed by anyone. Bernice had been in the room at nine-oh-five.

Bernice had said, to Greta, at the Harbor Office lot, *I was home by nine-thirty.*

Greta looked at the line for a long time.

Then she picked up her pencil and began to do what she had been trained to do in the first year of her career, long before she had ever learned to love a courtroom. She took a fresh sheet of yellow legal paper and began to write out the corrected record by hand, line by line, from the tape, with the time marks in the margin and the speakers' names in the left column. She did not try to make it pretty. She did not try to make it smooth. She simply wrote down what the tape said, in plain English, the way the room had sounded.

It took her until nearly two in the morning. Judge slept through all of it, curled on the quilt with his tail over his nose. At one point he woke, stretched his legs out, and walked slowly across the bench, stepping on the back of her hand as he passed, and she did not even look up.

At the bottom of the last page, under the time mark for *9:14, adjourned*, Greta wrote a single line, in capitals, the way she would have written it for a judge:

*NO MOTION TO APPROVE THE LEASE WAS MADE. NO VOTE WAS TAKEN. THE COUNCIL POSTPONED ITEM 7 TO JULY 27 BY A VOTE OF 5 TO 0. COMMISSIONER WHITCOMB DID NOT SPEAK AFTER RECESS.*

She signed it, dated it, and then sat looking at the signature for a long moment, as though it belonged to someone else.

## Chapter 11: A Sentence Never Spoken

Priya came over on Friday morning with two cups of coffee from the Gull, a box of lemon bars from Frances, and a paperback copy of an old Agatha Christie novel with a cracked spine that she set on the kitchen table like a gift. Judge sniffed the bookmark, decided it was not edible, and went to sit in the sunny window with his back to them both.

"I want to hear the sentence," Priya said, sitting down. "The one Dale never said. Read it to me."

Greta had been up since six. She had slept for three hours on the couch with the lamp still on, and she had woken with the tape in her lap and the taste of cold tea in her mouth. She picked up her printed copy of the draft minutes, folded to item seven, and turned it so Priya could see the line.

"*The survey satisfies my concerns,*" Greta read. "*I withdraw my objection to the lease and ask that the council proceed to a vote.*"

Priya was quiet for a moment. Then she laughed, short and surprised, the way people laugh when something is so wrong it becomes almost funny.

"*Ask that the council proceed,*" she repeated. "Greta, Dale Whitcomb never in his life asked anybody to proceed. He told them to get on with it. He said *let's go*. He said *we're wasting daylight*. He said *Ruth, for God's sake, call the vote*. I sat through nine years of his speeches from the gallery, and I'd know his voice in a crowd of a thousand." She tapped the page with one finger. "That isn't his sentence. It's a lawyer's sentence. It's the kind of sentence somebody writes when they're trying to sound like a man who's agreeing to something he doesn't want to agree to."

"That's what I thought," Greta said.

"What did he actually say? At the end?"

"Not a thing," Greta said. "He didn't say a word after the recess. He came back into the room, sat down, folded his hands on the table, and stayed silent until Ruth gaveled the meeting closed. He didn't look at Gordon Strand once. He looked at his hands the whole time, like he was trying not to hit someone." She set the paper down. "And then he came to my table, and he asked me whether my machine lied."

Priya's face went soft and sad. "He would ask that," she said quietly. "He used to ask it of everybody who ever wrote anything down in Lantern Bay. He thought the town was a bad sentence that somebody had decided to read aloud as a good one. He wanted a record that couldn't be bent."

"He got one," Greta said. "And somebody bent it anyway."

They were silent for a while. Outside, a pair of ravens landed on the railing of the porch, pecked at each other briefly, and flew off again toward the bluff.

Greta stood up and went to the bookshelf beside the fireplace. The shelf held the paperbacks she could never bear to give away, and behind them, in a flat cardboard box that had once held a box of legal pads, she kept a stack of old transcripts from the Carrow County courthouse. Most of them were from her own years at the bench, but a few were from before her time, copies that a retired judge had given her as a kind of wedding gift when she first started. She pulled out a bundle of them, held together with a red rubber band, and carried it back to the table.

"These are from the old courthouse," she said. "Other reporters' work. Some of it is very good. Some of it is not."

"Why do you have them?"

"Because I learned from them," Greta said. "Every reporter has a voice on the page, Priya. You can tell who wrote a transcript the way you can tell who wrote a letter. The rhythm, the phrasing, the way they handle a difficult speaker. I used to keep a list of the ones I admired and the ones I wanted to avoid." She untangled the rubber band and laid the top transcript on the table. "Bernice Oakes was the best of the people I worked beside. She was fast and she was exact. But she had a habit, from her first year in the courthouse. She liked to write *the record reflects* when she wanted to smooth something over. Judges hate that phrase. They say it sounds like a clerk talking about his own feelings."

Priya leaned forward, her coffee forgotten. "And the minutes say *the record reflects*?"

Greta opened the printed draft to the recess, and ran her finger down the page, and stopped. There it was, in the paragraph beneath the eight fifty-two line, in the neat type of the official document. *The record reflects that Commissioner Whitcomb stated the following, which the council received before returning to session.*

"*Which the council received*," Priya read slowly. "That's not how anybody talks."

"It's how Bernice writes," Greta said. "And it's how Bernice wrote for the courthouse for twenty years. She never once said *which the court received* in any transcript I ever read. But she did say *the record reflects*. Every time." She turned to the old transcripts and opened one at random, a dull probate hearing from the year the courthouse had replaced its carpets. There, in a sentence near the top of the second page, in a stenographer's neatly typed prose, was the same phrase: *The record reflects that counsel approached the bench.*

Priya stared at the page. Then she looked back at the draft.

"It's the same person," she said, very quietly.

"It's the same habit," Greta said. "That's not proof. A court can't convict on a verbal tic. But Greta Lowe can write down what she heard, and what she heard was a man saying things in his own voice, and what the clerk typed was a voice that wasn't his, in a sentence Bernice Oakes would have written." She sat down again. "And there's something else."

"What?"

"The time," Greta said. "The minutes put the withdrawal at eight fifty-two. The recess began at eight-fifty. Ruth called it, I wrote it down, and Ted Vance went out to the stairs with Carla. But the sentence says the council *received* Dale's statement *before returning to session*. Which means the council must have been in session when Dale spoke. Which means the recess wasn't a recess at all, not in the way the minutes describe it. The clerk, or the transcriptionist, or whoever typed the sentence, didn't know what the room was doing at that minute. She just knew where to put the words."

"She wasn't there," Priya said.

"She was there," Greta said. "She was in the lobby. Just not the way she told Sam."

Priya was silent for a long moment. Then she reached over and put her hand on Greta's wrist, lightly, the way she had done on a dozen occasions over the years when Greta was too tired to notice how much she was hurting.

"You know what this sounds like, don't you," Priya said. "It sounds like something a sheriff would call a *theory*. It sounds like a lot of stuff about commas and phrasing. Ruth's going to laugh you out of the Center."

"Ruth won't be laughing," Greta said. "She's been sitting on that dais for eleven years with a clerk who types what she's told. She knows exactly how the minutes are made. She's just never had to think about what the minutes say."

Priya squeezed her wrist and let go. "So what are you going to do?"

"I'm going to listen to the room," Greta said. "What's left of it." She picked up the printed minutes and folded them, slowly, in half. "I'm going to ask Helen for the audio again, and when she says she doesn't have it, I'm going to ask her who else in the world could have heard that recess."

"And who's that?"

"Anyone who was standing in the lobby with a cup of coffee," Greta said. "And anyone who was listening through the door."

Priya got up, took her coffee cup to the sink, and rinsed it out slowly with her back to Greta. When she turned around, her face was set in the stubborn, fierce lines that Greta had seen once or twice before, always in the bookshop, usually when a customer had tried to buy the last copy of something rare.

"Then take me with you," Priya said. "I'm not going to sit at the shop and watch you do it alone. I was in the gallery. I saw the coffee urn and I saw who went out to it. And I know how Dale talked when he was angry, which is more than Sam Ortiz will ever know."

Greta looked up at her.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me. I've got three hundred paperbacks to put on the shelves and a book club that thinks Agatha Christie is a sensible choice for a summer read." Priya grinned, wiping her hands on a dish towel. "You owe me lemon bars, Greta Lowe. Hold the box."

## Chapter 12: The Hearing Notice

The notice went up on the town's website at nine o'clock on Friday morning, under a banner with the Lantern Bay seal and the words *SPECIAL SESSION*. Greta found it in the same place she had found the draft minutes, on the library's slow computer, with Joan Cho standing behind her chair and reading over her shoulder without apology.

*The Lantern Bay Town Council will hold a special session on Monday, July 27, at 7:00 p.m., in the council chamber at Harbor Center, 14 Front Street. The purpose of the session is (1) to consider the record of the regular meeting of July 14 and the draft minutes prepared from it, and (2) to take action on the request of Strand Lakeside Partners for a forty-year lease of the north basin of the harbor, as provided in item 7 of the July 14 agenda. Public comment will be received. The meeting is open to the public.*

"They're going to vote," Joan said, in the flat, cautious voice of a librarian who did not want to be quoted. "On the twenty-seventh. Just as before."

"Just as before," Greta said.

"And what about the minutes? They're going to be approved?"

"That depends on what the room says," Greta said.

Joan looked at the notice a moment longer. Then she tapped the words *to consider the record* with one finger, and said quietly, "So. That's something, at least."

Greta printed the notice, folded it into her bag, and thanked her. She could feel the fine dust of the library's carpet clinging to the soles of her shoes as she walked out into the heat. The street outside was busier than it had been on Wednesday. People stood in small groups on the sidewalk, talking in low voices, glancing up toward the Harbor Center's tall brick windows. A car went by slowly, its driver peering out at the crowd with a camera on the dashboard. Somebody had hung a hand-lettered sign in the window of the hardware store that read *DALE WHITCOMB, 1955–2026, WE WILL MISS YOUR TEMPER*, and beneath it, in smaller letters, *Funeral Saturday, Our Lady of the Harbor, 10 a.m.*

Greta stood on the sidewalk and read the sign twice.

At the far end of the block, a black sedan had pulled up in front of the Lakeshore Inn. A tall man in a gray suit was getting out of the passenger side with a leather briefcase tucked under one arm, looking up at the facade of the inn as if he were pricing it. Beside him, a smaller figure in a gray dress hurried around the front of the car and took his arm, and Greta recognized the cut of the dress, and the swift, practiced way the woman turned her head to scan the street. It was Eleanor Strand. She was not looking at the Harbor Center. She was looking directly at Greta, and when she saw that Greta had seen her, she smiled, very brightly, and turned away.

Malcolm Pryce, Greta thought. From Grand Rapids. Tessa had said the private dining room. She had not said it would be the lawyer's wife who came to walk him through the door.

She walked to her car without hurrying, got in, and sat for a moment with the air conditioner blowing on her face.

Her phone buzzed. It was Sam.

"Corliss's gotten the photographs," he said without preamble. "He called me at seven this morning, shouting, and then he called me again at eight to say he'd talked to the county prosecutor's office, and then he called me a third time to say he wanted to know why I'd spent a whole night on a survey plat and a lunch meeting in Grand Rapids. I told him it was a stamp, Greta. A stamp that doesn't match a date."

"What did he say?"

"He said a stamp is a stamp, and the state doesn't reopen a lease over a smudge on a sheet of paper. He said the town attorney will handle the survey question at the special session, and that if Dale Whitcomb had a complaint about a survey, he should have brought it to the commission in June, before he died." Sam sighed. "And then he said, *But I'll tell you what, Ortiz. If the ME comes back with anything other than a fall, I want to hear about it first.*"

"That's something," Greta said.

"It's not much," Sam said. "But it's more than he gave me on Wednesday." He paused. "Greta, I got the toxicology back from the lab in Lansing this morning. Dana Reyes called me at home. She wanted me to know before it went in the report. The heart pills were there, the usual dose, nothing unusual. But the fall analysis is still pending. And she found something on the upper arms during the autopsy." He hesitated. "Bruising. Two marks on each arm, about this far apart." He lifted his hand, and Greta could almost see the gesture through the phone, a grip tightening at the wrist. "She said it's consistent with somebody holding him hard. Not with a man catching himself on a railing."

Greta closed her eyes.

"I know what you're thinking," Sam said quietly. "I'm thinking it too. But Dana said she can't say for certain it was a grip. She said it could have been the stair rail, or a fall against the wall, or a dozen other things. She put it in the file as *bruising consistent with force, mechanism undetermined*. And she told me to tell nobody until she's finished the analysis."

"Then I won't," Greta said.

"You're going to tell Priya."

"I'm going to tell Priya I'm going to a public meeting on Monday night," Greta said. "And that I'd like her to come with me."

Sam was silent for a moment. Then he said, in a voice that was much gentler than before, "Be careful, Greta. The people who wanted that recording gone are going to be in that room. And they're going to be looking at you."

"I know," she said. "I'm counting on it."

She ended the call and sat looking at the sign in the hardware store window across the street, with its cheerful, bitter words. *WE WILL MISS YOUR TEMPER.* Somewhere beyond the harbor a horn sounded twice, long and low, and a gull answered it.

Her phone buzzed again, a few minutes later, while she was still sitting in the car. This time it was an email, from an address she knew well, though she had not seen it in nearly two years. It came from a domain she recognized at once, the small private firm in Fennick Falls that had been, for the better part of a decade, her most dogged professional rival.

*Dear Ms. Lowe,*

*Oakes Transcription has been retained by the Town of Lantern Bay to prepare the verified transcript of the July 14 regular meeting. We understand that the town's record has been the subject of some public concern, and we want to assist in any way we can.*

*We would welcome the opportunity to meet with you, at your convenience, to review the audio and our transcript together. We believe that a careful, independent review would reassure the council and the public. We have attached a PDF copy of the verified transcript for your reference.*

*We look forward to hearing from you.*

*Bernice Oakes, Certified Shorthand Reporter (CSR)*
*Oakes Transcription, Fennick Falls*

Greta read the email twice. Then she opened the attachment.

It was a clean, professional document, nine pages long, bound with a cover sheet and a seal. It had a header that read *Verified Transcript — Lantern Bay Town Council, Regular Meeting, July 14*, and a line beneath that said *Prepared from the official audio recording of the meeting, reviewed for accuracy, and certified by the reporter.* Greta scrolled to item seven and read the recess. Her eyes moved slowly down the page, and then stopped on the sentence she had been waiting for, neatly typed in the middle of the page, exactly as the draft minutes had said.

*The record reflects that Commissioner Whitcomb stated the following, which the council received before returning to session.*

The transcript was certified. The reporter's signature was there, in careful blue ink, above a line that said *Date: July 17*. Two days after the meeting. Two days after Dale died.

Greta set the phone down on the passenger seat and stared out through the windshield at the sign in the hardware store window, and at the harbor beyond it, where the masts of the sailboats swayed gently in the afternoon wind.

Bernice had done it again. She had typed the sentence, certified it, and sent it out into the world with her name at the bottom, and then she had written to Greta Lowe, politely, in the same voice she had used for twenty years in the courthouse, and offered to help her see the record more clearly.

It was not an apology. It was not a warning, exactly. It was an invitation, and it had been sent on a Friday afternoon, when most people in Lantern Bay were thinking about funerals and boats and the weather.

Greta picked the phone up again and typed a single line in reply.

*I'll come to Fennick Falls on Monday, July 20, at ten o'clock. Please have the original audio ready.*

She read it through once, slowly, and then pressed send before she could change her mind.


## Chapter 13: The Author Field

Our Lady of the Harbor had been built of yellow brick in the 1890s by a congregation of Polish fishermen who had wanted a church with a view, and the view was still the best thing about it. On Saturday morning the tall windows along the north wall stood open to the bay, and the breeze that came through them smelled of cut grass and lake water and the warm wax of the altar candles. The pews were full. People stood at the back, three deep, and more waited on the steps outside in the sun, listening through the open doors.

Greta sat near the middle of the church, on the aisle, where she could see the front without turning her head. Priya sat beside her with a folded program in both hands, reading the same hymn number over and over. Walt Brandt sat two rows ahead in a dark gray suit that had clearly been bought for a different man, thirty pounds ago, and he held his hat on his knees with both hands, as if it might blow away.

The casket stood at the foot of the altar, closed, with a spray of white lilies on top. Someone had laid a navy windbreaker across the lid, folded neatly, its zipper pulled up to the collar. Greta looked at it for a long time before she could look anywhere else.

Ryan Whitcomb sat in the front pew with his back very straight. He was forty-one, tall and rawboned like his father, with the same deep lines beside his mouth and the same way of holding his jaw when he was trying not to say anything. He had arrived from Chicago on Wednesday night and had not, so far as Greta knew, left the Whitcomb house on Pier Street since. He wore a black suit that fit him well and a pair of dark glasses he did not take off, even inside the church.

Sam Ortiz stood at the back in his dress uniform, hat in his hands, his face very still. He had caught Greta's eye when she came in, and had nodded once, and had not come any closer.

Ruth Kalinowski gave the first reading herself, in a clear, careful voice that broke twice and recovered both times. Ted Vance, sitting across the aisle, wept openly into a handkerchief the whole time. Carla Sayer and Ed Matlin sat together with their heads bowed. Frances Dunn from the Gull had closed the café for the morning and sat in the fourth row in a navy dress with her arms crossed, looking at the casket as if daring it to move. Lyle Kemp sat alone near the side door with his cap on his knee and did not look up at all.

The lilies on the casket had come from Strand Lakeside Partners. There was a card tucked into the arrangement, small and cream-colored, and Greta could read the words from her seat: *With deepest respect. Gordon and Eleanor Strand.* Eleanor sat in the second row on the far side, in a black dress with a high collar, dabbing at her eyes with a tissue. She did not look toward the casket. She looked at her hands.

Tessa Quill had come. She sat at the very back, on the aisle, alone. She wore the loose green cardigan again, and her face was white and set. When the congregation rose for the final hymn, she did not sing. She stood with her hands clasped in front of her, holding a small paper program, and her eyes never left the lilies.

Bernice Oakes was not there.

Greta looked for her twice, out of habit, at the back and along the side aisles, and then stopped looking. It was not surprising. A transcriptionist who had sat through a town's record of its own grief did not necessarily want to sit through the funeral of the man she had helped to rewrite it. Still, Greta noticed the empty place where the black cardigan should have been, and she noticed Eleanor Strand glance toward the door, once, as though she too were counting people.

Father Dominic gave a short homily about fishermen and the sea. He spoke simply and kindly, and he said that Dale Whitcomb had been stubborn enough to outlive three of his friends and loud enough to annoy the rest, and that the parish would miss the sound of him at the back of every meeting. A ripple of quiet laughter went through the pews, and Walt Brandt bowed his head and pressed his hat hard against his knees.

They buried Dale in the cemetery on the hill above the harbor, where the wind came in off the water and the grass grew long between the stones. Greta stood at the edge of the crowd with the sun on her back and watched the coffin lower into the earth, and she thought about a man in a navy windbreaker asking her whether her machine lied. She did not cry. She had not expected to. What she felt instead was a steady, cold pressure behind her breastbone, the feeling she had when a witness had finally said the thing she had been waiting for, and she knew that it was going to be hard to say.

Afterward, the crowd drifted toward the parking lot and the cars. Ryan Whitcomb stood at the edge of the grass, shaking hands with men who had known his father, saying little and listening more. When he saw Greta, he nodded to her briefly, almost curtly, and turned back to the old man who was speaking to him. He did not come over.

Helen Marsh did.

She came up the gravel path from the parking lot with her cardigan buttoned to the throat, though it was warm, and she walked straight to Greta's side without speaking. For a while they stood together looking out over the harbor, where the sailboats were already going out for the afternoon, their white sails catching the wind like laundry.

"Frank wanted me to stay home," Helen said at last. "He said I looked like a ghost. I told him I had to come. I couldn't let Dale go into the ground without at least standing where somebody could see me do it."

"I'm glad you came," Greta said.

"Are you?" Helen's voice was hoarse. "I'm not sure I'd be glad to see me."

Greta said nothing.

Helen opened her handbag and took out a folded sheet of printer paper. She held it out, and Greta took it. It was a page from a document properties window, printed in grayscale, with a long column of labels and values down the left side.

*Title: Draft Minutes, Regular Meeting, July 14, 2026.*

*Author: oakes-tx.*

*Created: 7/14/2026 10:58 PM.*

*Last modified: 7/14/2026 11:41 PM.*

*Last modified by: oakes-tx.*

Greta read the lines twice.

"I printed that on Friday, in the library, after you'd gone," Helen said. "I got it from the town's document system. I'm the administrator, so I can see what the file history says. I've never looked at it before. Nobody does, really. You just click *approve* and go to bed." She swallowed. "Greta, the draft was created at ten fifty-eight on Tuesday night. Two hours after Ruth gaveled the meeting closed. And Bernice sent me the link at eleven-thirty, and I approved it at eleven fifty-two, and it had been changed at eleven forty-one, after I'd already looked at it on my phone."

"Changed how?"

"I don't know. The system doesn't show the old version. It only shows that someone opened it again and saved it." Helen's hand trembled as she took the paper back and folded it along its creases. "But why would the minutes change after I'd already been sent a link to approve? Why would anybody need to fix a document that everyone had already agreed was finished?"

Greta looked out at the harbor. The sun was bright on the water. Far out past the breakwater a single boat was turning slowly toward the open lake, its wake spreading white behind it.

"Because the first version wasn't finished," Greta said quietly. "The first version didn't have the sentence in it. Bernice wrote the minutes in a hurry, from her own notes and from the audio, and when she listened to the lobby she realized the recording didn't have what she needed. So she went back in at eleven forty-one and put the sentence in. And then she sent it to you."

Helen went very still.

"You think she made it up," Helen whispered.

"I think she wrote down what she was told to write down," Greta said. "And I think she wrote it down a lot faster than she should have, and didn't stop to ask who had told her what to say."

Helen covered her mouth with one hand. She did not cry this time. She simply stood there with her eyes closed, breathing slowly through her fingers, until a family walked past them on the path with a small child in a yellow dress, and the child waved at Helen, and Helen managed to wave back.

"I'm going to sit in the car for a while," Helen said at last. "Then I'm going to drive home and tell Frank everything. He's going to be furious with me. He's been waiting eleven years for me to finally do something exciting, and I've gone and done it wrong." She tried to smile and failed. "Will you be at the session on Monday?"

"I'll be there," Greta said. "So will Priya. And I'd like you to be there too."

"I don't know if I can stand up in front of the whole town."

"You don't have to stand up," Greta said. "You just have to sit next to the record and tell the truth when somebody asks you what you did."

Helen nodded slowly, as though she were learning a new language. Then she turned and walked back down the gravel path toward the lot, her shoulders squared against the wind.

Greta stood alone on the hill for a moment longer. Below her, at the far end of the harbor, the Harbor Office sat low and white beside the marina, its back door hidden by the cedar hedge. From here she could not see the concrete steps. She could only see the roof, and the one window, and a single gull perched on the flat edge of the parapet, watching the water.

She folded her hands in front of her and thought about a shape that had been laid out neatly under a blue tarp, and a pair of rubber boots placed carefully beside it, as if somebody had wanted to be sure they would not be lost.

Then she turned and went down the hill to find Priya.

## Chapter 14: Dale's Green Notebook

Ryan Whitcomb called her on Sunday morning, which surprised her, because she had not given him her number and had not expected him to want it. His voice on the phone was rough, as if he had been awake all night.

"Greta. It's Ryan. You were in the church yesterday, sitting on the aisle. I saw you." A pause. "I want to come by the house on Pier Street. There's something I should have told you at the funeral, and I couldn't say it in front of the priest."

"I'll come," Greta said. "What time?"

"Six. I'll be there. I'm not going to be there long. I've got a flight to Midway at eight tomorrow morning and a meeting with a lawyer on Tuesday that I'd rather not be late for."

"Is the lawyer about your father's estate?"

Ryan laughed, short and bitter. "Everything's about my father's estate, Greta. That's the only thing he ever left anybody in this town. A boat, a shop, a list of people who owed him money." He was quiet for a moment. "Six," he said again, and hung up.

The house on Pier Street was a narrow, two-story frame building painted a faded yellow, with a porch that sagged a little at one end and a row of potted geraniums along the rail, all of them dead except one. Dale had not been a man for flowers. Greta supposed someone else had planted them, long ago, and he had simply not had the heart to pull them out. A pair of old fishing rods leaned against the porch column. A green tarp covered the bed of a truck parked in the drive. The lawn had been mowed that morning, and the cut grass smelled sharp and sweet in the evening heat.

Ryan opened the door before she knocked. He had taken off his suit jacket and rolled his shirtsleeves to the elbow, and his face looked older than it had at the church, as though the funeral had taken something out of him that would not come back.

"Come in," he said. "Please. Don't mind the mess. He never threw anything out."

The inside of the house was a museum of a particular kind of life. The living room held a worn brown recliner and a radio the size of a suitcase, its dial still tuned to a station that played old country songs. A calendar from Whitcomb Marine Supply hung on the wall beside the window, turned to July, with a red circle drawn around the fourteenth and a single word written in neat block capitals beside it: *COUNCIL*. The kitchen beyond was spotless, though the cabinet doors had been left open, and a single coffee mug sat upside down on the drying rack, the way a man left a cup when he had only needed it for a minute.

Ryan led her through to the kitchen and pulled out a chair. He did not sit down himself.

"I talked to him on Tuesday night," he said. "Dad. I haven't talked to him properly in a year. Not since the holiday dinner, when he told me I'd made a fool of myself in front of the whole family and he wasn't going to pay for the next mess I got myself into." His hands were gripping the back of the chair. "I was in Chicago. I was at a bar on Lincoln Avenue with a couple of guys from work. I'd had too much to drink, I'll admit that. I looked at my phone at nine-fifty and it said *Dad*, and I almost didn't pick up."

"But you did."

"I did." He let out a breath. "He was angry. Not at me. At somebody else. He said he'd been at the council meeting and he'd heard two people in the lobby talking about cleaning up the record. He said, *They think they can make the town forget what I said, Ryan. They're going to cut my voice off the tape.* I didn't really understand what he was talking about. I said, *Dad, you're upset, go to bed.* And he laughed, and he said, *Your mother would have had a fit.* And he hung up on me." He swallowed. "That was the last thing he said to me. *Your mother would have had a fit.*"

Greta looked at the young man standing in his father's kitchen, with his sleeves rolled up and his hands white on the chair, and felt a flicker of something she had not expected. Not sympathy, exactly. Recognition. He was a man who had spent his life trying to get his father to hear him, and had never once succeeded.

"Ryan," she said gently. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be," he said. "Please. I don't want anybody being sorry for me. I want somebody to find out who did it."

"You think somebody did it."

"I don't know what I think." He let go of the chair and rubbed his face with both hands. "I think my father fell down a set of steps he'd walked up and down for forty years, and I think he was carrying something he shouldn't have been carrying, and I think somebody in this town knew it. And I think they'll let the whole thing go away, because that's what this town does with people like Dad. It shakes its head, and it says *he was a hard man, but he was ours*, and then it forgets."

"I won't forget," Greta said.

He looked at her for a moment, then nodded. "I know. That's why I called you."

He went out of the kitchen and returned a minute later with a small green notebook in his hand. It was a plain clothbound book, the kind sold in stationery shops in a box of three, its spine faded and cracked, a stub of pencil wedged between its pages. He held it out to her.

"He kept everything in this," Ryan said. "Every job. Every debt. Every time somebody crossed him. He had a notebook like this in every drawer in the house, and he always bought the same brand, because he said the paper didn't bleed. I found this one under the seat of the recliner this morning, when I was looking for his reading glasses. I didn't read it until just now."

"Why didn't you read it before?"

"Because I was afraid of what it would say about me," Ryan said. "There's a page in there with my name on it. I don't want you to read that page."

"I won't," Greta said.

She took the notebook carefully and opened it on the kitchen table. The writing inside was small and square and entirely familiar. She had read a thousand pages of handwriting in her life, and she knew at once that it was Dale's, pressed hard into the paper, the strokes sharp and deliberate.

The entries were dated, but not always neatly. Some of them had only a day. Some had only a time. Greta read them slowly, one by one, aware of Ryan standing silently behind her chair.

*Tues. Helen won't release TownShare key. "Procedure." Procedure be damned. Tell her she can explain procedure to the state.*

*Tues. 5:10 p.m. Gull. Walt says I'm a hard man. Good.*

*Tues. 8:50. Recess. Lobby. Gordon + B.O. at the urn. Heard it plain: "Have it cleaned up by morning, before she approves it." Clean up WHAT? I was on my feet.*

*Tues. 9:02. Back in the room. Remained there like a fool with my hands on the table, not trusting my mouth. Walked out at 9:14. Called G.L. Steno. Seven a.m.*

Greta stopped reading and looked at the words for a long time. Then she moved on.

*B.O. came to the Harbor Office in June with an envelope for Helen. Wanted to know where the TownShare key lives. Strange for a transcriptionist. Wanted me to sign for it. I didn't.*

*Bo L. fired 6/20. Swore he'd get even. Bo can't spell "lease." Ask Lyle where he sleeps now.*

*Tessa Q. Scared. Good kid. Ask her about the survey. Ask her who gave her the July stamp.*

*Survey signed 7/2. Comment closed 6/30. Thirteen words of law and a stamp. That's all they need.*

*Wednesday. 6 a.m. Harbor. Pull the audio off that drive myself if I have to. Check the back stairs. Rail's loose at the bottom. Tell Lyle.*

Under that line, Dale had drawn a sketch in pencil, quick and rough. It was a flight of steps, seen from the side, with a landing at the top and a railing along one edge. Thirteen short lines marked the stairs, one for each step. At the bottom, beneath the last line, he had drawn a small neat cross, and beside it, in capitals, he had written a single word.

*HERE.*

Greta closed the notebook and sat very still.

"What is it?" Ryan asked quietly.

"Your father meant to go to the Harbor Office at six o'clock on Wednesday morning," Greta said. "He meant to get the audio. He wrote down the time and the place. He was going to check the stairs." She looked up at him. "He was planning to be there before anyone else."

Ryan's face crumpled, just for a second, before he got it back under control. He turned toward the window and stood with his back to her.

"He was always early," he said roughly. "He'd be at the boat ramp at five in the morning, sitting in that truck with the engine running, waiting for the fog to lift. I used to think he was crazy. I used to think it was the only thing he cared about."

"Who is B.O.?" Greta asked.

Ryan turned around. "I don't know. Bo Lindqvist, maybe. The dockhand he fired in June. He used to come around the marina at night and throw rocks at the windows of the fuel shed. Dad was going to report him to the county." He frowned. "Or it could be somebody from the transcription place. Dad hated that outfit. He said the woman who ran it looked at him like he was a piece of furniture she'd been asked to dust."

"Bernice Oakes," Greta said.

"Maybe." Ryan shrugged, tired. "I don't know, Greta. I don't know anything about this town anymore. I've been gone too long."

Greta opened the notebook again and read the line about Tuesday at the Gull. *Walt says I'm a hard man. Good.* It was such a small thing, such an ordinary thing, and it made her chest ache.

"Ryan," she said. "Do you have a copy of this? A photograph, or a scan?"

He looked at her in surprise. "No. Why?"

"Because somebody else may want it," Greta said. "And I'd rather the town see it in your father's hand than in mine." She closed the cover. "I'm going to take it to Sam Ortiz tomorrow morning, if you'll let me. He'll want the original, and he'll give you a receipt for it. But I'll make a copy of every page tonight, and I'll give it to Priya to hold, and she'll keep it in a safe in the back of the shop until the session on Monday."

Ryan stood for a long moment, looking at the notebook, then at her.

"Take it," he said. "Take the whole thing. He'd have hated that I was being careful with it."

She tucked the notebook into her canvas bag, next to the tape. The two things sat together against the cloth, one a record of what had been said in a room, the other a record of what a man had been thinking while he waited for someone to tell him the truth.

On the way out, Ryan walked her to the porch. The evening was softening into dusk, and the sky over the harbor had gone the color of apricots. Down on the water, the navigation lights on the moorings had begun to blink, one after another, like small patient eyes.

"Tuesday night," Ryan said. "I was in the bar until about twelve-thirty. I've got the receipt in my wallet. I'll show it to Sam if he asks. And the bartender remembers me. I was crying into a beer and telling her about my father's boat." He gave a short, humorless laugh. "I was a mess, Greta. But I wasn't in Lantern Bay."

"I didn't think you were," Greta said.

"Everybody thinks I was," Ryan said. "I can see it in their faces, at the church, in the Gull. The son from Chicago who never came home." He looked down at the porch boards. "I wish I had come home."

Greta reached out and touched his arm, briefly, the way Sam had touched hers on the morning of the death.

"You're here now," she said.

He nodded without looking up. Then he went back inside, and the screen door swung shut behind him with the same soft complaint that she had heard at her own house a hundred times, and the porch light went off, and Greta walked down the steps to her car, carrying a dead man's notebook under her arm like something that might still be warm.

## Chapter 15: Fennick Falls

Priya drove. She had insisted on it, on Monday morning, pulling her old white van into Greta's gravel drive at a quarter past eight with two travel mugs of coffee in the cup holders and a box of lemon bars on the passenger seat that Greta had not been allowed to refuse. "You're going to sit in the back of a transcription office," Priya had said, "and listen to a woman tell you that the recording is unreliable. You are going to need someone who can tell when she's lying and tell you about it in the car."

The road to Fennick Falls ran south along the shore and then inland through a long stretch of cherry orchards, their trees heavy with dark fruit that nobody had yet picked. The fields had been mowed, and the air coming through the open windows smelled of hay and sweet rot. Greta sat with her canvas bag on her knees and the tape folded inside it, and she did not talk for most of the first half hour. Neither did Priya. They listened to the radio, which was playing an old program about a family of radio actors who had once lived in a town very much like Lantern Bay, and Priya laughed once at a joke about a fish, and Greta did not.

Fennick Falls was a market town in Carrow County, with a red brick bank, a Methodist church with a white steeple, a double row of storefronts along Main Street, and a river that ran brown and fast under a stone bridge at the edge of town. Oakes Transcription occupied a narrow Victorian house on Maple Street, three blocks from the courthouse, with a wide front porch and a brass plate beside the door that read *Oakes Transcription — Accuracy Since 1997*. The lawn was trimmed. The windows were clean. A white hatchback stood in the gravel lot behind the house, its left taillight cracked straight across like a hairline fracture, and beside it a silver minivan with a child's car seat in the back.

Priya parked on the street. Neither of them moved for a moment.

"That's the car," Priya said quietly.

"I know," Greta said.

"Do you want me to come in with you?"

"Yes," Greta said. "Please."

A young woman met them at the door. She was perhaps thirty, with cropped dark hair and a cardigan of soft gray wool, and she introduced herself as Denise Park, the office manager, in a voice that was polite and not quite friendly. She showed them into a front parlor that had been converted into a reception room, with a long oak table, a row of upholstered chairs, and a framed certificate on the wall from the state's shorthand reporters' association. Beyond the parlor, through an open doorway, Greta could see a bright room full of workstations, each with a dual monitor and a foot pedal under the desk, and a row of tall gray filing cabinets against the far wall.

"Ms. Oakes is in the back," Denise said. "She's expecting you. She asked me to make coffee, and I did, but I'm afraid it's the kind that's been sitting since seven."

"We'll manage," Priya said, sitting down in one of the upholstered chairs and smoothing her skirt. "I've had worse at the shop."

Denise gave her a thin smile and went away down the hall.

Bernice Oakes came out of an office at the back of the house a minute later, closing the door carefully behind her. She wore a dark blue blouse and tailored gray slacks, and her hair had been pinned up in a neat knot at the back of her head. Her right wrist was bare, and her left wore two silver bangles. Greta noticed that the two that remained did not quite match, one a little thicker than the other, as though a link had been lost and the other had been pressed closed in its place.

"Greta," Bernice said. "Thank you for coming. And you've brought Priya Rao, which is a pleasant surprise." She smiled at Priya, who did not smile back. "Would you like to come through to the conference room? I've set up the audio on the monitor. It's really much clearer on the big speakers than it would be on a laptop."

The conference room was at the very back of the house, with a long window that looked out over a small herb garden and a row of tall sunflowers. A round table stood in the center, with a laptop open on one end and a pair of studio headphones draped over the back of a chair. A single printed transcript lay at the other end, its cover page facing up, bound in a clean blue binder. Bernice sat down at the head of the table and gestured for Greta to take the seat across from her. Priya sat beside Greta, her hands folded in her lap.

"I want to be clear before we begin," Bernice said pleasantly. "The town has asked me to produce a verified transcript for the special session on Monday. I'm happy to share my work with you, because I have nothing to hide. But the original audio file belongs to the Town of Lantern Bay, and I cannot release a copy without a written request from Helen Marsh, which she has not sent. What I can do is let you hear the portion of the recording I used to prepare the transcript, on my own workstation, while you're in the room with me." She tapped the laptop with one fingertip. "That's standard practice. I'm sure you understand."

"I understand," Greta said.

Bernice turned the laptop toward her and pressed a key. A waveform appeared on the screen, long and jagged, marked with a timeline in minutes and seconds. The file was labeled *CC_0714_Regular_WORKING.wav*. Bernice scrolled the cursor to a point near the middle and clicked.

The room came up out of the speakers. Greta heard it at once, the thin hiss of a microphone in an empty space, the faint rumble of the old refrigerator in the Center's kitchen, the low gurgle of the coffee urn in the lobby, and beneath it, very far away, the muffled voices of people talking in a corridor. It was the sound of the council room at a quarter to nine on Tuesday night. She knew it the way she knew her own handwriting.

"That's the recess," Bernice said, and her voice was calm and professional. "You can hear the room going quiet as people move toward the lobby. There's a door, and then the lobby mic picks up the coffee machine. That's the only other input we have."

Greta listened. The voices in the corridor rose and fell. A laugh, somewhere. A chair scraping. Then the refrigerator hum, steady and familiar, the urn gurgling on under it, and the thin hiss of the microphone.

"Stop there," Greta said.

Bernice clicked the mouse. The playback stopped.

"Play it again," Greta said. "From eight-fifty."

Bernice hesitated for only the smallest instant, and then she moved the cursor back to the start of the recess and pressed play.

Greta closed her eyes and listened. She had spent most of her life listening to recordings in just this way, with her eyes shut and her hands flat on a desk, waiting for the sound that did not belong. The refrigerator hummed. The voices in the corridor drifted. The hiss of the microphone was steady.

And then, at eight fifty-two and fourteen seconds, the refrigerator stopped.

It did not fade. It did not waver. It simply vanished, as though someone had pulled a plug out of the wall. The refrigerator stopped, and the hiss stopped, and the urn stopped mid-gurgle, and in their place the whole building went absolutely silent, a dead, flat silence with no sound in it at all, for a little under twenty seconds. Then the hum came back, a little higher in pitch, and with it a faint new sound, a click, and the voices in the corridor rushed back in, loud and close, as if the door had just been opened by a hand.

Greta opened her eyes.

"That's a dropout," Bernice said. "The system cuts out for a few seconds when the gain changes, and it sounds very strange. The vendor calls it a *gap artifact*. It's common on old installations."

"It's a splice," Greta said quietly.

Bernice's expression did not change. "I'm sorry?"

"I learned to hear a splice on a reel-to-reel tape in the nineteen-eighties," Greta said. "When the courthouse still used machines that needed a razor blade and a roll of cellophane tape. Somebody cuts a piece out of the middle, and then sticks the two ends together. The sound comes back a little different, because the room tone has changed and the machine's hum has shifted. That's what I heard at eight fifty-two. The refrigerator stopped, and then it started again, and it was a different refrigerator." She opened her eyes fully and looked across the table. "Your gain artifact doesn't change the pitch of the hum, Bernice. A splice does."

Priya let out a breath beside her, very softly.

Bernice sat back in her chair and folded her hands on the table. For a moment she looked at Greta with an expression of such perfect, professional patience that Greta almost admired it.

"Greta," she said at last, "you've always been brilliant with your hands. I used to tell the judges so. But you're not an audio engineer, and I am the person who has been responsible for this recording since the vendor installed the system in March. I know what its artifacts sound like. I've spent a hundred hours with that file." She tilted her head slightly. "The gap is real. It's a dropout, not a splice, and it occurred during the recess, when nobody was speaking in the room. I can show you the logs."

"Show me," Greta said.

Bernice clicked the mouse. A window opened on the screen, filled with rows of small gray text. *Gain event 20:52:14 — dropout 00:18 — auto-restore.* Greta read the line twice. It looked official. It also had a time stamp that matched the moment the refrigerator stopped, to the second, which meant somebody had written the log after the fact, or had written it with knowledge of the gap.

"Who generated this log?" Greta asked.

"The system does," Bernice said. "Automatically."

"And when did you print it?"

"Wednesday morning," Bernice said. "Just after eight. Before Helen called me in a panic about the minutes."

Greta looked at her across the table, at the bare wrist and the two uneven bangles, and felt a cold, quiet certainty settle over her, the way it did when a witness finally lied in a way that could be proved.

"You were at the urn," Greta said.

For the first time, Bernice's eyes flickered. It was barely a movement. A slight narrowing, like a woman who has heard a noise in the next room and is deciding whether to get up.

"I was in the lobby," Bernice said evenly. "Yes. I went out during the recess to get a cup of coffee. Gordon Strand asked me whether the transcript would be ready by the end of the week, and I told him it would, and then I went back inside. I didn't say anything to Dale Whitcomb. I didn't speak to him at all."

"You said you left at quarter past eight," Greta said. "To Sam. To me, in the lot."

"I said I was home by nine-thirty," Bernice said. "I left the meeting early. I did leave early. I had a client to think about, and a long drive. I went out the back way while the council was still talking, and I didn't come back until the next morning, when Helen called me." She spread her hands on the table. "If you want to ask me whether I stood by the urn for ninety seconds on Tuesday night, the answer is yes. I did. It's the most ordinary thing in the world. Ask anyone who was in that lobby."

"I'm asking you," Greta said.

"And I'm telling you." Bernice's voice was level. "Greta, I have worked for twenty-nine years in rooms full of people who wanted the record to say something it didn't say. Lawyers, judges, witnesses, clients, the families of the dead. The job is to take down what is said, accurately, and to be scrupulous about it. I have never once in my life put a word in anybody's mouth. I'm not going to start with a man who died on a flight of steps."

Priya stirred beside her, and Greta felt her friend's hand touch her elbow, lightly, a warning or a question.

"Dale came to your office in June," Greta said. "Did not he?"

Bernice blinked. "Yes."

"With a brown envelope," Greta said. "He asked where the town keeps the key to the recordings. He told the commission you'd been asking the same question."

"Dale Whitcomb came into the Harbor Office in June and shouted at my courier," Bernice said, her voice tightening for the first time. "The courier had come to pick up a signed release for a set of transcripts. Dale told him the town didn't need a Fennick Falls boy poking around its harbor, and then he told the commission that I'd sent him. I went myself the next day to clear it up. I signed a form. I took the transcripts back. And Dale was rude to me in front of his staff, which is his privilege, I suppose." She drew a long breath. "But I never asked him where the key was. I don't know where the key is. I've never needed to know."

"And the envelope?"

"What envelope?"

"The one Dale mentioned in his notebook," Greta said. "The one you brought to Helen."

Bernice's jaw tightened. "I brought Helen an invoice in June, for the spring transcripts. It's in my files. It's a perfectly ordinary invoice, Greta, and I'd be glad to show it to you, along with every other document I've ever sent this town."

"I'd like to see the invoice from Wednesday," Greta said. "The one for the rush correction. The one that says *per G. Strand*."

Bernice did not answer for a moment. Then she closed the laptop, very gently, with both hands, the way a person might close the lid of a piano.

"That invoice is between the Town of Lantern Bay and my firm," she said. "And between my firm and whoever pays it. You're not the town, Greta. You're a retired reporter with a cat and a grudge."

Greta stood up. Priya stood with her, one hand still on her arm.

"Thank you for the coffee," Greta said.

"Greta," Bernice said, as they reached the door. Her voice was quiet, almost kind. "You always did like being the last one in the room. Even when you were a girl in the courthouse library, you'd sit in the corner with your pencils until everyone else had gone home, just in case someone said something you needed to hear." She folded her hands again. "I'd stay home on Monday, if I were you. It's going to be a very long meeting, and I don't think you'll enjoy what the record says at the end."

Greta did not turn around. She walked down the narrow hall, past the reception parlor and the bright room full of workstations, where Denise Park was typing quickly with her head down, and out onto the porch into the warm, dusty light.

Priya did not say anything until they had reached the van and climbed inside and shut the doors. Then she started the engine, and sat with both hands on the wheel, and let out a long, shaky breath.

"She knew you'd come," Priya said. "She had the whole thing set up for you. The monitor, the laptop, the coffee, the binder with the blue cover. She wanted you to see the gap and think it was a dropout."

"She wanted me to hear it," Greta said. "And to know she could say what she liked about it, and nobody would be able to prove her wrong, because the only other copy was in a house that had been emptied out." She looked back at the Victorian, with its clean windows and trimmed lawn and its brass plate beside the door. "But she made one mistake."

"What?"

"She told me the dropout was a gain event," Greta said. "But a gain event on one channel doesn't stop a coffee urn in the lobby, and it doesn't stop a refrigerator on the other side of a wall. The urn kept gurgling through every other second of that recess, and it stopped with everything else at eight fifty-two. Whoever cut that file cut the whole building out, Priya, not one microphone."

Priya turned and stared at her. Then, slowly, she smiled.

"You're a terrible person to play cards with," she said.

"I'm a court reporter," Greta said. "I don't play cards."

The van pulled away from the curb, and the white hatchback in the gravel lot behind the house sat very still in the sun, its cracked taillight catching the light like a thin red thread through a stone.


## Chapter 16: The Bookshop Alibi

Page and Thistle closed at six on weekdays, but on Tuesday evening the front door stayed locked and the sign in the window still read *Back in a Moment*, which Priya had lettered herself three summers ago and had never once changed. Inside, the shop was dim and smelled of paper, lemon oil, and the faint sweetness of the candles Priya lit on Fridays and then forgot to blow out. The floorboards creaked. The shelves rose to the ceiling on three sides, crowded with mysteries and cookbooks and old travel guides with cracked spines, and in the back corner, under a brass reading lamp with a green shade, a round oak table held a stack of books waiting to be shelved and a box of lemon bars that was almost empty.

Greta sat at the table with a pencil and the back of a bookseller's order form, which Priya had given her with a look that said she was not going to argue about it. The order form was printed with the shop's name in a cursive script and a small drawing of a pine tree. Greta had turned it over, smoothed it flat, and written the time at the top in neat, small capitals, the way she would have written it on the first page of a transcript.

*TUESDAY, JULY 14. WHO WAS IN THE ROOM. WHEN.*

Priya sat across from her with a mug of tea and her reading glasses pushed up into her hair. She had not said much since they came in. She had watched Greta write, the way she might have watched a customer choose between two identical paperbacks, waiting for the moment when the decision was finally made.

"Start with me," Priya said.

"You were in the gallery at seven-oh-seven," Greta said. "You left at eight forty-four. Your phone rang, and you went out to the lobby to take the call, and then you walked back to the shop."

"The alarm company," Priya said. "One of the motion sensors in the stockroom was throwing a fault. The woman on the phone wanted to know if I was going to be at the shop for the next hour, because she was sending a technician at nine. I told her I'd be there. I walked home at a clip, because the book club was coming at nine and I'd promised them coffee." She frowned into her mug. "The call log says eight forty-six, if you need proof. It's on my phone. I'll show you."

"I believe you," Greta said, and wrote *P.N. left 8:44. Alarm call 8:46. Shop by 8:55.*

"Ted Vance was the first one out," Priya said. "I noticed because he was holding a pen in each hand and looking like he wanted to drop them both. He went out the side door at eight-twenty. I think he was sick. He walked home along Cutter Street, I'm fairly sure, because I saw him from the window of the shop when I got in. He was walking very slowly, as though his feet hurt."

Greta wrote *T.V. out 8:20 (sick?). Walked home.* She underlined *sick?* and then, after a moment, drew a line through it, because Ted Vance had never once been sick in the room in eleven years of service, and she did not believe he had been sick that night.

"Then the recess," Greta said. "Ruth called it at eight-fifty. Carla and Ed went to the stairs, to the coffee. Helen went after them with her laptop, and she came back at nine-oh-five, and she sat with her hand on the screen the rest of the meeting. That's all I can say for certain, because I was watching the lobby door and it was only open for a moment at a time."

"And Walt?"

"Walt stayed in his seat," Greta said. "Under the portrait. He had his coffee cup on his knee the entire time."

"He would," Priya said softly. "Walt has never left a room before the end of anything in his life."

"Then Gordon," Greta said, and wrote *G.S. lobby 8:50 — 9:05.* "He was in the front row when the recess was called. He stood up, buttoned his jacket, and went out after Dale. I saw him reach the door at eight fifty-one. He came back in at nine-oh-six, about a minute after Dale did, and he sat down with his arms folded."

"Dale went out first," Priya said.

"Dale went out first. He was in the lobby with Gordon, and then Bernice went after them. She had her laptop bag on her shoulder and her bangles were chiming at the door. I heard them." Greta set the pencil down for a moment. "Bernice came back at nine-oh-five. She sat down in the back row beside Walt. I wrote it on the tape, in the margin. *Mrs. Oakes back, row four, bangles.*"

"And Tessa?"

"Tessa stayed in her seat," Greta said. "She never left the room. She sat with that green folder on her knees for the whole meeting, and she didn't take a single note after Dale spoke about the survey. She was looking at the folder as if it might bite her." She wrote *T.Q. stayed in room. Folder.* "And after the vote, she left through the front with Gordon's jacket over her arm. I saw her go down the stairs at nine-fifteen. Or thereabouts."

"She signed the back door sheet at nine-thirty-five," Priya said. "Sam told me. Sam tells me everything when he's been up for twenty hours and forgets I'm not a deputy."

"He told me too," Greta said.

"And Dale?"

Greta wrote the line slowly. *D.W. back in room 9:09. Silent. Out 9:14, adjourned. Walked home (Pier Rd).* She stopped and looked at the words. She could see him in her mind, walking down the stairs with his jaw set and his hands in the pockets of his navy windbreaker. She could see him stop beside her table and ask his question.

"He walked home," she said. "He always walked home. He lived on Pier Street, six blocks from the Center. He called me from his kitchen at nine forty-one, I'd guess. He had his cup of coffee and he was sitting by the radio with the calendar on the wall, and he was thinking about what he'd heard in the lobby."

"Greta," Priya said quietly. "You don't know that."

"No," Greta said. "I know what the notebook says."

Priya's mouth tightened. She did not argue.

"And Bernice?" she asked.

Greta looked at the order form for a long moment. Then she wrote the last line.

*B.O. home by 9:30 (her statement). Lobby 8:50. Back row 9:05.*

"She says she left at quarter past eight," Greta said. "She says she was home by half past nine. She told Sam that. She told me that, in the lot, with her hands folded in front of her. She says she didn't speak to Dale, and she didn't go back into the room."

"And the tape says she did."

"The tape says she was back in the room at nine-oh-five, with her bangles on the chair arm and her notebook open. That's not a matter of opinion, Priya. It's typed."

Priya took her glasses out of her hair and turned them over in her hands, slowly, the way she did when she was deciding how much to say.

"I was closing the shop that night," she said. "Did I ever tell you that? Not the meeting. Afterward. The book club went home at ten past ten, and I counted the till, and then I stood in the front window for a while because I couldn't face going upstairs. It was very quiet on Front Street. Nobody out but a couple of teenagers on bikes, and Gene Osterhout's truck going past the fuel dock." She put the glasses down. "And at about ten to ten, a white car came up the street from the south. It went past the window very slowly, the way people drive when they're looking for an address. It had one taillight that was cracked. I noticed because the streetlight caught the red on that side and made it look like a bruise."

Greta's pencil stopped.

"What time?" she asked.

"Nine-fifty," Priya said. "Maybe nine-forty-eight. I looked at the clock on the register because I was waiting for the book club's last check. It was going north, toward the harbor. Toward Pier Street." She swallowed. "I didn't think anything of it. I thought it was some tourist who'd got lost on the way to the lake. Then I saw the car again on Monday, when you and I drove out of Fennick Falls and it was sitting in the gravel lot behind that house. I didn't say anything. It was none of my business."

"You didn't tell me."

"I didn't know what it meant," Priya said. "I didn't want it to be anything. She's been a friend of mine for twelve years, Greta, in that stupid, polite way people are friends at book fairs. She used to bring me galley copies of the transcripts for the courthouse reading group. She sent flowers when my mother died." She pressed her hand flat against the table. "I didn't want to be the one who said *white car*."

Greta looked at her friend for a long moment. Then she reached across the table and put her hand over Priya's, lightly, the way she had once seen Helen touch her laptop.

"You're not the one who said it," she said. "You're the one who saw it. There's a difference, and the difference is going to matter on Monday night."

Priya breathed out slowly.

"So Bernice Oakes lied to you," she said. "And to Sam. And to Helen, probably, though Helen never asked her where she'd been. She told you she was in Fennick Falls by nine-thirty, and she was driving north to Pier Street at ten to ten, with the cracked light catching the streetlamp."

"Yes."

"And Tessa saw her," Priya said. "At the Bait and Tackle, when she got back with the folder. You told me that, this morning, on the phone."

"Tessa said Bernice's white car was parked behind the building when she got there, after ten," Greta said. "She didn't say what time Bernice had arrived. But she did say they were laughing."

"Laughing," Priya repeated, and her face went very still.

"Laughing about a man who would be dead by morning," Greta said quietly, "and who had written down, in a green notebook under his reading glasses, that he was going to the Harbor Office at six o'clock the next morning to get the audio back."

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

At the front of the shop, the wind pushed against the glass door, and the little brass bell above it rang once, very softly, and fell silent. Somewhere upstairs, Priya's cat, a fat orange tom named Chapter Eleven, thumped to the floor from a bookshelf and went to see what the commotion was about.

"There's one more thing," Greta said at last. "Ryan Whitcomb was in a bar in Chicago until half past midnight. Sam's checked the receipts. He called me this afternoon to say the bartender's statement matches, and the card charges are time-stamped at nine-twenty and twelve-forty. So Ryan's out."

"Poor man," Priya said.

"He's grieving," Greta said. "He's also been reading his father's voicemails for a week, trying to hear something in them that isn't there. I told him we'd find out what happened. I don't know yet whether that's a promise I can keep."

"You'll keep it," Priya said. "You always do." She stood up, went to the back of the shop, and came back with a plate of the last lemon bars, which she set down between them with a small, deliberate clatter, as though she were defying the entire contents of the afternoon. "Eat one. You look like the ghost of an old judge."

Greta took one. It was tart and sweet and dissolved on her tongue.

"Walt," Priya said suddenly, as if the thought had just arrived. "Walt goes out on the water at six every morning. He's been doing it for forty years. He sees everything from that boat, Greta. The fuel dock, the harbor office, the cars parked along the shore road. Every single one."

"He told me he'd see the cars," Greta said slowly.

"Then go and ask him," Priya said. "And ask him what he saw on Wednesday, before anybody was standing on those steps."

## Chapter 17: The Boat Yard

The Lantern Bay boat yard lay at the southern end of the harbor, past the fuel dock and the long concrete ramp where the trailers backed in on summer weekends. It was a sprawling, untidy place, half sheds and half open gravel, with a crane on rails above the travel lift, a row of stacked hulls under blue tarps, and a corrugated metal office with a window unit that dripped steadily onto the ground. Lyle Kemp had agreed to walk Greta through it on Wednesday morning, though he had made it clear he did not think much of the idea.

"Bo Lindqvist's been living in his truck since June," Lyle said as they walked along the gravel. He had his cap pulled low and his hands jammed in the pockets of his canvas jacket. "Dale fired him for stealing fuel. Bo says he didn't steal anything, that he was only borrowing it to run the outboard on his brother's skiff. Dale didn't want to hear that. He told Bo to clear out of the yard by sundown, and Bo threw a rock through the window of the fuel shed that night, and then he threw another one, and then he called Dale a name that's not fit for church." He spat neatly into the gravel. "I don't like Bo. I'm not going to pretend I do. But I don't think he killed anybody."

"Did you see his truck on Wednesday morning?"

"I think so," Lyle said. "I came in at six-fifty to open the marina. His truck was parked by the travel lift, the way it always is. He was asleep in the cab with the window down. He didn't look up when I went past." He hesitated. "I didn't think anything of it. He's always asleep in that truck."

They found Bo Lindqvist sitting on the step of a battered green camper trailer parked behind the largest of the sheds. He was thirty-four years old, thin and sunburned, with a shaved head and a patchy red beard, and a faded anchor tattooed on the inside of one forearm. He had a plastic cup of instant coffee in one hand and a cigarette in the other, and when he saw Lyle, he stood up so quickly that the cup spilled down the front of his T-shirt.

"Oh, for God's sake," he said. "What do you want, Lyle?"

"She wants to talk to you," Lyle said, and jerked his head at Greta. "Be polite, or I'll tell your brother you're sleeping in a truck again."

Bo looked at Greta with narrowed eyes. He did not recognize her, she thought, and then he did, and his face changed.

"You're the court lady," he said. "The one with the tape machine. You sat in the back of the council room every month, typing with your eyes shut. I used to watch you when I came to the meetings to get my paycheck." He flicked his cigarette into the gravel. "What's Dale's machine lady want with me? I didn't kill him, if that's what you think. I'd have liked to. But I didn't."

"I don't think you killed him," Greta said. "I want to know where you were."

Bo laughed, a short, hard sound. "I was in my truck. Tuesday night. I was drinking, if you want the truth, and I was crying, which I'm not going to pretend I didn't do, because a man got fired for being a sorry buzzard and I was trying to figure out what I was going to do about it. Then I slept. Then I woke up, and it was getting light, and I went to the bathroom in the marina shower, and when I came back Lyle was walking past my truck with his cap on like he was going to a funeral." He shook his head. "That's all. I didn't go near the Harbor Office. I didn't go near Dale. I haven't set foot in that building since June, and I'm not going to, not for you or the sheriff or anybody."

"Did anybody see you in the truck?"

"The fish," Bo said. "And the gulls. And Walt Brandt, probably, because he goes past the yard every morning at six in that rusted-out Ford of his. He always gives me a look like I'm something he stepped in." He sat back down on the step, slowly. "Is that enough?"

"For now," Greta said.

He snorted. "That's what the sheriff said, too. He was up here on Thursday asking the same questions. He wrote it all down in a little book and told me not to leave town."

Greta looked at him for a long moment. He was not a clever man, she thought, and he was not a gentle one, but he was not hiding anything large. His hands were shaking. His eyes kept going back to the shed, where a rusted metal sign still hung on the door. *FUEL — NO SMOKING — DALE WHITCOMB, MANAGER.*

"Dale kept a notebook," she said.

Bo's face went blank. "Yeah?"

"He wrote your initials in it. *B.L.* On a page with the date in June, and the words *can't spell lease*. Do you know what he meant by that?"

Bo laughed again, but this time there was something different in it, something almost sad. "He meant I can't spell. Which is true. He used to stand over me in the fuel shed and spell words out for me, like I was a kid. *L-E-A-S-E.* As if it were a school test." He looked down at the gravel. "He was a mean old buzzard, Dale. But he never once pretended he was something he wasn't. You had to respect that."

"Was there another set of initials?" Greta asked gently. "B.O."

Bo looked up sharply. "B.O.? That's not me, lady. I'm B.L. My mom used to call me Bo, that's all."

"Who else would he write as B.O.?"

Bo was quiet for a moment. Then he looked past Greta toward the road, where a white car had just turned in off Pier Street and was moving slowly along the edge of the gravel, its driver peering through the windshield as though looking for someone.

"The one with the bracelets," Bo said. "The transcription woman. Bernice something." His voice had dropped. "She came around the harbor all June, you know. Every week, in that white hatchback with the dent in the door. She'd park by the fuel shed and sit there with the engine running, looking at the Harbor Office like she was waiting for somebody to come out. Dale noticed. He told me to stay away from her, the one time she came inside. He said she was a snake in a sweater." He shrugged. "I thought he was being a jerk. Then I thought he was right."

The white car had stopped at the edge of the yard. Greta could see the driver's face through the windshield, pale and composed, watching them. The bangles on the left wrist caught the sunlight, two of them, uneven, one thicker than the other.

Bernice did not get out. She simply sat there, looking at Bo Lindqvist, and then at Greta, and then at Lyle Kemp, and after a long moment she lifted one hand from the steering wheel in a small, neat wave, as though she had just recognized an old acquaintance across a crowded room. Then she put the car into reverse, backed out onto Pier Street without hurrying, and drove away toward the harbor.

"That's her," Bo said quietly. "That's the one."

"Thank you," Greta said.

"Don't thank me," Bo said. He picked up his coffee cup, looked into it, and threw the rest of it into the gravel. "I didn't do anything but get fired. You're the one who's going to get hurt."

Lyle walked Greta back along the gravel toward the marina road. Neither of them spoke for a while. The sun was higher now, and the air over the water shimmered with heat.

"She knew you'd come," Lyle said at last.

"Everybody knows everybody's business in this town," Greta said. "She had the same information I did. Bo told the whole county what he thought of Dale. And she knew you'd bring me to him, because you're the only one in the harbor who'd take the trouble."

Lyle grunted, unconvinced. "She drove past the yard slow as a funeral procession. She didn't have to stop."

"No," Greta agreed. "She didn't."

At the edge of the marina road, Lyle stopped and looked back toward the sheds, where Bo had gone back to his trailer and sat down on the step again with his head in his hands.

"He's not a killer, Greta," Lyle said quietly. "I've known him since he was nine years old and fell off the end of the fuel dock. He cried for three hours that day because he thought he'd drowned his brother's boat hook." He shook his head. "He's just a kid who never got to be anything but angry."

"I know," Greta said.

"And Dale was the one who made him angry," Lyle said. "I used to think Dale did it on purpose. As if it were a kind of sport." He turned and looked at her, his face tired and unsure. "I don't know why I'm telling you this."

"Because you saw something on Wednesday that you didn't tell Sam," Greta said.

Lyle's jaw set. For a long moment he did not answer.

"I saw Walt's truck," he said at last. "At quarter past six, on the shore road, going the wrong way. He never goes that way. He always goes out past the breakwater. And he was driving slow, with his lights on, the way a man does when he's looking for something he's lost." Lyle swallowed. "I didn't stop. I thought it was none of my business. Walt's been a fisherman all his life. He does what he likes."

"Walt was going to the Harbor Office," Greta said.

"Or he was coming back from it," Lyle said. "I don't know which. But I've been a dockmaster for twenty years, Greta, and I've never once seen Walt Brandt drive past his own boat without stopping to look at her first."

## Chapter 18: Ted Vance Remembers

Ted Vance came to the lake house on Thursday night, after dark, on foot. Greta heard him before she saw him, a slow, uneven footfall on the gravel of Juniper Lane, and then a pause at the bottom of the porch steps, as though he had walked a long way and was not certain he wanted to climb the rest of the way up. She went to the screen door with the porch light on and found him standing at the foot of the steps in a rumpled blue shirt, his hands in his pockets, his face pale and damp under the light.

"Greta," he said. "I'm sorry. I didn't know who else to come to."

"Come up, Ted," she said gently. "Take a seat before you fall down."

He climbed the steps very slowly and sat on the top one, with his back against the porch column, and she brought him a glass of water from the kitchen and sat in the wicker chair beside him. Judge came out through the screen door, looked at the stranger with mild contempt, and sat down on the boards between them, his tail wrapped around his front feet.

For a long while Ted did not say anything. He drank his water in small, careful sips and stared out over the railing at the dark line of the pines, where the bay glimmered faintly beyond the trees.

"I've been drinking," he said at last. "A little. Not much. Enough to make me say things I shouldn't."

"Then don't say them yet," Greta said. "Wait until you're sober. Or tell me what you'd tell a judge, and then sleep it off."

He laughed, unhappily. "You always were the hardest woman in town, Greta. Even when you were nice to people, they could tell you were keeping score." He set the glass down on the boards. "I went to see Malcolm Pryce on Friday night. The lawyer from Grand Rapids. At the Lakeshore Inn, in the private dining room. Ruth was there. Gordon wasn't. Eleanor Strand came in at the end, to say hello, and then she sat down next to me and put her hand on my arm and told me how much the boardwalk would mean to my grandchildren."

"What did Pryce say?"

"He said the minutes reflected the council's will," Ted said. "He said the council had reached a consensus on the lease at the July fourteenth meeting, and that a clerical question about the vote could be resolved at the special session. He said there were consulting fees available to any council member who helped the process move forward. A retainer, on the side, for the insurance agency I've been running out of my house since my wife died. He said it would be a shame if the agency's license had to be reviewed by the state while the town was dealing with a serious question about its record." He looked at his hands. "He didn't say it like a threat. He said it like he was reading the weather."

"And you said yes."

"I said I'd think about it," Ted said. "And then I went home and sat in my kitchen for three hours. And then I got drunk on a bottle of bourbon I'd been keeping for my brother's funeral. And then I thought about Dale." His voice cracked. "He was the only one of us who ever said what he thought in that room, Greta. Ruth tiptoed around everything. Ed and Carla agreed with whoever was talking last. I'm the one who can't make up his mind. I've been the one who can't make up his mind for eleven years, because it's easier than being the one who gets yelled at."

Greta waited. Ted's breathing had changed, grown slow and shallow, the way it did when a man was getting ready to tell the truth and was afraid it would come out in the wrong order.

"Tell me about the recess," she said quietly.

Ted closed his eyes.

"I went out to the lobby because I couldn't breathe in the room," he said. "It was hot, and Ruth was going on about the survey, and I had my pens in both hands and I couldn't put them down. So I went out the side door, toward the stairs, where the coffee is. I thought I'd get a cup of water and stand in the corridor for a minute. There was nobody there. Just the urn gurgling and the light buzzing over the stairs. And then I heard somebody coming up from the lobby behind me, and I turned around."

"Who?"

"Bernice Oakes," Ted said. "With her bangles. You could hear them all the way down the corridor, like a little chime. She came straight at me. She had her laptop bag over her shoulder and her face very calm, and she stopped about two feet away and smiled at me like we were old friends at a wedding." He opened his eyes. "And she said, *Dale's come round, Ted. He'll withdraw his objection before the vote. When it comes back in, you vote with Ed and Carla and it'll be done.*"

Greta felt the hair stand up along her arms.

"What did you say?"

"I said Dale never withdraws anything in his life," Ted said. "I said it out loud. I think I was trying to be funny. And Bernice laughed, very softly, and she said, *Then he'll be surprised, won't he.* And she patted my arm, Greta. Just patted it, the way you'd pat a dog that had done a trick." He shuddered. "And then she walked past me into the lobby, and I heard her say something to Gordon Strand, and he laughed, and I went back into the room because I couldn't think what else to do."

"You didn't tell Sam."

"I didn't tell anybody," Ted said miserably. "I thought she'd been making a joke. I thought Dale was going to make a speech about it later, and everybody would laugh, and it would be the kind of thing people talk about at the Gull for a week and then forget. Then Dale was dead on the steps, and I was sitting in my kitchen with a bottle of bourbon, and the minutes came out the next day and said he'd withdrawn his objection in the middle of a recess when nobody was listening." He pressed both hands over his face. "And I thought, *oh God, she meant it.* She wasn't joking. She was telling me what was going to be written down."

Judge got up, stretched his front legs, and walked over to Ted's knee. He sat down there, with his face turned up, and after a moment Ted reached down and rested one hand on the cat's back. Judge tolerated this with the resignation of a creature who knew his duty.

"Ted," Greta said. "When you came back into the room. What did you see?"

"Dale," Ted said, without looking up. "He was sitting in his chair with his hands on the table, like he'd been slapped. He wasn't looking at anybody. He just sat there, and his face was the color of paper. And Walt was in the back row with his coffee, not looking at anybody either. And Bernice came in a minute after me and sat right down beside him, and she took out her notebook, and her bangles went *chime, chime, chime* as she opened it." He lifted his head at last and looked at Greta with red-rimmed, desperate eyes. "And when she looked up at the front of the room, she was smiling. Just a little, as if she'd won something she didn't want anyone else to know about."

Greta sat very still in the wicker chair. Across the porch, a moth had found the light and was beating itself softly against the glass.

"Would you say that to Sam?" she asked.

"I already have," Ted said. "This morning, at the diner on Cutter Street. He came in for a coffee and sat down across from me and didn't say a word. He just waited, the way he does, until I started talking. I told him everything. I think he almost cried." He wiped his face with his shirtsleeve. "He said he'd need a statement. I said I'd give it to him on Monday, in the Center, in front of the whole town, if that's what it takes. And then I'm going to stand up at the special session, Greta, and vote the way I should have voted in July. I'm going to vote no."

"That will be difficult."

"Everything's difficult," Ted said. "I've decided I'd rather be difficult than sorry." He almost smiled. "That's something Dale used to say. I stole it from him, the way I've stolen everything else I've ever said in that room."

They sat together for a while in silence. The night air was cool and smelled of pine and water. Far off, across the bay, the lights of a freighter moved very slowly along the horizon, a row of small bright windows on the dark.

"There's one more thing," Ted said at last, very quietly. "I didn't tell Sam this part, because I didn't understand why it mattered until now. When Bernice walked past me in the corridor, she was turning her left wrist the way you do when a bracelet pinches, and the bangles were knocking against each other. I counted them without meaning to. Three. You don't forget a sound like that when it's coming straight at you down a hallway."

Greta looked at him. "Three on Tuesday night."

"Three," Ted said. "And when she came back into the room after the recess, I heard them again, all three, against the arm of the chair." He spread his hands, helplessly. "I don't know what it means that I'm sure of it."

"Neither do I," Greta said slowly. But she was thinking of a white hatchback in a gravel lot, and a bare wrist resting against the edge of a counter, and a silver link lying somewhere under the stairs of a Harbor Office, where nobody had thought to look.

"Walt was in the back row," Ted said. "He saw her come back in. He saw everything. Ask him what he saw on Wednesday morning, Greta, when he took his boat out past the breakwater the way he's done for forty years. I think he saw her there. And I think he's been waiting for somebody to ask him properly."

He stood up slowly, one hand on the porch column, and looked down at Judge, who looked back at him with the weary dignity of a cat who had decided, after careful consideration, that this particular human might be all right after all.

"Thank you," Ted said. "For listening. I know it wasn't your job."

"It's the only job I've ever had," Greta said.

He nodded, went down the steps, and walked off along Juniper Lane into the dark, one hand trailing along the pine trunks as he went, as though to be sure of his way home.


## Chapter 19: Walt's Six O'Clock

The *Mabel Rose* was a thirty-two-foot wooden fishing boat with a cabin the size of a pantry, a varnished tiller worn pale by sixty years of hands, and a name painted in faded blue letters across her transom. She rode at the end of the north dock, third slip from the fuel pump, exactly where Walt had said she would be. When Greta came down the gangway at a quarter to six on Friday morning, the water was still flat and gray, and the mist lay in low wisps over the surface of the basin like something that had not yet decided whether to rise.

Walt was sitting in the cockpit with the cabin door open and a tin mug of coffee in both hands. He did not look surprised to see her. He lifted the mug an inch in greeting, and then he lifted his chin toward the open hatch, and Greta understood that she was invited.

"Mind your head," Walt said. "She was built for men who were shorter than they should have been."

The cabin smelled of tar, old wool, and diesel, and of the sharp, clean coffee he had brewed in a dented pot on a two-burner stove. A bench ran along one side under a row of small round portholes. A chart of the lake hung on the far wall, its corners curling, with a dozen red pencil circles marking places where something important had happened in forty years of fishing. On the table, under a clip, lay a single sheet of lined paper, covered in Walt's blocky, careful handwriting.

Greta sat down on the bench. Walt poured her a cup from the pot without asking and set it down in front of her, then sat across from her and folded his big weathered hands around his own mug.

"You're going to ask me about Wednesday," he said.

"Yes," Greta said.

"I thought you might." He looked at the chart on the wall, not at her. "I've been waiting for somebody to ask me since the funeral. I thought Sam would come. I thought Ted Vance would tell it, the way he tells everything, with his whole chest. But nobody's come. So I wrote it down for myself last night, so I wouldn't have to say it twice."

He turned the sheet of paper around on the table and slid it across to her. Greta read it slowly, in the gray light from the portholes.

*Tues. 5 p.m. Gull. Dale says, be my witness at six at the Harbor Office. Says he wants somebody who saw the room. I said yes.*

*Wednesday. 6 a.m. Got up at 5. Drove the shore road. Passed Dale on Pier Street, walking. He had his hands in his pockets. He lifted one hand. I lifted mine. He looked like a man on the way to a fight.*

*6:12. Harbor Office lot. White car behind the hedge, nose in, by the fuel tanks. Hatchback. Red light cracked on the left side. Nobody in it.*

*6:14. Remained in the truck with the engine running. Thought, I'll wait for Dale. Thought, I'll wait a minute more.*

*6:20. Light on in the back room. Shadow in the window. Thought it was Dale. Did not go in.*

*6:31. Heard something. Sounded like a chair going over. Or a door. Remained there. Did not go in.*

*6:40. Left the truck. Went to the boat. Drank a cup of coffee. Did not go in.*

Greta looked up from the page. Walt's face was turned toward the portholes, where the first thin light of the morning was creeping over the water.

"I didn't go in," he said quietly. "That's what it says, Greta. Four times. I wrote it four times, like a schoolboy doing punishment lines. I didn't go in."

"Walt," Greta said gently.

"I'm seventy-seven years old," Walt said. "I've been on this water since before Dale Whitcomb had a mustache. I've pulled men out of the lake who were drowning and I've taken a hook out of my own hand with a pair of pliers and a bottle of rum. I'm not afraid of a set of concrete steps." He turned his mug slowly on the table. "But I was afraid of that car. And I was afraid of walking through the back door of the Harbor Office at six in the morning, to stand next to a man who'd asked me to be his witness, and find out I was too late to be anything but the old fool who came to watch."

"You were early, Walt," Greta said. "Not late."

"I was sitting in my truck," Walt said flatly. "With the engine running, like a coward, while a woman sat in a white car behind the hedge and waited for him to come through the door."

Greta reached across the table and put her hand over his. His skin was cold and rough as bark.

"Tell me about the car," she said. "All of it. Exactly what you saw."

Walt took a long breath and let it out slowly.

"It was nose-in behind the hedge, the way you park when you don't want to be seen from the road," he said. "The fuel tanks are on the other side, so you can't see it from the marina. I only saw it because I'd turned around at the end of Pier Street and come back, to look at the back lot. It was a little white hatchback with a dent in the rear door on the passenger side. I could see the dent, because the sun was just coming up over the breakwater and it caught the metal. And the left taillight was cracked straight across, the way you'd crack a plate if you dropped it on a tile floor. I noticed that because a cracked light is hard to miss, and I was thinking that Walt Brandt was an old man who'd seen enough broken things for one morning."

"Was anybody in the car?"

"Not when I first looked. The windows were fogged a little, and I couldn't see through the back glass." He frowned, concentrating. "I looked at the Harbor Office. There was a light on in the back room, the one with the window that looks onto the landing. I could see a shadow move across the blind. Just the once. It might have been a man. It might have been a coat on a hook."

"And at six-thirty?"

"I heard something," Walt said. "Through the truck window, with the engine running. A kind of bang. It sounded like a chair going over on a hard floor, or a door swinging shut too hard. And then a shout, but it might have been a gull." He shook his head slowly. "I told myself it was a gull. I've been telling myself that for a week."

Greta waited.

"And then I got out of the truck," Walt said, "and I didn't go in."

The mist had begun to lift off the water. Outside the portholes, a pair of cormorants drifted across the basin, low and silent, looking for fish.

"Did you see the car leave?" Greta asked.

Walt was silent for a long moment.

"Yes," he said. "At six-forty. I'd gone back to the boat, and I was in the cabin with the door open, and I heard the engine start. I came up into the cockpit to see. I could just make out the back of the Harbor Office through the hedge, and the car came around the far side of the building, fast, the way nobody drives on Pier Street because of the gravel. It went right past the end of the north dock, not thirty yards from where I'm sitting. And the woman driving it had one hand on the wheel and the other pressed against her face." He stopped. "I couldn't tell whether she was crying or laughing. I thought she might be sick. She looked like somebody who's just been told a joke she doesn't understand."

"You saw her face?"

"Only for a second," Walt said. "She turned her head toward the water, and I saw her profile through the windshield. Gray cardigan. Hair pinned up at the back. And the bangles on her left wrist, flashing in the sun when she moved her hand away from her cheek. I'd know that sound anywhere. I sat next to it for three hours on Tuesday night." He looked down at the table. "I'm sorry, Greta. I should have said so on Wednesday. I should have said so to Sam when he came around asking who'd been on the shore road."

"You didn't know what you'd seen," Greta said.

"I knew exactly what I'd seen," Walt said. "That's the trouble with getting old. You stop being surprised by things, and you start being ashamed of the ones you let happen."

He stood up slowly, bent almost double under the low cabin roof, and took the coffee pot off the stove. He filled his own mug, and then, after a pause, filled Greta's, which she had not touched. He set it in front of her with a hand that trembled, very slightly.

"Dale asked me to be his witness," Walt said, sitting down again. "On Tuesday, at the Gull. He came in at five o'clock with his phone in his hand, and he was so angry he couldn't hold still. He said, *They're going to make me sound like a fool, Walt. I need somebody who was in that room to stand next to me when I say so.* And I said, all right, I'll be there. Six o'clock, the back door. I'd have been there, Greta, if I'd had the nerve to walk up the steps."

"He called me at nine forty-one," Greta said quietly. "He didn't know what time he'd meet you. He only knew he wanted the tape."

Walt nodded slowly. "He didn't wait for either of us," he said. "He was never any good at waiting."

They sat in silence for a while. The cabin was warm now, and the coffee was bitter and strong. Greta looked at the sheet of paper on the table, with its four careful lines of *didn't go in*, and thought of a clerk who had typed a sentence she did not understand, and a man who had walked up Pier Street with his hands in his pockets, looking like a man on the way to a fight.

"Walt," she said. "Will you say this to Sam? Today?"

"I'll say it to Sam," Walt said. "And I'll say it to the sheriff, and to Ruth Kalinowski, and to Gordon Strand's lawyer if he comes around asking. I'll say it at the special session on Monday, in front of the whole town." He picked up the sheet of paper and folded it carefully in half. "I'll say I was a coward, and I'll say I watched a woman drive away from a dead man with her hand on her face. And then I'll sit down in the back row and let them decide what to do with an old fisherman who should have walked up the steps."

"They won't decide you're the one at fault," Greta said.

"They might," Walt said, without self-pity. "But it'll be true, won't it? And I'd rather be true than comfortable." He tucked the folded paper into the breast pocket of his flannel shirt and buttoned the flap over it. "Dale would have liked that. He used to say I was the only man in Lantern Bay who never told him a single pleasant lie."

Outside, the first boat of the morning was starting its engine at the fuel dock, a steady, rising rumble that carried across the water. The light had turned gold on the breakwater. Greta drank her coffee slowly, and then she stood up, her head bent under the cabin roof, and went up the companionway into the morning.

Walt did not follow her. He sat on the bench in the warm cabin with his hands folded on the table, looking at the lake chart on the wall, and she heard him say, very quietly, to no one at all, "Four times. I wrote it four times."

## Chapter 20: The Silver Link

Sam Ortiz was waiting for her at the foot of the Harbor Office steps at eight o'clock on Saturday morning, sitting on the bottom step with a thermos of coffee beside him and his hat on the concrete next to his knee. He had not shaved. He wore a plain gray T-shirt and a pair of faded jeans, and he did not look like a deputy. He looked like a man who had not slept.

"Walt called me at six-thirty," he said, before she had reached the bottom of the walk. "He said you'd been to see him. He said he'd tell me everything in writing by noon, and then he hung up before I could say anything useful." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I've had two hours of sleep, Greta. I've been up since three reading Dana Reyes's draft report on the kitchen table, and I've been thinking about your old man in the boat. I'm not sure I've ever been so angry at a seventy-seven-year-old in my life."

"He's not the one you should be angry at," Greta said.

"I know that." Sam held out the thermos. "Coffee. It's terrible. Lyle made it."

She took a cup. It was terrible. She drank it anyway.

Sam looked up at the back of the Harbor Office, where the landing stood empty in the early light and the iron railing still bore its bright new scrape. "Dana called me last night," he said. "She's finished the fall analysis. She won't sign the final report until Monday, because the county wants a second opinion from the state lab. But she told me what she thinks, off the record." He paused. "She says the bruising on his upper arms is consistent with two hands gripping him from the front, hard enough to leave a pattern. She says the abrasions on his right palm look like he was trying to grab the railing as he went over backward. She says the fall would have been enough to kill him, given the heart condition, but that if somebody had pushed him, the manner of death would change from *accident* to *undetermined*, and then to *homicide*, depending on what the lab finds." He looked at her. "She wants me to find out whether he was pushed, Greta. She says that's not her job. That's mine."

Greta set her coffee cup down on the bottom step.

"Then we'd better look at the stairs," she said.

They searched the way Sam had been trained to search, which meant slowly, with latex gloves and a flashlight in the bright morning, and with the same stubborn attention to the wrong places that Greta remembered from a dozen courtroom depositions. Sam went up first and worked the landing, the railing, and the threshold of the back door. He photographed the bent iron bar on the lower half of the flight, where the bright scrape ran from the top of the upright to the joint. He photographed the concrete at the top of the stairs, a little pitted and stained from the rain. He photographed the back door's frame, where a faint brown smear marked the wood below the handle.

Greta went down the stairs.

She went one step at a time, the way she had counted them on the first morning, with one hand on the railing and her eyes on the concrete. The steps were rough and gray and had been swept clean by the rain. At the fourth step from the bottom she stopped, because the railing was loose, and when she put her weight on it, the whole upright shifted with a dull metallic groan, and the bolt at its base rattled in its socket.

"Sam," she called. "Look at this."

He came down the steps behind her and crouched beside the bolt. The concrete around it had cracked in a small, ragged half-moon, and a fine crust of rust dust had fallen out onto the step below. He looked at it for a long time.

"Dale wrote about this," Greta said quietly. "In the notebook. *Rail's loose at the bottom. Tell Lyle.* He knew it was loose. He'd known it for weeks. He was planning to check it on Wednesday morning, before the tide, because he'd have walked down these steps every day for forty years and he never once thought the railing would hold a man who didn't want to be held."

Sam's jaw tightened. He took a photograph of the bolt, and then of the step beneath it, and then of the drainage grate at the bottom of the flight, where a rusted iron grille lay flat across a shallow channel of water and gravel. The grate ran the full width of the stairs, a little below the lowest step, to carry the rain away from the boat ramp. Beneath it, the channel was full of dark, silty mud and a tangle of old seaweed.

"Hold this," Sam said, and handed her the flashlight.

He knelt on the lowest step with his face inches from the grate and began to work his gloved fingers into the gap at one end, lifting the grille a fraction of an inch at a time, while Greta shone the light down into the channel. The iron groaned. A small stone rolled out and splashed into the mud. Sam paused, breathing hard, and then lifted the grille another inch and reached in.

He drew out his hand slowly, and in the middle of his palm, glinting dully in the morning light, lay a short length of thin silver metal, no longer than a finger, bent almost into a circle, with a small, neat clasp at one end and a jagged break at the other.

Greta did not breathe.

"That's a bangle," Sam said quietly. "Or part of one. Look at the join. Somebody's bracelet came apart, and the link went down a crack." He turned the metal in the light, carefully, as though it might still be warm. "It's been in the mud for a week. It's fine. The silver's not tarnished much, because the water's been running over it. It would have come apart with some force, Greta. Not with a fall. A hand pulling away."

"Or a hand grabbing," Greta said.

"Or a hand grabbing." Sam closed his fingers around the link and stood up slowly, his knees cracking. "I'm going to bag it and tag it, and then I'm going to take it to the lab in Lansing myself, and I'm going to tell them to match the silver and the join against the broken bangle on the wrist of every woman in this county who wears one." He looked at the bolt in the railing, then up at the empty landing, then down at Greta. "And I'm going to tell Corliss he can keep his file open until Christmas."

"Will he listen?"

"No," Sam said. "But he'll read it." He put the link into a small plastic evidence bag and sealed it, then wrote on the label with a black marker, carefully, in his square deputy's capitals, the date, the time, and the location. *7/25 8:41 AM. Bottom step, harbor office, beneath drainage grate. Silver link, bangle type, broken.* He signed his name and handed the bag to Greta, who signed beneath it, as witness.

She looked at her own signature for a moment. It looked steady.

"There's something else," Sam said. He had taken a folded sheet of paper from his back pocket and was holding it in both hands, not quite looking at her. "Tidewater emailed the county yesterday afternoon. I didn't tell you, because I was waiting until I'd read it twice. They sent the access logs for the Harbor Office back door. The whole month of July, all the codes." He hesitated. "There's one entry I can't explain, and I'd rather not explain it alone."

"Show me," Greta said.

He held out the paper. She took it, and read the lines he had highlighted in yellow, the rows of plain text with their neat columns of codes, dates, and times.

*07/15/2026 05:58:21 — PIN 0419 — Entry — Back door — Assigned: Oakes Transcription (after-hours TownShare upload) — Reissued: D. Park.*

*07/15/2026 06:14:07 — PIN 2214 — Entry — Front door — Assigned: D. Whitcomb (Commission).*

Greta read them twice. Then she looked up at the back of the Harbor Office, at the one window with its blind half drawn, and at the landing with its loose railing and its bright new scrape.

"Five fifty-eight," she said. "She came in at five fifty-eight, with a code that had been reissued to her office manager. Dale came in at six-fourteen through the front, with his own key code. Walt saw her car at six-twelve." She swallowed. "And the file was gone by six-thirty-one."

"You know that?" Sam asked sharply.

"I know that Helen's audit log will show it, if anyone asks for it," Greta said. "And I know that a woman who was home by nine-thirty on Tuesday night doesn't have a reason to be in the Harbor Office at a quarter to six on Wednesday morning, unless she was there to take something away."

"Denise Park," Sam said. "The office manager. Reissued. You're going to tell me she was the one who came in at five fifty-eight, aren't you?"

"I'm not going to tell you anything," Greta said. "I'm going to ask you to find out where Denise Park was at five fifty-eight on Wednesday morning, and who she gave that code card to, and whether she ever got it back."

Sam was quiet for a long moment. Then he folded the logs, very carefully, and slipped them into the inside pocket of his T-shirt, as though he were putting away a letter.

"I've called Denise," he said. "This morning, before I came down here. She's coming to the county office in Carrow City on Sunday afternoon to give me a statement. Her lawyer's going to be there." He looked at Greta. "She told me she gave the code card to Bernice on Tuesday afternoon, for a late delivery of transcripts to the Harbor Office. She said she never got it back. She said she was in Fennick Falls all Wednesday morning, at home, asleep, with her daughter, and she has a text message from her daughter's school to prove it." He paused. "She also said she'd been worried for a month that Bernice was going to get her into trouble. She said Bernice has been asking everybody for keys and codes since June, and nobody in the office was ever allowed to say no."

"Did she say why?"

"She said Bernice told her the town was going to need the codes to get the record back." Sam's voice was flat. "She said Bernice was very calm about it. She said it was the calm that frightened her most."

Greta thought of a woman in a dark blue blouse, sitting at the head of a round table with a binder in front of her, speaking in a voice of perfect patience about artifacts and gain events and the things a judge needed to hear.

"Sam," she said quietly. "The judge will need more than a code and a bangle."

"I know," Sam said. "I'm going to Judge Vickers on Sunday afternoon, with everything I've got. The logs. The bangle. Walt's four lines. Tessa's photographs. Ted's statement, which he's giving me this afternoon. Helen's approval time, and the invoice, and the purchase order that says *per G. Strand*." He tucked his hands into his back pockets. "It's not enough to arrest anybody. But it might be enough to look in a trunk."

"Corliss?"

"Corliss's going to call me a fool," Sam said. "And then he's going to sign whatever I put in front of him, because he doesn't want a dead man's son in his office asking why nobody did anything." He shrugged, tiredly. "That's how this town works, Greta. The people with money are very polite. And the people with the power to stop them are very tired."

They walked back up the back steps together, past the bent railing and the open door, and out onto the gravel where the sun was already hot on the shell. Sam stopped at the corner of the hedge, where a white hatchback had once been parked nose-in, and looked at the bare ground for a long moment.

"She was here," he said quietly. "She was sitting right there, with the engine running, while he walked up Pier Street."

"And then she went in," Greta said.

Sam did not answer. He only turned and looked at the open back door, where the light fell out across the landing in its long pale stripe, just as it had on the first morning, and Greta saw his face change, from weariness to a kind of hard, cold resolve, the look of a man who has finally decided not to be polite any longer.

"Then I'm going to find out what she did when she got there," he said.

## Chapter 21: The Keypad Log

Denise Park came to the Carrow County office on Sunday afternoon in a navy blue sundress, with a lawyer in a gray suit and a daughter of about nine who sat in the hallway with a handheld video game and did not look up once. The lawyer was a thin, tired man named Peter Haas, who had been the public defender in Carrow City for twenty years and who had clearly been told to expect the worst. He sat beside his client in a room with a scratched table and a window unit that rattled, and he did not say much while she spoke.

Greta was not in the room. She had no business there. But Sam had asked her to wait in the corridor, on a bench beside the water fountain, in case Denise needed a glass of water or a second person to say that she had been telling the truth about the lunch break. Greta sat with her canvas bag on her knees and the tape folded inside it, and she listened through the thin wooden door to a voice that was careful and frightened and very precise.

Denise said that she had been office manager for Oakes Transcription for six years. She said that Bernice had given her a key card and a four-digit code for the Lantern Bay TownShare system in March, when the town's contract was signed, because the after-hours uploads had to be made from a secure account and Bernice did not trust anyone else with the password. She said that the card was supposed to be kept in the office safe. She said that on Tuesday, July fourteen, at about three o'clock, Bernice had come into the front room of the office with her laptop bag over her shoulder and asked her, very pleasantly, for the card, because she was driving to Lantern Bay that evening and wanted to be able to upload a late transcript from the Harbor Office if she needed to.

"Did you give it to her?" Sam asked.

"I gave it to her," Denise said. "I didn't want to. I said, *Bernice, what about the safe?* And she said, *Denise, you know I'll bring it back before you open on Thursday.* And she smiled at me. And I gave it to her."

"Did she bring it back?"

"No." A pause. "She came back to the office on Wednesday at noon, from Lantern Bay. I was in the front room. She walked straight past me to her office and shut the door. She didn't say anything about the card. I was too scared to ask."

"Scared of what?"

"Of her," Denise said quietly. "She was calm. She's always calm. But she had a cut on her right hand, across the knuckles, and she'd wrapped it in a paper towel and she kept touching it, like she didn't know it was there. And when I asked her what happened, she said she'd caught it on a fence on Pier Street." Another pause. "I've been trying not to think about that for a week."

The lawyer said something quiet, and Denise's voice rose slightly and then fell again.

Sam asked her where she had been on Wednesday morning at five fifty-eight, and Denise said she had been at home in Fennick Falls, in bed with her daughter, who had come into her room with a nightmare at five-thirty and had stayed there until seven. ## Chapter 22: A Note in the Mailbox

The mailbox for the lake house stood at the mouth of Juniper Lane, a battered black box on a cedar post that leaned a little toward the road, as if it were listening for the mail carrier's truck. Greta had driven past it a thousand times without looking. On Sunday evening she stopped the car beside it with the engine still running and the late sun slanting through the pines, and she sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, thinking of nothing at all, before she got out.

The box was not empty.

A plain white envelope lay across the bottom, folded once to fit, with no stamp and no return address. Her name was typed on the front in the centered, careful style of a letter prepared for a formal occasion. *Greta Lowe.* Not another word.

She stood at the post with the envelope in her hand and did not open it right away. The gravel was warm under her shoes. Somewhere in the trees a woodpecker was working steadily at a dead trunk, and the sound carried down the lane like a slow, patient clock.

Then she opened the envelope with her thumbnail, the way she had been taught never to open anything in a hurry.

Inside was a single card, heavy and cream-colored, with a deckled edge. She knew the stock at once. She had seen it on Tuesday in a bunch of white lilies on a casket, tucked into the arrangement with a card that said *With deepest respect*. This card said nothing as elaborate. It had been typed on the same kind of paper, and in the same clean serif font that had appeared on every page of the draft minutes, and on the transcription firm's invoice that Helen Marsh had shown her in the minivan.

*Leave the minutes alone, Greta. The record is settled. You have been kind to this town for a long time. Don't let it be unkind to you.*

There was no signature. There was no need for one.

Greta read it twice, slowly, holding the card up to the last of the light. She looked at the letters, one by one, the way a reporter examines a typeface to tell one machine from another. The *e* in *Leave* sat a hair higher than the other letters on the line, as if it had been struck a fraction too early. The same *e* appeared in *settled*, and again in *kindly*, and once more, faintly, in the word *unkind*. She had seen that *e* on invoices for five years. She had seen it on the bottom of the courthouse transcripts that Bernice had typed when they were both young and angry at each other across a long hallway.

She turned the card over. The back was blank.

For a while she simply stood there, with the card in one hand and the envelope in the other, listening to the woodpecker and the faint hiss of the lake beyond the pines. The lane was empty. A single car had passed on County Route 9 a few minutes earlier, its headlights flickering through the trees like a lantern being carried away. The rest stayed still.

Then she took out her phone and called Sam.

He answered on the second ring. His voice was hoarse and tight, the voice of a man who had been on the phone for six hours with people who did not want to hear what he had to say.

"Greta," he said. "I was about to call you. Judge Vickers signed it. Five-fifty this afternoon. The warrant covers the Oakes Transcription offices in Fennick Falls, the white hatchback, and the workstation OT-03. I'm going down there at seven tomorrow morning with two of Carrow County's deputies and a locksmith who used to be a Marine, and Denise Park is going to meet us at the front door with her key." He let out a breath. "Corliss called me a hundred and nine names, and then he asked me to send him a copy of the warrant so he could tell the state police we'd done it properly. I think that's the nicest thing he's ever said to me."

"Sam," Greta said. "There's a card in my mailbox."

Silence on the line. Then: "Repeat that."

She read him the words. She read him the description of the typeface, and the *e* that sat too high. She did not have to describe the paper. He could hear it in her voice.

"Don't touch it," Sam said quickly. "Don't put it back in the envelope, either. Put it on the front seat of your car, face up, on a clean newspaper, and then get in the car and lock the doors. Is your cat inside?"

"Yes."

"Then go in and lock the house. Then stay there. Don't go out to the mailbox again. I'm sending a patrol car to your road, and I'm going to call Corliss back and tell him I want someone on Juniper Lane all night." His voice was flat and very controlled. "Greta. This is the first time anyone's threatened you in writing. I don't like it."

"It isn't a threat," Greta said. "It's advice."

"That's what makes it a threat," Sam said. "People who write threats don't usually bother with the nice paper."

She drove the last quarter mile very slowly with the card on the passenger seat beside her, face up, on a copy of the *Lantern Bay Gazette* folded to the classifieds. Her hands were perfectly steady on the wheel. It was only when she reached the gravel drive and turned off the engine that she realized her heart was pounding so hard she could feel it in her teeth.

She let herself into the house. Judge met her at the top of the hall, as he always did, and walked beside her all the way to the kitchen with his tail held straight up, as though nothing whatsoever had happened in the world that day. She locked the front door behind her. She locked the back door. She checked the latch on the screen porch and the window over the sink and the little window in the spare room, which had a cracked pane she had been meaning to replace since spring. Then she stood in the middle of the kitchen with her hands flat on the counter, breathing slowly, and listened to the silence of the house.

It was not a comfortable silence. It was the kind a house makes when it knows something is standing outside it.

At a quarter to seven, a patrol car rolled slowly past the end of the lane, its spotlight sweeping the pines. It did not stop. It came back a few minutes later and went past again, and then a third time, and each time Greta saw the shape of the officer behind the wheel turn his head toward her house. She did not wave. She did not need to. The car stayed at the bottom of the lane until after dark, its engine idling, its single bright eye fixed on the road.

At eight-thirty, another vehicle came up the hill from the direction of Front Street. It was a small silver sedan, moving at a crawl, with a crumpled front bumper and a child's booster seat visible through the rear window. It slowed at the bottom of Juniper Lane, very near the patrol car, and stopped for a long moment. Greta could see the driver's head through the windshield, pale in the dusk. Then the sedan turned around, carefully, in a three-point maneuver along the shoulder, and drove back down the hill toward town.

Greta stood at the living room window with her hand on the curtain and watched it go.

Her phone buzzed on the sill. It was a text, from a number she had saved only that week, after a conversation in a silver car outside a bait shop.

*I drove past. I didn't want to come in. The lights were on. I'm glad. T.*

Greta typed a reply with one thumb. *Thank you. Come to the Center on Monday night. Be with Walt.*

The response came back almost at once, two words.

*I will.*

She set the phone down and went to the kitchen to feed the cat, who had been sitting by his empty bowl for some time with an expression of quiet, professional reproach.

## Chapter 23: Judge Goes Missing

She did not sleep in the bedroom. She had decided that at nine o'clock, when the patrol car's headlights had finally disappeared up the road, and she had taken her pillow and a blanket down to the couch in the front room and laid the lamp on the floor beside it, turned low. She lay awake for a long time listening to the house tick and settle. The clock in the hall said ten-fifteen, then eleven, then a little after midnight. Somewhere far off on the bay, a foghorn sounded twice, long and low, the way it always did on the nights when the mist came in off the water.

Judge had been asleep at the foot of the couch when she lay down. He had stretched, looked at her for a long moment with his eyes half closed, and then curled into a gray ball against her feet, purring like a small engine. She had fallen asleep with one hand resting on his warm back.

She woke at a quarter past two because the purring had stopped.

For a moment she lay perfectly still, not sure what had woken her. Then she heard it again, a sound from the back of the house, faint and muffled, the sound of a window sash being eased very slowly up its track. It stopped. Then it began again. Then a soft, heavy thump, like a sack of flour set down on a wooden floor.

Greta sat up. The room was dark except for the faint gray glow from the window and the streetlight at the end of the lane. The couch beside her was empty. Judge was not curled at her feet. He was not on the armchair, or the rug, or the sill of the front window, where he liked to watch the moths.

"Judge," she whispered.

Not a word.

She got up as quietly as she could, took the heavy brass lamp off the floor and held it by its base like a club, and moved down the hall toward the spare room with her bare feet silent on the boards. The door was open. She had shut it before she went to bed. She was certain of that.

She pushed the door wide and reached for the light switch.

The room was cold. The window over the desk was open a foot, and a damp breath of night air came through it, carrying the smell of the lake and cut grass. The screen had been lifted out of its frame and set neatly on the lawn below, where she could see it, pale in the moonlight. The desk was disordered. Her machine case lay open on its side. The bench under the window, where her quilt had been folded for months, was empty, and the old stack of yellow legal paper that had lain there since the night of the tape, the careful handwritten record she had made at two in the morning, with its capitals and its time marks and the single line at the bottom, was gone.

She stood in the doorway and stared at the empty bench.

Beside it, on the floor, lay a small square of cream-colored card, face up, with its deckled edge catching the light from the hall.

She did not pick it up. She went down on her knees beside it, carefully, and read it where it lay.

*Some records are better left unread. We have all been kind to Lantern Bay. Let the town rest.*

No signature. But in the bottom corner, very small, pressed into the paper rather than printed on it, was a shape she recognized from the lilies at the church and the letterhead on Gordon Strand's invoices: a small, stylized sailboat under a single star.

"Oh, you clever woman," Greta whispered.

She stood up slowly. Her knees ached. Her heart was going hard again, but her mind had gone very still and clear, the way it did in a courtroom when a witness made a mistake that would be fatal to them and had not yet noticed.

Someone had come through the window while she slept on the couch twenty feet away. Someone had lifted the screen, and climbed in, and walked past the desk and the bench and the box of old steno notes, and taken the one thing in the house that nobody had seen except herself and Priya and Sam Ortiz: a handwritten record of a meeting, in capital letters, with a single line at the bottom that said the council had not voted.

And they had left the card behind, because they had wanted her to know who had sent them.

She went back down the hall to the front room and turned on every light in the house. She checked the kitchen. She checked the back porch. She checked under the dining table and behind the piano and in the linen closet, calling her cat's name in a low, steady voice, the way she had called him in from the lake every evening for nine years.

No answer.

She went out the front door onto the porch in her bare feet. The night air was cold on her skin. The mist had come up from the bay and lay in long pale threads through the pines, so that the lane seemed to vanish a few yards from the house. The patrol car was still at the bottom of the hill. She could see its single white light, glowing faintly through the fog, and she could hear the low, patient murmur of its engine.

"Judge," she called. "Judge, come on, it's late."

Something moved at the end of the porch, under the steps, in the dark gap between the lattice and the ground.

She went down on her knees on the cold boards and peered into the gap. Two green eyes looked back at her, very steady, very bright.

"There you are," she breathed.

The cat did not move. He stared at her with the stern, wounded dignity of a magistrate who had been kept waiting in court for an hour, and then he yawned, enormously, and turned his head away from her with an air of deep and lasting offense.

Greta began to cry then, quietly, with her hands over her face, kneeling on the cold porch in the fog. It was not a long cry. It was over before she had quite understood it had started. When she lifted her head, Judge had come out from under the steps and was sitting a few feet away, looking at her with his tail wrapped neatly around his front paws, as if nothing had happened and he had never once been afraid.

"Come here," Greta said, and held out her arms.

He came, grudgingly, and let her pick him up, and she held him against her chest with his heart beating hard under her hand, and felt him shivering. There was a thin, fresh scratch across the bridge of his nose, a little pink line where his fur had been pushed aside by something sharp. She touched it gently. He tolerated that too.

"Somebody came in through the window," she said softly. "And you hid. Good. That's exactly what I'd have done."

She carried him inside, shut the front door, and locked it behind her. Then she sat down on the couch with the cat in her lap and called Sam Ortiz, and when he answered on the first ring, his voice thick with sleep, she told him everything in a flat, level voice, the way she would have read a transcript aloud to a judge.

When she had finished, there was a long silence on the line.

"Don't move," Sam said at last. "I'm coming now. Don't touch anything else in that room. And Greta?"

"Yes?"

"Lock the door behind me this time. Then unlock it when I knock. Three times, the way I did when I was twelve and you used to let me sit in the courthouse library after school." He breathed out slowly. "I should have been out there tonight. I'm sorry."

"You were doing your job," Greta said.

"I was asleep," Sam said. "On my kitchen floor, with the warrant on the table. Corliss's going to have a fit when he hears this. He's going to say it proves Bernice Oakes is a harmless fool who wanted her old notes back. And then he's going to call Malcolm Pryce, and Malcolm Pryce is going to call Gordon Strand, and Gordon Strand is going to pretend he never heard of a sailboat with a star on it." He was silent for a moment. "I'm going to be on the porch in twenty minutes. Put the kettle on."

He arrived in eighteen. He came up the steps in a rumpled county jacket over a white T-shirt, his hair flattened on one side, his face gray with fatigue. He knocked three times. Greta unlocked the door and let him in, and he stood in the hall with his hat in his hands, looking at the open window in the spare room through the doorway, and then at the cat, who had come down the hall behind her and was now sitting in the middle of the carpet, washing one front paw with grave concentration.

"He's fine," Greta said.

"I can see that," Sam said. He looked at the cat for a long moment, and something in his face softened. "Good. That's one thing, anyway."

He went into the spare room and stood beside the desk with his hands on his hips, looking at the cream card on the floor. He did not touch it. He crouched beside it, very carefully, and tilted his head to read the sailboat in the corner.

"Strand Lakeside," he said quietly. "The old Bait and Tackle sign. Matches the letterhead on the invoice Helen showed me." He straightened slowly. "Greta, somebody in that house wants you to know they were in your bedroom. And they want you to know they took your handwriting."

"Yes," Greta said.

"Who knew you'd written it out by hand?"

Greta thought for a moment. "Priya," she said. "You. Me. The paper was on the bench for four days. Anyone who came into this house could have seen it, if they'd looked."

"Did anybody come into this house in the last week?"

"Tessa Quill, once, in the afternoon, with a box of lemon bars from Priya," Greta said slowly. "Ted Vance, last Thursday, on foot, after dark. Priya, most days. You, on Saturday, with the bad coffee." She paused. "And Eleanor Strand, at the church door on Saturday, not in the house at all. She asked whether I'd take a cup of tea in the parish hall after the service. I said no. She stood there a long time after that, looking at my face."

Sam was silent. Then he took out his notebook and wrote the names down, one under another, in his careful capitals.

"Eleanor Strand," he said, and underlined the name twice. "She knew about the handwriting?"

"She knew I was writing things down," Greta said. "She's been watching me since the first meeting. The whole town has."

Sam closed the notebook and tucked it back into his shirt pocket. Then he looked at the cream card on the floor again, and at the little sailboat, and at the window with its screen lying out on the lawn in the first gray light of the morning.

"Whoever did this," he said quietly, "didn't come to steal a piece of paper, Greta. They came to tell you that they could walk into your house while you slept. That's a message for somebody who's made up her mind to be brave." He took out a pair of evidence gloves from his jacket pocket and snapped them on. "Now I'm going to take the card and the screen and the photographs, and then I'm going to drive to Pier Street and have a conversation with a man who sleeps in a very large bed."

"Don't let him bully you," Greta said.

"He's never once in his life needed to bully anybody," Sam said. "He just sends his wife."

## Chapter 24: Helen's Phone Call

The phone rang at twenty to seven on Monday morning. Greta was still on the couch, with the cat asleep on her chest and the front door locked and a cup of cold coffee balanced on the arm of the cushion. Sam had left at half past five, after an hour of careful photographs and a long, quiet conversation on the porch about the drive to Fennick Falls. The patrol car had gone with him. The lane was empty except for the first birds in the pines and the slow, hopeful gleam of the sun on the lake.

She reached for the phone without looking. The name on the screen made her sit up so fast that Judge slid off her chest and landed on the rug with an offended thump.

"Helen?"

"Greta." Helen's voice was very quiet and very steady, the voice of a woman who had been awake all night and had decided, somewhere around three in the morning, that she was done being afraid. "I'm sorry to call so early. I didn't know if you'd be up."

"I'm up," Greta said. "Are you all right?"

"No," Helen said. "But I'm sitting in my kitchen with a cup of tea and Frank is asleep upstairs, and I've been reading a letter that came in the town mail yesterday afternoon. I didn't open it until an hour ago. It's from Malcolm Pryce. On his firm's letterhead, with a very expensive seal." A dry, humorless sound. "It says that my approval of the July fourteenth draft minutes was *a matter of record, and not subject to unauthorized disclosure*. It says that if I give the town's audit log to *any party not authorized by the Town Attorney*, the firm will consider me personally liable for the cost of the special session, and for any injury to Strand Lakeside Partners's reputation in the community."

"He's threatening you," Greta said.

"He's threatening me in a very polite letter," Helen said. "On paper I could frame. He thinks I'm a tired clerk who will fold the first time a lawyer uses a long word." Her voice cracked slightly, and then steadied. "I'm not going to fold, Greta. I'm going to go to the Center tonight and I'm going to sit in the chair beside Ruth and tell the whole town what I did. I've already called the town manager. He's on a plane. He said, *Helen, don't you dare say anything to anybody until I land.* And I said, *Dale would have wanted me to.*" She drew a breath. "I've been thinking about Dale a lot this morning. About the way he looked at me in the clerk's office when he asked for the key. As though I were a clerk who'd been given a key and forgotten what it was for."

"You're not that," Greta said gently.

"I hope I'm not." Helen was quiet for a moment. "There's something else. I didn't want to tell Sam, because I'm not sure it means what I think it means. The letter from Pryce came with an attachment. A ledger. Strand Lakeside's accounts payable, for June and July. He must have sent it by mistake, with the wrong page, or meant to send it to somebody else. It's twelve pages. Most of it's landscaping and insurance. But there's a line on the seventeenth of June, and another on the fifteenth of July, both to Oakes Transcription, both for *document services*. The first was for three thousand dollars. The second was for four thousand five hundred."

Greta closed her eyes. "The fifteenth of July," she said.

"The day after the meeting," Helen said. "The day Dale died. The day Bernice billed the town for her rush correction, *per G. Strand*, and sent me the invoice with the purchase order typed right beneath it." She swallowed audibly. "I think she was paid twice, Greta. Once for the minutes and once for the silence. And I think Gordon's lawyer sent me the proof by accident, in a letter meant to frighten me into being quiet."

"Send it to Sam," Greta said. "Now. Before you do anything else."

"I already have. 10 minutes ago. He texted me back that he was on the road to Fennick Falls and that I was a better clerk than the whole town council put together." Helen gave a small, wavering laugh. "I've never been called that before."

The silence on the line was long and full of unspoken things.

"Greta," Helen said at last, very quietly. "Denise Park called me at six. She was crying so hard I could hardly understand her. She said Bernice came into the office at five this morning, before the sun was up, and packed a suitcase and a garment bag into the back of the white car. She said Bernice didn't say goodbye to anyone. She just took her laptop bag and her black cardigan and drove north, toward Lantern Bay."

Greta sat very still on the couch. The cat had come back and was sitting on the arm of the sofa, watching her face with bright, interested eyes.

"North," Greta repeated.

"Toward the Center," Helen said. "Toward the session. Denise thinks she wants to be there. Denise thinks she's going to sit in the back row, with her notebook open, and certify the transcript in front of the whole town, and then get up and walk out of the room as if nothing has happened." Her voice shook. "She said Bernice told her last week that she would never miss the chance to put a record right. That she'd see the town through it."

"Then she'll be in the room tonight," Greta said quietly.

"Yes," Helen said. "I think she will."

They were silent again. Outside the window, the sun had cleared the tops of the pines and was lighting the lake to a bright, hard silver. A gull cried somewhere over the bay, a thin, sharp sound that carried clear across the water.

"Helen," Greta said. "Are you going to be all right tonight? In the room?"

"No," Helen said honestly. "I'm going to be terrified. Frank says he's going to drive me himself and sit in the parking lot with the engine running, the way Dale used to wait outside the Center for Ruth to finish talking." A short, tired laugh. "Dale would have hated that. He'd have said Frank was a fool for parking with the engine on."

"He'd have been right about the engine," Greta said.

## Chapter 25: The Spare Tire

## Chapter 26: The Voice Memo

The back room of Harbor Center had been the clerk's office for a hundred years, and it still had the look of a place where people went to be told something they did not want to hear. It was a narrow, high-ceilinged room with a single tall window that faced the bay, a long oak table scarred by generations of coffee rings, a row of gray filing cabinets with their drawers marked in faded white ink, and a portrait of the town's first clerk, a stern man with a bushy mustache who seemed to be listening for a mistake. He set it down very carefully in the middle of the oak table, between the brass lamp and the coffee rings, and stepped back.

"Ryan," he said quietly. "You want to do this?"

Ryan came forward slowly. He did not take the phone out of its bag. He leaned over it and looked at the screen through the plastic for a long moment, the way a man might look at a face in a photograph he had not expected to see again.

"Dad," he said under his breath.

Then he took the keys from his other hand, set them on the table, and used his thumb to punch in four numbers through the plastic. The screen lit up with a soft white glow. It's near the bottom, on the second page."

*Voice Memos.* A list of recordings appeared. Most of them had names like *Walt's boat* or *Ryan, call back* or *Gull special*. Near the top, under a date in July, one name stood alone, in plain type.

*Harbor.*

Sam tapped it.

The room was silent. Greta could hear the faint tick of the clock above the door and the distant, muffled sound of voices from the council chamber on the other side of the wall, where the crowd was beginning to gather for the session. Walt had stopped touching his tie. Helen had put her hand over her mouth.

*Harbor. July 15, 2026. 6:33 AM. Duration 6:45.*

Sam turned up the volume on the phone and set it on the table in the middle of the circle of lamplight, and pressed play.

For the first few seconds there was only the hiss of a small microphone in a room with a high ceiling, and the faint, distant clatter of an old radiator. Then came a sound that Greta knew at once, though it had been ten days since she had last heard it: the heavy metal scrape of a door opening on a hinge that needed oil.

A man's voice, very close, hoarse and breathing hard.

*"Bernice Oakes. I know it's you. Your car's out front with that light cracked. I saw it from the road."*

A woman's voice answered, low and perfectly controlled.

*"Dale. You're early."*

*"I'm early for the first time in my life. You can put that drive down."*

*"It's the town's record, Dale. It's the official record of the meeting. Gordon says it needs to be made clear. If you'd only sit down, we could talk about it like adults."*

*"Gordon can go to hell. I was standing at that urn. I heard you. 'Have it cleaned up by morning, before she approves it.' I heard every word, Bernice. I've got a voice, and you don't get to take it off the tape."*

*"Don't you dare say that to me. Don't you dare."*

A chair scraped on the floor. Ryan Whitcomb made a small sound, very quiet, and turned his face to the wall.

*"Take a seat before you do yourself an injury,"* the woman said. Her voice had not risen at all.

*"I've been sitting down for twenty years in front of people like you. Hand me the drive."*

*"You're not going to take it, Dale. I'm sorry."*

*"Then give it to me."*

*"Let go of my wrist."*

*"Then let go of the drive, Bernice."*

*"Let go, Dale. You're hurting—"*

A rattle, a clatter, like something light and bright bouncing off a hard surface. A woman's cry, cut off.

Then a man's voice, suddenly farther off, in a strange, flat, startled tone, with a hard edge of alarm in it.

*"Ah—"*

A sharp sound. Then a second sound, heavier, that went on for a long time and ended all at once.

Greta felt her hands go cold on the oak table. She did not look at Walt, or at Ryan, or at Sam. She looked at the phone, and at the thin white light from its screen, and she did not move.

For a while there was only the wind. A gust of it, roaring faintly across a microphone in the open air, and the slap of water against concrete, and far off, the thin, insistent cry of a gull.

Then a woman's voice, very close to the phone, breathless and quiet.

*"Oh, Dale."*

A longer silence.

*"I'm going to get help. Stay there. Stay there, Dale, please. I'm going to get—"*

*"Oh God, your phone. Oh, God."*

A soft tap, a click, and then the woman's breathing, fast and shallow.

*"Forgive me, Dale."*

The recording ended.

Priya went to him and put her arms around him, and he let her, and Greta stood up from the table and went to the window and looked out at the bay, where the last of the sun was sinking behind the lighthouse and the water had gone the color of a bruise.

"Yes, Deputy. I know. Let me have a moment, please."

Then a pause. Greta turned from the window.

Through the open connecting door she could see a narrow corridor, and at the far end of it a second door standing wide, with the cool blue light of the evening coming through it from the outside. Beyond that door was a small landing, and a rusted railing, and a flight of concrete steps running down toward the boat ramp in the fading light.

She had her back to the Center. She was looking down the stairs, toward the water, with her hands folded in her lap and her black cardigan drawn neatly around her shoulders. Her left wrist was bare. Her right hand rested on the concrete beside her, very still, and across the knuckles, pale and healing, ran a thin, straight line of scar.

She paused, and looked down the room. "The council has asked Ms. Greta Lowe, who took down the proceedings in shorthand on that date, to read the record aloud, for the benefit of the town. Ms. Lowe?"

Greta stood up at the clerk's table with her transcript in her hands. The room was silent. She could feel the eyes on her like the warmth of a lamp.

She had read aloud in courtrooms for thirty-one years. She had read testimony that made grown men weep and testimony that made judges reach for their glasses. She had read the words of murderers and saints and frightened children in a voice that did not change, because the job was to let the record speak. She had never before read a record of her own town, to the people who had lived in it, with the dead man's son sitting in the third row and the woman who had written the false minutes sitting at the back of the room in handcuffs.

She cleared her throat.

"I will read the record as it was spoken," she said. "With the time marks. No words added. No words removed."

She looked down at the first page.

"Seven o'clock. The council is called to order. The roll is read. Clerk Marsh, Council Member Kalinowski, Council Member Matlin, Council Member Sayer, Council Member Vance, Council Member Haskins. Commissioner Whitcomb, in the audience."

She turned the page.

Carla Sayer makes a speech about the sidewalk benches. It runs for six minutes. Council Member Matlin drops a pen."

A ripple of quiet laughter went through the room, startled and brief. Carla Sayer covered her face with one hand.

Greta read on. She read the sewer grant, and Ted Vance's three confused questions, each one tangled in the last, so that the room could hear him searching for the door in the wall. She read the sound of the coffee urn in the lobby, which she had typed as a single stroke in the margin at seven-thirty, because it had been loud enough to interrupt Ruth and Ruth had made a face. She read the moment at seven-fifty-eight when Ruth called item seven, and the long, polished speech Gordon Strand had given about sixty units and a boardwalk with a coffee shop at one end.

She did not look at Gordon when she read it. She did not need to.

"Seven-thirty-nine," she read. "Commissioner Whitcomb, from the aisle. This town gave its harbor to the families who built it. My father hauled bait out of that basin. Walt there hauled perch out of it. Greta's husband, God rest him, rebuilt the fuel dock with his own hands."

At the far end of the room, Walt Brandt bowed his head.

Greta kept reading. She read Dale's speech, every sentence, the sharp corners and the old-fashioned turns of phrase, the words he had said to a room full of people who had known him all their lives and had never once listened to him properly. *I want somebody to explain to me how a survey signed after the comment period closed gets to be the reason we vote on Tuesday night.* She did not raise her voice. She did not need to. The room was so still that she could hear the water slapping against the seawall outside the open windows.

She turned the page.

"Eight-thirty-eight. Council Member Vance asks whether the council may delay the vote until the survey question is settled. Council Member Kalinowski says she is inclined to agree. "The council votes to postpone item seven to the twenty-seventh of July, at seven o'clock, in this room. Motion by Council Member Kalinowski. Seconded by Council Member Vance. Carried, five to nothing."

She looked at Ruth.

Ruth had taken off her glasses. She was looking straight at Greta with her chin lifted and her eyes wet, and she did not look away.

"That is the record, Council Member," Greta said quietly. "It was carried five to nothing. There was no motion to approve the lease that evening, and no vote on it."

A sound went through the room, low and rising, like wind moving through dry grass. Somebody near the doorway said, "Oh, my God," in a loud voice, and somebody else shushed them.

Greta turned the page.

"Eight-fifty. The council recesses for ten minutes." She paused. "Time mark. Eight-fifty-two. No entry is recorded. The room is silent. The reporter is seated at the clerk's table with her hands in her lap. The reporter is watching the lobby door, which is open a crack, and sees Commissioner Whitcomb go out, followed by Mr. Strand, and then by Mrs. Oakes, with her laptop bag over her shoulder."

She did not look at the back row. She kept her voice perfectly even.

"Nine-oh-five. The council reconvenes. Mrs. Oakes returns through the rear door. She takes the seat in the back row, beside Mr. Brandt. Her bangles are audible when she opens her notebook." Greta paused. "The reporter has noted this in the margin of the tape, at the time, in these words: *Mrs. Oakes back, row four, bangles.*"

A soft, sharp intake of breath came from somewhere near the back of the room, and then silence again.

Greta read on. "Nine-oh-nine. Commissioner Whitcomb returns to the chamber. He sits. He folds his hands on the table. He does not speak for the rest of the meeting. The reporter notes that the commissioner does not look at Mr. Strand at any point after the recess. He looks at his hands."

She turned the page. Her voice did not change.

"Nine-fourteen. The council adjourns. Commissioner Whitcomb leaves the room. He stops at the reporter's table and speaks to her. He says: *Your machine doesn't lie, does it?*"

She looked up, across the room, at the front row.

"The reporter replies: *It only writes what's said.*"

Greta closed the transcript. Her hands were steady. The room was perfectly silent, so silent that she could hear, very faintly, the rustle of Bernice Oakes's notebook as its owner's cuffed hands moved over its cover.

"That is the record of the fourteenth of July," Greta said. "No motion was made to approve the lease. No vote was taken on it. The minutes prepared from the audio were altered after the meeting. The statement attributed to Commissioner Whitcomb during the recess was not made in this room, and the reporter did not hear it. The tape shows a silence of fourteen minutes and eleven seconds." She paused. "The reporter has signed the transcript, and she will swear to it."

She sat down.

For a long moment, no one moved. Then Ruth Kalinowski put both hands flat on the council table and bowed her head, and her shoulders began to shake, very slightly, as though she were trying not to laugh or weep and could not decide which.

"Thank you, Ms. Lowe," she said, in a voice that barely carried.

Malcolm Pryce rose from his seat in the first row, slowly, the leather folder open in one hand. His face was smooth and courteous.

"Madam Chair," he said, "I object to the reading of this unauthenticated—"

Pryce hesitated, then sat down, very slowly, and closed his folder.

"Thank you," Ruth said. She looked up, and her eyes moved along the front row to Walt Brandt. "Mr. Brandt. I believe you wished to speak."

Walt stood up. He did not go to the lectern. He stood in the aisle beside his chair, in the suit that had been taken in, with his big weathered hands at his sides, and when he spoke, his voice was rough and clear and carried to every corner of the room.

I drove down Pier Street at a quarter past six, and I saw Dale walking ahead of me with his hands in his pockets. I lifted my hand. He lifted his. I drove around the back of the Harbor Office, and there was a little white car parked behind the hedge, nose in, with a red taillight cracked on the left side." He stopped. The room held its breath. "I sat in my truck with the engine running. I heard a sound at half past six. It sounded like a chair going over on a hard floor. I told myself it was a gull. I didn't go in. He looked across the room, past the crowd, to the back row.

"I'm sorry, Bernice," he said quietly. "I should have walked up those steps."

Bernice did not look up from her notebook. But something in her shoulders eased, very slightly, as though a weight she had been carrying for a long time had been set down on the floor beside her.

Walt sat down.

Ted Vance stood next. His face was white, and his hands were shaking, and he held the handkerchief in his fist so tightly that his knuckles had gone pale. He did not look at the room. He looked at the table.

"I was in the lobby at eight fifty-three," he said. "I went out to get water. Bernice Oakes came up the corridor behind me, with her bangles chiming. She said, *Dale's come round, Ted. He'll withdraw his objection before the vote.* She said, *When it comes back in, vote with Ed and Carla and it'll be done.* I said Dale never withdraws anything in his life. She laughed. She said, *Then he'll be surprised, won't he.* And she patted my arm." He swallowed hard. "I didn't tell anybody. I should have. I thought it was a joke. It wasn't a joke. And tonight, I'm going to vote the way I should have voted on the fourteenth. That's all."

He sat down, and put the handkerchief to his eyes, and Lorraine Haskins reached out without looking and laid her knitting needle very gently on his sleeve.

Helen Marsh stood at the clerk's table, and this time she did not hide behind her laptop. She stood straight, with her hands clasped in front of her, and she spoke to the room in a voice that did not waver.

"At eleven-thirty on the night of July fourteenth, I received a text message from the transcription office in Fennick Falls, with a link to the draft minutes. At eleven fifty-two, I approved them on my phone, from my kitchen table. I did not read them through. I looked at the header and the vote, and I pressed the button." She drew a breath. "The file history shows the draft was created at ten fifty-eight that night, under the transcriptionist's account, and changed at eleven forty-one. The sentence attributed to Commissioner Whitcomb was added at eleven forty-one. I was the clerk of this town for eleven years, and I signed a record I had not read. That is my responsibility. I've resigned as clerk, effective at the end of this session, and I'll answer for it to anyone who asks." She looked at the back row, briefly, and then away. "I'm sorry, Dale. I'm sorry, Walt. I'm sorry, Ruth. I'm sorry for every line I didn't read."

Ruth shook her head slowly, but she did not speak.

Tessa Quill stood up next. She was trembling so badly that Walt reached over and steadied her elbow. She came to the front of the room with the thin green folder clutched in both hands, and she did not look at Gordon Strand as she passed him. She stopped at the council table and opened the folder, and laid out three photographs on the oak, one at a time, very carefully, like cards in a game she did not want to win.

"This is the original survey," she said. Her voice shook, but it held. "Signed July second, with the commission's stamp. The comment period closed June thirtieth. This is the copy Mr. Strand asked me to swap for it, with a June stamp, emailed from the surveyor's office on July thirteenth." She laid down the second photograph. "And this is the envelope. Brown manila. Mrs. Oakes brought it into Mr. Strand's office on the morning of the fourteenth. I saw it on his desk. I saw her take it away again, after she'd counted it." She swallowed. "He told me, *Bernice will do it, Tessa. She knows how to make a record say what it needs to say.* And he told me, after the meeting, that if Dale withdrew his objection, nobody would ever reopen the comment period."

"Ms. Quill," Ruth said gently. "Is that all?"

"No," Tessa said. "I took those photographs with my own phone on the night of the fourteenth, at nine-fifty-two. I've never let them go. I'm not going to let them go now."

She sat down beside Walt, very straight, and put her hands flat on the empty green folder, and did not cry.

Gordon Strand rose slowly from the front row. He did not look at Malcolm Pryce. He looked at the council, with a small, sad, reasonable smile, the smile of a man who had explained a great many things across a great many tables and had always been believed.

"I'd like to say something," he said.

"You may address the council during public comment, Mr. Strand," Ruth said. "Not now."

"I'd like to say this now," Gordon said, and his voice was warm and clear. "I never asked Bernice Oakes to lie. Not once. I asked her to tidy the record. To make sure the council's discussion was clear to the state. That's all. I paid her for a correction, and I'd pay her again, because the record in this town has been a mess for twenty years, and nobody has ever once tried to—"

"Gordon," said Malcolm Pryce sharply, and put a hand on his sleeve.

Gordon shook him off. "—tried to make it clean, Ruth. I did. I paid for it. And I'm not ashamed of that." He turned, slowly, and looked at the back row, where Bernice Oakes sat with her head bowed over her notebook, and for the first time something cracked in his face, and what showed through was not regret, but fury. "I paid for a correction. That's all I ever paid for. And now she's sitting there in handcuffs, and I'm the one they're looking at."

Eleanor Strand stood up.

She did not say anything. She turned, very quietly, and walked up the side aisle toward the doors, her black dress whispering against the chair backs, her head high and her face white and set. People stood aside to let her pass. Nobody spoke. At the door, she paused for a moment and looked back at the front row, at her husband, who had not moved, and then she was gone into the corridor.

Gordon stood very still. Then he sat down again, slowly, and folded his hands in his lap, and stared at the floor.

Ruth let the silence hold for a long moment.

"Item seven," she said at last. Her voice was quiet, and steady, and very tired. "The request of Strand Lakeside Partners for a forty-year lease of the north basin. The council will vote. Clerk, call the roll."

Helen did not stand this time. She simply read it from the screen, in a clear voice, and each councilor answered in turn.

"Matlin," Helen said.

Ed Matlin looked down at his hands for a long moment.

"Aye," he said, very softly. "I voted aye on the fourteenth, and I shouldn't have. I didn't hear a motion. I didn't hear anybody say the word." He lifted his head, and his face was wet. "I'm changing it. I'm sorry. I vote no."

Helen looked up from the screen, startled.

"Sayer," she said.

Carla Sayer did not wait. "No," she said, her voice shaking. "No. God forgive me, no."

"Vance."

Ted Vance stood up, one hand on the back of his chair. He did not look at the room. He looked at the back row, at the bowed head and the steel cuffs and the brown leather notebook, and something passed across his face, grief and anger and a kind of terrible relief all at once.

"No," he said. "I've been the one who can't make up his mind for eleven years. I've made it up now. No."

"Haskins."

Lorraine Haskins put down her knitting. She folded her hands in her lap, and she looked at the Strand family's empty seats in the front row, and then at Gordon, who did not look up.

"No," she said. "I've been knitting through this entire mess since June. I'm finished now."

"Kalinowski."

Ruth took off her glasses and set them on the table.

"No," she said. "No. And I'm sorry it took a woman with a steno machine to make me read my own council minutes."

Helen read the tally from the screen, and her voice cracked on the last word.

"The motion to approve the lease," she said, "fails. One for. Four against."

No one cheered. The room did not make a sound. But somewhere near the back, Frances Dunn let out a long, shuddering breath, and Lyle Kemp put his cap back on his head for the first time all evening, and Denise Park began to cry quietly into her daughter's hair.

"The council will take up the matter of the July fourteenth minutes at a regular session on Wednesday," she said. "The council will not adopt the draft as prepared. It will adopt the record as read tonight. The clerk will so note." She looked up. "Thank you, Ms. Lowe."

"Thank you, Council Member," Greta said quietly.

"Sheriff Ortiz," Ruth said. "I understand you have something you wish to place in the record."

Sam stood up from his chair at the back of the room, beside the woman in the steel cuffs. He did not move toward the front. He held up a clear evidence bag in one gloved hand, with a black phone inside it, its cracked corner catching the light from the open windows.

"Madam Chair," he said. "With the council's permission. There is one recording from the morning of July fifteenth that I believe the council should hear. It was made on the phone of the late Commissioner Dale Whitcomb, at 6:33 a.m., in the back room of this building. It is the only audio of what was said there that morning. I'd like it played through the council's speakers, so the town can hear it in the room where it was supposed to be said."

Ruth looked down the room for a long moment. Then she nodded.

"Play it, Sheriff," she said.

Sam walked to the front of the chamber. He took the phone from its bag, and set it on the council's table beside Helen's laptop, and found the speaker cable Ruth's clerk had left on the desk beside the microphone. He connected it with careful, deliberate hands. Then he looked up at the back row.

Bernice Oakes had raised her head.

She was looking at the phone on the table. Her face had gone very still and very pale, and her cuffed hands were pressed tight together on top of the brown leather notebook. She did not look at Sam. She did not look at the crowd. She looked, for a long moment, at the small black phone in the center of the council table, as if she had never seen it before.

Then Sam pressed play.

The room filled with the hiss of a small microphone in a high, empty space, and then with the sound of a door on a stiff hinge, and a man's voice, hoarse and close and breathing hard.

*"Bernice Oakes. I know it's you."*

"You always did like to be the last one in the room," Bernice said. Bernice's mouth moved, very slightly, in what might have been a smile.

"No," she said. "I suppose not. You had the whole town to fill with the truth." She lifted her cuffed hands a few inches, and set the brown notebook down on the empty seat beside her, carefully, the way she might have set down a binder before a hearing. ## Chapter 28: The Side Room

The side room was a storage closet that Ruth's clerk had cleared out for the evening, with a folding table, three metal chairs, a box fan that did nothing but move the heat from one corner to another, and a window painted shut sometime during the Carter administration. Someone had put a glass of water on the table in front of the empty chair. Nobody had touched it.

Greta set her steno machine on the corner of the table and unfolded the tripod with careful hands. She checked the paper tape, the spare fan-fold, and the battery indicator, and then she ran a test stroke across the keys, and the machine answered with its familiar soft chatter. She had owned this machine for twenty-two years. It had been to four courthouses, two depositions in Chicago, and one memorable wedding reception in Carrow City where the groom had insisted on a transcript of the vows. She had never once turned it off in the middle of a job.

"Ready," she said.

Sam Ortiz came in with Bernice Oakes behind him. He had removed her steel cuffs in the corridor and replaced them with a single plastic restraint, which he had fastened loosely at the front of her wrists, so that her hands rested in her lap and did not look like the hands of a prisoner. She wore the black cardigan. Her hair was still pinned up neatly, and there was a small red mark at her temple where the edge of the bench had pressed into it. She looked at the room the way she had looked at every room in her working life, quickly and thoroughly, taking inventory of the exits and the furniture and the people in it.

She stopped when she saw the machine.

"Oh, good," she said. "You brought the good one."

"I brought the one I have," Greta said.

"That's what I meant." Bernice sat down in the chair across from her and smoothed her cardigan over her knees. "Take a seat, Greta. You're making me nervous, standing there like a bailiff."

Greta sat.

Sam took the chair at the end of the table and set a yellow legal pad in front of him. He did not write on it. He looked at Bernice for a long moment, and his voice, when he spoke, was careful and flat, the voice he used for the parts of the job he did not enjoy.

"Mrs. Oakes, before we start, I need to read you your rights. You've heard them before, I'm sure. You're not required to say anything. Anything you do say can be used against you in a court of law. You have the right to an attorney, and if you can't afford one, the court will appoint one for you. Do you understand each of those things?"

"Yes, Deputy. I understand them better than you do, I expect." She folded her hands. "I've read more of these than you've had hot dinners."

"Do you want an attorney present?"

Bernice was quiet for a moment. She looked at the glass of water, and then at Greta's machine, and then at the small window with its stripe of gray evening light.

"I'll want one," she said. "Later, I think. A good one, from Carrow City, who will tell me to be quiet and then send me a bill. But I'm going to talk to you tonight, Deputy, because I've been wanting to talk for a long time, and I don't believe I could stop now if I tried." She met his eyes. "I want the reporter to take it down. Word for word. I want it to be the record."

Sam glanced at Greta. Greta said nothing. She had been in enough rooms to know that the most important thing in any room was the thing nobody said out loud.

"Sheriff Ortiz will ask the questions," Greta said finally. "I'll take down what's said. I won't add anything, and I won't leave anything out."

"Oh, I know you won't," Bernice said. "That's why I asked for you."

She turned to Sam. "Ask, then, Deputy. I'll answer, and then you'll know what kind of woman you've been sitting across from for the last week, and you can tell the county whatever you like."

Sam started with the easy things, because he was a careful man. Her name, her date of birth, her address on Juniper Lane, which was rented, and her employer, which was Bernice Oakes Court Reporting, LLC, one employee, two laptops, and a shared office on Front Street that had once belonged to her late husband's insurance agency. She answered in a clear voice, without hesitation. Greta typed every word, and the machine chattered softly in the close little room, and the fan turned its useless circles.

Then Sam asked about the fourteenth of July.

"Tell me about the fourteenth, Mrs. Oakes," he said. "The council meeting. What you did that night."

"I reported it," Bernice said. "Seven o'clock to nine-fourteen. I was in the second chair at the clerk's table, with my machine on the carpet and my laptop open on the end of the table. I took it down the way I always take it down. Except for one thing, which I'll come to."

"Go on."

"Gordon Strand came to see me in April," Bernice said. "At my office on Front Street, in the afternoon, with a cup of coffee he'd brought for me from the Gull, because he always knew how I took it. He was worried about the north basin. He had an offer on the table from a developer in Carrow City, and he had three council votes lined up, and he had the one vote he couldn't count on, which was Dale Whitcomb's." She paused. "Dale had been making trouble about the survey. He'd been asking the clerk for copies of everything. Gordon said Dale was going to kill the lease, and then the whole town was going to lose the chance at a real future, and he didn't want the people in this town to hate him for it. He wanted me to help him make the record tidy."

"What did he mean by tidy?"

"He meant that the comment period on the survey had closed on the thirtieth of June, and that the survey itself had been signed on the second of July, which was after the period closed." Her voice was dry. "He meant that the council minutes ought not to show that Dale had raised it, or that the council had discussed it in any detail. He meant that the transcript ought to say what the council had decided, in a sentence that a state reviewer could read without stopping. He said I was the only person in this town who knew how to write a sentence like that. He was right, Deputy. I am."

"Did you agree?"

"Not in April." Bernice looked down at her hands. "In June, I agreed. He paid me three thousand dollars on the seventeenth, in a brown envelope, in cash. He told me it was for document services. I put it in my savings account, and I didn't sleep for a week, and then I took it out and bought a new carpet for the office, because I thought I'd earned something for the discomfort." She looked up, and her mouth twisted. "I had no idea how much more I would earn."

Sam's pen moved on the pad. "The survey."

"Tessa Quill was the one who had to swap it," Bernice said. "She's a child, Deputy, and I'll say that without any contempt at all. She was frightened of Gordon, and she was very good at her work, and he knew she would do what she was told. I arranged for the surveyor's office to send a second copy, dated June, to the clerk's inbox on the thirteenth. Gordon had the original shredded. Tessa took the one she was given and put it in the file. That's how a comment period closed on the thirtieth of June, in the eyes of the state, for a survey that was signed on the second of July." She shrugged, very slightly. "It's not clever. It doesn't have to be. Nobody in a county office ever reads the stamps."

"And the minutes."

"Ah," Bernice said softly. "The minutes."

She was silent for a moment, and when she spoke again, her voice had changed. It was lower, and flatter, and very precise, the voice of a woman reciting the one part of a long story she had gone over in her head a thousand times.

"The meeting ran long. Dale was very angry. He stood at the urn in the lobby at the recess and told Gordon, in front of me and Ted Vance and half of the room, that he was going to bring the survey to the state on Monday, and that he didn't care who was humiliated by it. Gordon came back into the chamber white in the face. He told me to get it cleaned up by morning, before Helen approved the draft." She paused. "I was the one who told him that Helen always approves what's in front of her at midnight. I knew she would. She's been a clerk for eleven years, and she has never read a single line of the draft minutes in all that time. Everybody in this town knows that, except Helen, I think."

"You prepared the draft."

"I prepared the draft from the audio, at my office, on the night of the fourteenth, at ten fifty-eight. I finished the first version by eleven. Then I got a text from Helen at eleven-thirty, with the link, and she was wanting to approve it before she went to bed. So I did something I'd promised myself I would never do in my life. I changed a record after it was made, and I put a sentence in the mouth of a man who had said nothing of the kind." Her hands tightened, very slightly, in her lap. "At eleven forty-one, I added the paragraph. *Commissioner Whitcomb stated for the record: 'The survey satisfies my concerns. I withdraw my objection.'* I changed the vote as well. I wrote 'carried, three to two,' because it had to be carried by three, and the two who would vote no would be the ones Dale didn't trust. I did it all very quickly. I was very good at it, and I hated myself the whole time, and at eleven fifty-two, Helen pressed the button on her phone and went to bed."

"Mrs. Oakes," Sam said quietly, "why did you make the change?"

"Because Gordon Strand asked me to," Bernice said. "And because I had three thousand dollars in a carpet, and four thousand five hundred more was waiting in an envelope on his desk for when the vote came in. That's the honest answer, Deputy. There's no better one. I'd like the reporter to write it down exactly like that."

Greta typed it down exactly like that. Her fingers did not stop, and her face did not change.

"The second payment," Sam said. "July fifteenth."

"Four thousand five hundred dollars," Bernice said. "In a brown envelope, on the morning of the fifteenth, in his office. Tessa saw it. Gordon told her I would do it, and then he sent me a text saying the job was finished, and I went home and cried at my kitchen table." She closed her eyes for a moment. "And then I went to the Harbor Office and I met Dale Whitcomb, and I told myself I was only going to ask him to be reasonable."

"Tell me about the Harbor Office."

She opened her eyes. "I had a code. Denise Park gave it to me on the Tuesday, at the Center, when I asked her to set up an after-hours card for the office computers. She's trusting, Denise. She didn't ask why I needed it. I told her the county had asked for the transcripts to be kept in the harbor records, which was a lie, but she believed it." Bernice's mouth thinned. "I used the code at five-fifty-eight on Wednesday morning, and I went in the back door, and I sat down at the workstation in the back office. Then I logged into the county's recording account, which Gordon's people had given me access to, and I found the file from the fourteenth. The audio. The original. The one that would have shown everyone exactly what I'd done to the minutes."

"And?"

"I turned off the trash, so it couldn't be recovered. And I deleted it." Her voice did not waver. "At six thirty-one. I remember because the clock on the wall was just over the monitor, and I watched the number change while my finger was on the key. I had been waiting in that chair since before six, listening to the water against the ramp, thinking about what I was going to say to him. And when I'd finished, I logged out, and I went to the back steps, and I waited for him to come up from the boat yard. He'd said he'd come by himself, at half past six, and he'd always been a punctual man."

Sam stopped writing. "He had a drive."

"He had a flash drive," Bernice said. "A copy of the audio, made from the office server on the night of the fourteenth. He'd made it himself, before the minutes were changed, because he had a suspicious mind and a good memory for the sound of a clock. He'd been carrying it around in his shirt pocket for three weeks. He told Walt Brandt about it at the bait shop, and Walt told Frances Dunn, and I believe the whole harbor knew by Tuesday that Dale had something he wasn't going to give up."

"And on Wednesday morning he came to the steps."

"He came up the back stairs at six thirty-three, a little out of breath, with his phone in his hand," Bernice said. "He was filming me before he'd even reached the top. He said, *You're early.* I didn't say anything for a while. Then I said, *It's the town's record, Dale.* And then he said I could put the drive down, and I realized he was asking me to put down the drive he was holding, the one he'd made, and I was so tired by then that I simply couldn't bear it any longer."

"Tell me what happened on the stairs, Mrs. Oakes."

For the first time, Bernice did not answer at once. She looked at her hands, at the thin plastic restraint across her wrists, and then at the glass of water on the table, which had begun to sweat in the close air.

"I took the drive out of his hand," she said finally. "He wouldn't let go of it, and I didn't let go of his wrist, and we were both pulling and shouting. Then he let go of the drive, all at once, the way a man does when he's surprised, and he stepped back to get his balance, and his heel caught on the top step. The one with the crack in it. He went down, Deputy. Backward. He went down the concrete stairs, and I still had his wrist in my hand, and the bangle on my own arm caught his cuff and snapped, and I heard it go. I heard it go like a glass in a sink." She swallowed. "I was holding his wrist when he went. I let go of it at the bottom, or he pulled it out of my hand. I don't know which. I've tried to remember, every night for a week, and I can't."

"You said you didn't push him," Sam said.

"I didn't push him," Bernice said. "I want that written down. I was holding on to him, the way you'd hold on to a rope you didn't want to see go. I didn't push him."

Greta's fingers moved steadily over the keys. She did not look up. She had known for a long time that this was what the bruises on the wrist would say, and that this was what the railing bolt would say, and that the people who had seen Bernice Oakes walking up the boat ramp that morning with her hand over her face would not be able to say anything different. She did not feel better for knowing it. She only felt the weight of the sentence settle across her shoulders, the way a record did once it was finally complete.

"What did you do then, Mrs. Oakes?"

"I went down the stairs." Bernice's voice had gone very quiet. "He was lying on the concrete at the bottom, against the railing post, with one leg bent under him, and his eyes were open. He was breathing. He was saying something, but I couldn't make out the words. I knelt down beside him. I said, *Dale, I'm going to get help.* I said it twice. And then his phone was on the concrete beside him, with the little red light still on, recording. I picked it up. I don't remember deciding to. I put my thumb on it and tapped it off. And then I looked at his face, and I thought about the drive he'd made, and the envelope in my car, and the twenty years I'd spent in courtrooms saying nothing, and I stood up and walked back up the stairs."

"You didn't call for help."

"No," Bernice said. "I went to my car. I sat in it with my hands on the wheel. And I heard him, through the open window, calling my name, very softly, from the bottom of the stairs. And I drove away." She lifted her head. "Walt Brandt watched me do it, Deputy. He's told you that. He's the only honest man in this town who was in a position to see it, and I've been grateful to him every day for the courage it must have taken to say so."

Sam set down his pen. He looked, for the first time since they had walked in, like a man who had been carrying something heavy for too long.

"Where is the drive now, Mrs. Oakes?"

"In the water, off the boat ramp," Bernice said. "I threw it from the top of the stairs, when I came back up the first time. I don't know why I came back. I think I wanted to see if he was still breathing. He wasn't moving, and I didn't go down." She paused. "My own drive is the one in the grocery bag, in the car. I broke it open at home on Wednesday, with a hammer, on my kitchen floor. I cut my hand doing it. The fragments are wrapped in a towel. I couldn't put them in the lake, either. I'm sorry, Deputy. I seem to have kept a great many things I should have thrown away."

"And the phone?"

"I wrapped it in Frances Dunn's dish towel," Bernice said. "I picked it up off the concrete beside him, after I'd tapped it off, because I thought the police would find it and I didn't want them to hear what it said. And then I put it under the spare tire in my trunk, because I couldn't bring myself to drop it in the bay and I couldn't bear to have it near me in the car." She looked at Greta, very directly, for the first time since the interview had begun. "I was going to throw it away. Every day since. I never did."

Greta stopped typing. She had reached the end of a page, and the machine's paper tape had run out a few inches past the edge of the table, and she did not reach for the next fan-fold. She sat with her hands on the cold keys and looked at the woman across the table, who had taught her half of what she knew about the proper form of a transcript, who had once told a judge in Carrow City that a reporter who could not keep her face still had no business in a courtroom, and who had killed a man on the steps of a boat ramp and then sat in the back row of his town's council chamber and taken its minutes for a week.

"Greta," Bernice said softly. "Continue typing, please. You've stopped."

Greta drew a breath and began again.

Sam asked the remaining questions, quietly, one at a time. Who had given Gordon Strand the copy of the audio. Whether Eleanor Strand had known about the envelopes. Whether Malcolm Pryce had read the draft minutes before they were filed. Bernice answered every question, precisely, and when she did not know the answer she said so, and when she was asked whether she had any reason to want to lie about any of it, she laughed, a short, dry sound with no humor in it at all.

"Lie, Deputy?" she said. "I've been lying for two years, for money, in a room full of people who believed me. I'm tired. I want to be accurate. It's the only thing I've ever really been good at."

At eight-forty, Sam sat back and said the words that closed the interview, and asked whether Bernice wished to make any further statement.

"Only this," Bernice said. She turned in her chair so that she could see Greta's face directly. "Read it back to me, Greta. The whole thing, from the beginning. I want to hear the record in your voice before anybody else gets to put it in theirs."

So Greta read it back. She read the question about the carpet and the envelope, and the answer about the survey stamp. She read the minutes, the paragraph, the vote changed from two to three. She read the drive on the back stairs, the snapped bangle, the wrist, the words *I didn't push him*, the phone under the spare tire, the cut hand, the grocery bag, the towel. She read *Forgive me, Dale*, which Bernice had said on the tape and had not repeated in the room, and then she read it again, because Bernice asked her to, in a steady and unhurried voice.

When she had finished, the side room was silent except for the faint tick of the clock on the wall and the slow turning of the fan.

"Yes," Bernice said. "That's exactly what I said."

"Mrs. Oakes," Sam said, "you'll be asked to sign the transcript."

"I'll sign it," Bernice said. "Hand me your pen, Deputy. And Greta, bring me the last page. I want to see my name in your handwriting, under the time mark, the way it's always been."

Greta handed her the transcript. Bernice read the last page through, very slowly, with her lips moving faintly at the edges of the words. Then she took the pen from Sam's hand, bent over the page, and signed her name in a small, neat, perfectly legible hand, the same hand that had signed a thousand court documents in a thousand courtrooms across eight counties.

She set the pen down.

"Thank you," she said. "That's the record."

## Chapter 29: The Corrected Minutes

The Lantern Bay Town Council met again on Wednesday evening, at seven o'clock, in the same chamber with the same tall windows and the same portrait of the harbor's first commissioner looking down from the wall. The crowd was smaller than it had been on Monday, but it was no less determined. People stood along the side aisles with their arms folded, and a few of them had brought folding chairs from home and set them up in the corridor, where the loudspeaker crackled whenever the door opened.

Greta sat at the clerk's table with a cup of cold coffee, a legal pad, and a fresh fan-fold in the steno machine that she had not yet needed to use. Ruth had asked her to attend as the council's independent reporter, and to take down whatever was said, and she had agreed without having to think about it. She had not slept more than four hours since Monday. Priya had brought her a sandwich at noon, and she had eaten half of it standing up, in the lobby, between phone calls.

Helen Marsh had resigned as clerk on Monday night, as she had said she would, and the council had accepted the resignation by a vote of five to nothing, with Helen abstaining from her own motion, which she had insisted upon. Her laptop sat closed at the far end of the clerk's table, beside a neat stack of printed agendas. Frank Marsh had brought her a thermos of tea. She had not touched it.

Ruth opened the session at seven-oh-two. She did not use the gavel.

"The Lantern Bay Town Council will come to order," she said. "This is the regular session. The first item on the agenda is the record of the July fourteenth meeting. The council has before it a motion, which has been circulated since Monday, to adopt the transcript prepared by this council's independent reporter as the official record of that meeting, and to strike the draft minutes prepared from the audio as altogether unreliable. I've asked Ms. Lowe to confirm the record, and then the council will vote."

Greta stood and read the confirmation in a clear voice. She had read it aloud on Monday, but this was the formal version, the one that would go into the town book and the county archive. She had spent the afternoon on the phone with the state records office in Carrow City, a woman named Joan Cho who had been courteous and exact and very fond of the word *verbatim*. Joan had told her that the transcript would be accepted, provided the reporter signed it, which Greta had already done, in her own handwriting, at the bottom of every page.

"The transcript of the July fourteenth meeting of the Lantern Bay Town Council," Greta read, "prepared from the official audio recording and the reporter's notes, is accurate to the best of my knowledge and belief. The draft minutes dated July fourteenth, filed at eleven fifty-two p.m. that evening, contain statements not made in the meeting and a vote not taken. The draft was altered after its creation. The reporter has no knowledge of any such statement being made by any member of the council or any member of the public during the recess of that meeting." She turned the page. "The tape shows a silence of fourteen minutes and eleven seconds during the recess. Greta Lowe, certified reporter."

She sat down.

Ruth looked around the room. "Is there any discussion?"

There was not. The council voted, one at a time, in a steady voice, and each of them said *aye* without hesitation. Ed Matlin's hand shook as he raised it. Ted Vance kept his eyes on the table. Lorraine Haskins said her aye in a voice that could be heard in the lobby, and then she picked up her knitting and began to work the needles again, very quickly, as though she had decided to finish something before the evening was over.

"The motion carries," Ruth said. "Five to nothing. The clerk will note that the draft minutes are withdrawn, and that the transcript is entered as the official record. Helen, you'll want the file moved to the county."

"Yes, Ruth," Helen said, from the end of the table. Her voice was quiet, but it was steady. "I'll do it tonight."

"Thank you." Ruth took her glasses off and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "Item two. The council has received, this afternoon, a letter from Strand Lakeside Partners, through its counsel, Malcolm Pryce. I'll read it, since it's short." She unfolded a single sheet of cream-colored stationery and held it at arm's length. "*Strand Lakeside Partners hereby withdraws its application for a forty-year lease of the north basin, effective immediately, and requests that the council take no further action on the matter. The firm of Pryce and Halvorsen no longer represents Mr. Gordon Strand in any matter related to the Town of Lantern Bay.*" She set the letter down. "It's signed by Mr. Pryce, and it has the look of a man who would rather be at home."

A ripple went through the room, not quite a laugh. Somebody near the doorway said, very clearly, "Good riddance," and somebody else told him to hush.

Ruth did not raise her voice. "The application is withdrawn. The north basin remains in the town's trust for public moorings and the harbor's commercial fishing fleet, as it was before April. Mr. Brandt, I believe you have something to say about the fleet."

Walt Brandt stood up in the front row. He wore a clean flannel shirt and his old fishing jacket, and he had shaved, although not very well. He did not go to the lectern. He looked at the council table, and then at the windows, where the sun was going down over the breakwater in a long, rose-colored bar.

"The fleet's got eleven boats," he said. "Eleven families. Dale's boat's still in the slip, with his name on the transom, and his boy Ryan's been sitting in her every evening since Monday, not doing anything. I'd like the council to know that the north basin's going to stay open to them. That's all. I don't need a speech." He paused. "I'd like the town to keep Dale's slip reserved for Ryan, if he wants it. And I'd like Tessa Quill to have a job at the bait shop for the rest of the season, if she'll take it, because I owe her more than a wage."

Tessa was sitting beside him, very pale and very straight, with her hands in her lap. She did not look up. But she nodded, once, very slightly, and Walt laid his big hand on her shoulder for a moment, and then he sat down again without another word.

Ruth wiped her eyes with the back of one hand. "Thank you, Walt. The council will so note."

The rest of the session passed quickly. The council approved the sewer grant, which had been waiting since May. It voted to hire an independent auditor to review every set of minutes filed since January, and to place the reporting function under the county court reporter's office in Carrow City, pending a permanent solution. It agreed, after a long and unhappy debate, to rename the bench on the harbor esplanade for Dale Whitcomb, and it did so over the objection of Carla Sayer, who said she would rather the council spend the money on a proper pier. Carla lost, but she did not sit down until the vote was recorded, and then she put her face in her hands and said, to no one in particular, "He always did hate that bench."

At nine-oh-four, Ruth adjourned the session. Nobody left at once. People stood in the aisles and talked quietly with their neighbors, and some of them came to the clerk's table to shake Greta's hand and say something kind, and she thanked each of them and meant it.

Ted Vance came last. He stood in front of the clerk's table with his hat in his hands, and he looked, for the first time in eleven years, as though he had nothing to hide behind.

"Greta," he said. "I'm sorry about the fourteenth. About the questions. About that whole business with the sewer grant. I was never much good at making up my mind, and I let people make it up for me." He turned the hat in his hands. "I'd like to resign from the council, if the town will let me. Ruth says I can stay on until the November election. I think that's fair."

"It's your decision, Ted," Greta said.

"I know it is." He smiled, very faintly. "It's the first one I've made in a long time, and I'd like to be the one who makes it."

He put on his hat, nodded to her, and went out into the corridor.

Lorraine Haskins had finished her row. She folded the knitting into the bag over her arm, stood up, and came to stand beside Greta at the table.

"I'm making Ted a scarf," she said, in her dry, precise way. "It's going to be ugly. He'll wear it anyway. He always does." She glanced at the back row, where the chair beneath the portrait had been pushed neatly against the wall. "I used to think Bernice was the only one of us who ever knew what she was doing. I see now she was only the one with the best handwriting."

"She was very good at it," Greta said.

"She was. That's the terrible part." Lorraine tucked a strand of gray hair behind her ear. "Good night, dear. Go home and sleep."

## Chapter 30: Lantern Bay Lights

The lighthouse was relit on the third Saturday in August, after two weeks of work that nobody in Lantern Bay had expected to be finished so quickly. Walt Brandt led the crew up the iron ladder on the first morning, with a box of new bulbs under one arm and a pair of old leather gloves in his back pocket, and he would not let anybody else climb the last twelve rungs to the gallery. Ryan Whitcomb carried the lens cleaner. Tessa Quill held the flashlight, and Frances Dunn, who had never once in her life climbed anything taller than the bar stool at the Gull, stood at the foot of the ladder with her arms folded and her chin up, shouting encouragement at everyone who passed.

The old lantern had been dark since the winter the county cut its funding for the light keeper's stipend. Nobody had complained much, at first. Ships had the navigation buoys, and the marina had its own lights along the breakwater, and the town had gotten used to looking at the black tower at the end of the stone wall as a sort of decoration. But in July, after the council hearing, somebody had started a petition to bring the light back, and by the end of the month it had three hundred names on it, most of them in handwriting that Greta recognized from the sign-in sheets at the Center. Ruth had signed it first. Ted Vance had signed it second, and had written beside his name, in tiny, careful letters, *I should have done this years ago.*

Greta watched from the end of the breakwater, sitting on the warm gray stone with her shoes beside her and Judge curled in the shade of her canvas bag, which she had brought for the purpose of carrying a thermos, a sandwich, and a paperback she had no intention of finishing. The day was bright and cool. A few sailboats drifted near the marina, their white sails bellying slowly in the light wind off the bay, and somewhere along the esplanade a child was laughing at a gull that had stolen a French fry from a paper cone.

Priya came down the stone wall with two coffees and sat beside her, her bare feet dangling over the water.

"They've got the new bulb in," she said. "Walt just shouted something very rude at the lens. I think it's working."

"Good," Greta said.

"You're not watching."

"I'm watching," Greta said. "I'm just not looking up."

Priya laughed and handed her a coffee. "Is that the transcript from the Carrow hearing? I thought you were done with that."

"It's the review hearing from last week," Greta said. "The state reviewers' session on the north basin paperwork. Joan Cho sent me the audio on Thursday, for the record." She turned the thermos over in her hand and did not open it. "It's nothing. The usual. A councilor coughing. A chair scraping. Somebody in the back row whispering about lunch."

"And?"

"And nothing," Greta said. "Except that I've listened to it four times, and I'm not sure the sound at the beginning is the coffee urn."

Priya set down her cup. "You're not supposed to do that anymore, Greta. You promised Sam."

"I promised Sam I'd stop reading the record for the town," Greta said mildly. "I didn't promise I'd stop listening."

Judge stirred in the shade of the canvas bag, stretched one white paw toward the stone, and yawned at them both with enormous dignity. He had spent most of the summer on the windowsill of Greta's kitchen, watching the lane with the unblinking patience of a cat who believed he had solved the case weeks ago. He had not once in all that time stopped giving Greta his opinion, usually by sitting down squarely on whatever document she happened to be reading.

"He's been right about everything," Priya said.

"He's a cat," Greta said. "He's right about the food. The rest is coincidence."

Below them, on the water, a small green dinghy was rowing slowly out from the public slip toward the north basin. Greta recognized the figure at the oars, the bent head, the careful, unhurried strokes. Ryan Whitcomb had been taking the boat out every morning since the council voted to keep the slip for him. He did not fish. He did not go far. He rowed out past the breakwater, drifted in the open water for an hour with a green notebook open on his knees, and then rowed back in before the wind picked up. He had told Greta, on the second Tuesday, that he was reading his father's entries from the summer, one a day, in order, and that he was not yet sure what he was going to do when he reached the last page.

"He's going to stay," Priya said, watching the dinghy. "Did you know that? He told Walt he'd take the slip for a year. He's going to try to make a go of the charter boat. Walt says he'll be terrible at it."

"Walt says a lot of things," Greta said. "Most of them are about the weather."

"He's right about that, too."

They sat in companionable silence for a while, watching the lighthouse. Up on the gallery, Walt had finally gotten the lamp housing open, and a small, bright light flashed once in the sun, like a mirror catching a signal, and then went dark again. Frances Dunn cheered from the foot of the ladder. Tessa Quill laughed, a clear, unguarded sound that carried across the water.

Greta had not heard Tessa laugh like that before. She thought about the girl's face on Monday night, pale and trembling at the council table, laying out the photographs one at a time. Tessa had been offered limited immunity by the Carrow County prosecutor, in exchange for her full testimony about the survey swap and the envelope. She had accepted. She had moved out of the room above the Gull and into a small apartment over the bait shop, where Walt had given her the keys and a lecture about the cold in January. She was taking night courses in bookkeeping at the community college in Carrow City, three days a week, and she told everyone who would listen that she intended to become the most boring accountant in the state.

Bernice Oakes had been arraigned in Carrow City on the twenty-eighth of July, represented by the attorney she had finally agreed to hire, a sharp, gray-haired woman named Mara Lindgren. Mara had told the court that her client would enter a guilty plea to every charge that could be laid against her, and that she wished to be sentenced promptly. The judge had listed the matter for October. Sam Ortiz had driven the transcript of the side-room interview down to Carrow City himself, in a plain envelope, and had come home that evening with his face gray and his hands in his pockets, and had not said much except that she had signed every page in the same neat hand.

Bernice had written to Greta once. The letter had come in a plain white envelope, postmarked Carrow City, and it had been four lines long, in the same small, clear handwriting that had signed the transcript.

*Greta,* it said. *Please see that the record is accurate in every line. I trust you. You always did. The reporter always got it right. B.O.*

Greta had read it at the kitchen table and then put it in the drawer where she kept the things that mattered, beside her old steno notebooks from the courthouse in Fennick Falls. She had not answered it. She had not known how.

Gordon Strand had been charged on the thirtieth of July with conspiracy to falsify a public record, bribery of a public official, and tampering with evidence. His wife Eleanor had given a statement to Sheriff Corliss three days later, in a small room at the county building, with a folder of bank statements on the table between them. The folder had included the carbon copies of two checks, a ledger listing payments to *document services* in June and July, and a handwritten note in Gordon's crisp, confident script that said only, *Bernice will take care of the minutes. Don't ask.* Eleanor had not asked him anything. She had simply packed a suitcase, moved into her sister's house in Carrow City, and filed for a divorce that nobody in Lantern Bay had been surprised to hear about.

Malcolm Pryce had withdrawn from the case the same week. He had sent the county a letter that Sam described, with some satisfaction, as the most carefully worded resignation he had ever read.

Helen Marsh had moved to her sister's place by the lake in Carrow City, and she came back to Lantern Bay only on Sundays, to walk along the breakwater with Frank and to visit the grave at the top of the hill above the marina. Greta had gone with her once, in early August, and they had stood for a long time in the wind without speaking. Helen had said only, at the very end, *I should have read it, Greta. Every word.* And Greta had said, *Yes, you should have,* and then they had walked back down the hill together, slowly, because neither of them was in a hurry to go home.

Dale Whitcomb's boat was still in its slip. His name was still painted across the transom in careful black letters, and nobody had touched it. A memorial for Dale had been held on the water on the first Saturday in August, because Ryan had said his father would have hated a church full of people talking about the weather. The whole fleet had come. Eleven boats had sailed out past the breakwater at noon, in a single slow line, with their horns sounding. Walt had been the last to pass, at the tiller of his old blue trawler, with his hat in his hand and his face wet, and the sound of his horn had carried all the way across the bay, where Ryan stood at the stern of Dale's boat with his father's keys in his hand.

Greta looked out at the lighthouse. It was almost noon now. In a few minutes the lamp would be lit for the first time since the winter, and the light would sweep slowly across the bay, a long, pale, steady beam that would reach past the breakwater and the marina and the empty chair at the back of the council chamber, out across the water to the open lake. It would not make anything right. But it would be there, every night, where anyone could see it.

Judge stood up, stretched, and walked the length of the breakwater to the edge of the stone, where he sat down and looked out at the water with the air of a cat who had heard a sound that nobody else had noticed.

"Don't," Greta told him. "I know what you're going to say."

He did not look around.

Greta picked up the thermos, unscrewed the cap, and poured herself a cup of coffee that had gone lukewarm in the sun. She drank it slowly. Then she took out her phone and opened the email from Joan Cho in Carrow City, which she had read four times that morning and had not yet answered. The attachment was a single spreadsheet, a copy of the county's audit log for the TownShare account used by the Lantern Bay clerk's office, pulled for the state review of the July fourteenth minutes, with every login, every file access, and every deletion marked in neat columns of gray type.

Most of it she already knew. The login at five fifty-eight on Wednesday morning, from the back office workstation, under Denise Park's after-hours code. The trash purge disabled at six twenty-nine. The deletion of the July fourteenth audio at six thirty-one. The logout at six thirty-one and forty-four seconds. Bernice Oakes had told the truth about every one of them, down to the second.

But there was one line on the second page that Greta had not expected, and she had read it three times before she was certain of what it meant. It was dated Tuesday night, the fourteenth of July, at eleven twenty-six p.m. It showed a login to the same account, from a device registered to the Lantern Bay clerk's office, under the old clerk credentials that had been deactivated in June, when Helen Marsh had changed the password for the first time in eleven years. The session had lasted nine minutes.
